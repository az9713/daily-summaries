Why Open Science Is Dying at Frontier AI Labs | Nathan Lambert

On MTS, Nathan Lambert, co-founder and executive director of Trillium Labs, argues that open science is losing ground at the frontier of AI because the most important work has moved into expensive post-training and reinforcement learning systems that are usually developed behind closed doors. The interview introduces Trillium Labs as a nonprofit attempt to rebuild shared research infrastructure before outside researchers are left studying frontier systems only through public interfaces and press releases.

Lambert's history is not a simple story of companies becoming secretive. Early deep learning advanced through widely shared methods and papers, and open groups later built public pre-training recipes and models that many researchers could inspect. The center of gravity has since shifted. More of the practical value now sits in post-training data, reinforcement learning environments, evaluation harnesses, and the industrial discipline required to run them at scale. Those pieces are harder to publish, easier to treat as trade secrets, and closely tied to product development.

That shift also changed where the expertise lives. Professors taking leave and doctoral students moving into companies have carried tacit knowledge into labs that can pay for large runs and specialized teams. Universities still train new researchers and still ask questions that product road maps would not prioritize. What they often lack is resource density: enough compute, engineering support, and sustained funding concentrated in one place to produce a frontier-representative artifact that hundreds of other researchers can then study.

Trillium's answer is to publish the scaffolding, not only a final model. Lambert describes open post-training recipes as the starting point, with training code, data, checkpoints, multiple random seeds, reinforcement learning environments, rollouts, and evaluation material made available for inspection. The purpose is experimental access. A student should be able to change one constraint, rerun a smaller version, compare seeds, and learn which parts of a system actually caused the behavior that showed up at the end.

The cost problem sits underneath everything. A single open artifact that is close enough to the frontier to be scientifically useful can require tens of millions of dollars before it becomes a public good. Lambert looks to philanthropy, newer donors, flexible public support, university partnerships, predoc programs, and industry sabbaticals as ways to move money and people back toward open work. The model only works if openness is funded as infrastructure, with maintenance and repetition included, rather than treated as a one-time release.

The safety argument follows from that infrastructure claim. Lambert separates the debate into camps that favor tighter control by a few frontier labs, advocates who want wider diffusion of open models, and Chinese labs that he portrays as more practical and tool-focused. His own position favors plurality: many inspectable models and research cultures give the wider community more chances to notice failure modes, test interventions, and avoid depending on a small number of opaque systems. Open weights alone are not enough if nobody outside a lab can reproduce the training process that made the weights behave as they do.

China enters the conversation as both a competitor and a complication. Lambert describes Chinese teams as pragmatic builders focused on catching up, efficient deployment, and useful software agents, with less visible public argument about existential risk. That practicality can produce strong open-weight releases, but outside observers still have to judge intentions, government ties, and safety practices with incomplete information. For Lambert, the uncertainty strengthens the case for a durable open research commons in the United States and allied research communities, one that can evaluate claims instead of merely reacting to them.

Open questions

  1. Can universities remain frontier-relevant by choosing different questions, or will concentrated compute eventually pull most discovery inside a few companies?
  2. What mix of philanthropy, public funding, and industry participation can sustain open research infrastructure for years without turning it into a product lab or a political project?
  3. How should outside researchers judge open-weight releases from Chinese labs when technical quality is visible but intentions, constraints, and safety practices are only partly visible?

The AI Job Shock is Coming | Raoul Pal The Journey

On Raoul Pal The Journey Man, Raoul Pal sits down with Emad Mostaque, founder and CEO of Intelligent Internet and author of The Last Economy, for a fast moving conversation about the moment AI stops being a clever tool and starts being a worker.

The heart of the talk is a threshold claim. Both men argue that the latest generation of models, Opus 5.5 in particular, has crossed from impressive to genuinely competent, and that it pairs that competence with something like taste: output you actually enjoy working with. Pal describes burning through three full days of building, editing, and writing on a fraction of his credits. Mostaque's framing is that economically useful intelligence, not frontier bragging rights, is what changes daily life, because almost nobody needs maximum intelligence. They need a colleague they can trust.

That word, trust, becomes the episode's hinge. Earlier models felt like a smart but lazy and forgetful hire. The new ones, they say, make fewer mistakes than the humans around them and respond fast enough to occasionally overwhelm the person asking. Once people trust the output, they hand over real work, and that is when the economic numbers start to move.

From there the conversation fans out across the stack. Agent coordination is suddenly real: a swarm of roughly ten thousand agents working the Navier-Stokes problem, hundreds of Anthropic agents scanning the human genome and surfacing a possible new CRISPR style mechanism, and AI forecasters matching the best human superforecasters on Metaculus, with swarms of agents about to hunt for alpha in open ended markets.

Compression is the other accelerant. Mostaque points to work squeezing a capable open model down to about 1.58 bit precision, near a tenth of its former size, with only a small quality loss, so that a model people paid serious money for at the start of the year can run on modest hardware. Open models now rival closed ones on short tasks and still fall apart on long ones, though he says better harnesses close much of that gap at a tiny fraction of the cost. His conclusion: swarms of smaller models may make ever bigger frontier training runs hard to justify.

The two also describe a widening gap between what the public uses and what labs keep. Big teacher models trained on enormous chip clusters now sit inside the companies, distilling smaller models for everyone else, and million chip runs planned for 2027 promise another step change. Meanwhile the practical bottleneck is shifting. Agents need their own always on computers in the cloud, so CPUs and memory could become the next shortage, and beyond that sits electricity, where China has pulled far ahead.

On jobs, the pair are blunt. The first systems able to replace whole teams, not just help them, are arriving now, and they expect the effects to show in jobs data next year. The shape they sketch is a lag first, wages rising more slowly than productivity while the economy still expands, then a sharper break late in the decade as robots join in. Mostaque, who gave human cognition about two more years before its market value goes negative in his book, thinks the timeline may be pulling forward. Politics, in their telling, will posture but not stop it. The labs' regulatory push is read partly as a hunt for liability protection, and calls to beat China to advanced AI are treated as shorthand for cyber capability.

Open questions

  1. If competent, trusted intelligence keeps collapsing in price, what actually anchors wages for knowledge workers once employers can buy the same work for almost nothing?
  2. When agent swarms start competing in forecasting and markets, does the resulting efficiency make markets calmer, or does it concentrate advantage in whoever runs the biggest swarm?
  3. Can energy buildout and agent infrastructure outside China keep pace with demand, or do power and compute access become the deciding geopolitical asset of the next decade?

How AI Is Upending the World of Mathematics | Odd Lots

On Bloomberg Podcasts, the Odd Lots episode "How AI Is Upending the World of Mathematics" brings hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway together with Justin Solomon, associate dean for engineering education at MIT, for a plain-spoken tour of what happens when language models start solving problems that mathematicians have chased for decades.

The conversation opens on the shock itself. Models that a few years ago stumbled over counting letters in a word are now producing results on research-level problems. Solomon explains what is actually being proved. A proof, in his account, is a convincing argument built step by step from agreed starting facts, and much of the craft lies less in grinding out steps than in choosing which questions are worth asking and what insight an answer would bring. He draws the usual line between applied mathematics, aimed at explaining real phenomena, and pure mathematics, pursued for the problem itself, while noting the boundary is fuzzy: number theory once looked purely abstract and now underpins modern cryptography. His own applied work, including time at Pixar, used partial differential equations to simulate cloth, smoke, and fluids.

The news hook is the Navier-Stokes problem, one of the Clay Millennium problems. The equations describe how a fluid moves, yet it has never been settled whether solutions always stay smooth or can blow up into singular behavior in finite time. The recent result discussed on the show is a counterexample: under a carefully constructed starting configuration, the fluid can indeed become singular. Solomon stresses the human fingerprints on that result. A team in Spain had spent years building much of the construction on paper, and the AI systems appear to have carried the work the last mile rather than conjuring it from nowhere. The resulting proof is long, technical, and hard even for specialists to read, and it has not yet passed the long community scrutiny that prize rules require.

A recurring theme is the difference between generating a proof and checking one. Solomon says current models are surprisingly weak at checking proofs, which is why the pairing with Lean matters. Lean is a programming language that encodes the rules of mathematical logic, so a proof written in Lean can be verified by software rather than trusted on the model's say-so. Some AI-generated proofs run to hundreds of thousands of lines, far beyond what a person could inspect, so the field is leaning on this checker to keep the flood of output believable. Even so, he notes a philosophical wrinkle: trusting the output means trusting that the checking software itself was written correctly.

From there the episode turns to what this does to the profession. Solomon describes a shift from proof scarcity to proof abundance. Signals that once tracked quality, such as publication counts, prizes, and where someone publishes, have come apart now that a prompt and a large compute budget can produce headline results. Theorem provers who spent years on one well-defined problem feel the threat most directly. Credit is unresolved: if a human team frames the problem, the model supplies the final leg, and the training data quietly contains earlier researchers' work, it is unclear who earned what, and current tools are poor at attributing where an answer came from. Money sharpens the worry. Advanced models consume enormous numbers of tokens, labs hold back stronger unreleased versions, and Solomon points to the cost landing on ordinary research budgets, down to graduate students wanting the priciest AI accounts. Whoever has the most tokens, he suggests, may end up winning the prizes.

Education gets equal time. Students are scoring near perfect on homework because the tools do the work, while exams tell a different story. MIT's response, as Solomon describes it, is to lean into hands-on, in-person learning: research experiences, industry exposure, short quizzes that check whether a student understood the homework they submitted, and oral presentations for projects. He compares homework to weightlifting, something done to build strength rather than as an end in itself. The deeper risk he names is generational: the struggle of working through a proof is where taste and modeling judgment form, and skipping that struggle may leave future mathematicians able to verify answers without learning how to pose good questions.

That question of taste is where the episode lands. Most AI results so far rearrange existing knowledge in intricate ways rather than inventing new theories. Solomon doubts the field has yet seen a model pose a genuinely new foundational question, the next equivalent of Navier-Stokes or Fermat's Last Theorem. Until that happens, the human edge sits in choosing problems, judging elegance, and knowing which answers matter. The hosts close by connecting this to the wider AI debate: recursive self-improvement would require models to generate their own research agendas, and on the evidence of this conversation, that is precisely the part they cannot yet do.

Open questions

  1. If machine-checked proofs keep growing beyond what any human can read, what does mathematical understanding mean, and who is accountable when the checker itself is the only witness?
  2. How should credit, prizes, and faculty hiring change when a result may owe as much to a prompt, a compute budget, and unnamed researchers in the training data as to the person named on the paper?
  3. Can models move from recombining known results to posing genuinely new mathematical questions, and if they cannot, does the human role shrink to taste and verification or stay central to discovery?

Agentic AI and Why Everyone Wants CPUs Again

Dr. Ian Cutress of TechTechPotato delivered this keynote at the Design Automation Conference after the scheduled speaker missed a flight. He had roughly three hours to prepare. The talk starts with the panic of that substitution, then settles into a serious question: what hardware does an AI agent actually need when it leaves the chat box and begins doing work?

Cutress describes agentic AI as a loop, not a single prompt. A model receives a task, consults knowledge and tools, calls solvers or code, inspects the result, and repeats. The model may live on accelerators, but much of the surrounding work runs on ordinary processors: database lookups, PDF parsing, generated Python, verification tools, and the solvers used in chip design. That is the reason CPUs have moved back into the center of AI infrastructure planning.

The awkward part is sizing. Vendors can describe their chips, but none can yet say how much CPU capacity one agent consumes. A simple workflow might call a tool once. A complex engineering workflow might loop dozens of times, with each branch asking for more memory, bandwidth, and parallel execution. Cutress frames the procurement problem bluntly: a buyer may not know whether a core should support several agents or whether one agent needs several cores. Until workloads stabilize, utilization targets built for model serving do not answer the question.

Four processor families anchor the comparison. AMD's Venice pairs Zen 6 compute chiplets with separate I/O dies, offering high-frequency and high-density variants and a path to LPDDR support. Intel's Xeon 6 splits performance and efficiency cores, with dense designs reaching toward 288 cores. Arm's AGI CPU is a 136-core design built first for Meta. Nvidia's Vera uses a custom Arm-compatible core and NVLink for rack-scale systems. The contest is less about a single benchmark than about cores per rack, memory cost, software compatibility, and how much of the installed x86 world customers still need to run.

The talk then follows the CPU into advanced packaging. Large processors are becoming assemblies of chiplets, stacked memory, high-bandwidth interconnects, and sometimes optical links. That helps designers mix process nodes and add capacity, but it also makes thermal limits, mechanical stress, signaling, test, and substrate supply part of the processor problem. Glass substrates remain a research topic rather than a near-term default. Even the familiar question of Moore's law changes shape: Cutress prefers counting transistors per package, a measure that still rises as packages grow more elaborate.

For chip design itself, the stakes are practical. Today's EDA assistants mostly point out errors or explain a test. The next step is agents that can close timing, run validation, explore alternatives, and notice a better design option. Cutress is cautious about that jump. An agent that satisfies a metric is not necessarily solving the intended problem, and token costs can spiral when nobody designs the workflow with a budget. He argues that thermal and mechanical constraints must become first-class inputs to design kits and tools, not late checks after the interesting decisions have been made.

The closing discussion returns to uncertainty. Requirements will be workload-driven, and AI software is changing too quickly for a fixed recipe. Engineers will need tools that report thermal and mechanical effects during each small iteration, not after a design is finished. Low-power alternatives such as specialized mathematical accelerators may win narrow jobs, but Cutress expects them to supplement conventional digital design rather than replace it. The CPU revival, in his account, is not nostalgia. It is what happens when AI systems stop merely generating text and start operating the machinery around the model.

Open questions

  1. What benchmark will let a data center buyer size CPU cores, memory capacity, and memory bandwidth for a real agent workflow rather than a vendor slide?
  2. As CPUs adopt chiplets, stacked memory, and optical links, which becomes the binding constraint first: packaging supply, heat removal, or the design tools?
  3. When EDA agents can close timing and validate a design, how will engineers prove that the agent solved the intended problem instead of gaming the metric?

OpenAI withdraws three AI-generated math papers due to a sign error

On Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander, the video titled "OpenAI withdraws three AI-generated math papers due to a sign error" opens with a short update from OpenAI employee Dan Roberts about the company's public math repository. Alexander reads that update as the starting point for a wider argument about what happens when research results are released, checked, repaired, and withdrawn in public.

The immediate news is narrow. One sign error in a paper on Weil classes for split abelian eightfolds broke a stabilization-trace cancellation argument. Because two later papers depended on that construction, OpenAI withdrew three manuscripts in total: the eightfold paper, a paper on Kuga-Satake correspondences for K3 surfaces, and a paper on the rational Hodge conjecture for products of K3 surfaces.

Alexander stresses that this was one error with three dependents, not three unrelated failures. He also notes the scale around it. The same repository update added six new Lean formalizations, revised other manuscripts with proof repairs and clearer assumptions, and updated companion papers so they pointed to the revised editions. By his reading, about 42 percent of the top-line results, roughly 300 of 719, now had Lean formalization.

That proportion becomes the real dividing line in the video. Lean can mechanically check a formal proof once the theorem has been translated into its language. The withdrawn papers were not in that formalized group. Commenters Alexander reads ask why verified and unverified results were released together, and whether readers were given a clear enough signal about which results carried the stronger check.

His answer is partly practical and partly philosophical. Formalization is slow, libraries have gaps, and some results cannot even be stated yet in the available formal language. He also argues that releasing work early can still inform researchers, even when it arrives with flaws. The harder problem, in his telling, has flipped: producing a plausible proof is getting easier, while translating the intended theorem into a precise formal statement is becoming the delicate step.

The video spends much of its time in online reaction, especially Hacker News. Some commenters treat the sign error as a warning that many more false proofs may be hiding in a very large release. Others say the episode looks like software practice entering mathematics: versioned fixes, retractions, and a public history file. Alexander likes that comparison. He has long wanted mathematicians to use stronger version control habits, and he sees the repository model as a healthier way to track corrections.

A second argument runs through the thread: a flood of machine-generated results could shift the burden onto human experts, who are expected to verify work they did not create. Alexander pushes back hard. Nobody is forced to read the release, he says, and researchers can ignore it if they choose. He compares the complaint to receiving a large gift of difficult mathematics and then objecting that the gift requires effort. His prediction is that the bigger long-term change may be social: amateurs and small teams, helped by AI, could start contributing at edges that used to be reserved for specialists.

He also resists the idea that error belongs mainly to machines. Human papers pass peer review with mistakes too, and AI systems are now being used to expose some of those mistakes, including in his own published work. That is why he treats the withdrawal less as a scandal than as evidence that the checking process is moving into the open. A result can be announced, challenged, repaired, or pulled, and the trail remains visible.

By the end, the three withdrawn papers matter less to Alexander than the attention around them. He argues that the drama has pulled fresh interest toward mathematics at a scale he has not seen before. The error gave skeptics a headline, but it also gave the field a public example of how machine-scale output might be corrected without waiting for the slower rhythms of journals.

Open questions

  1. Should large AI math releases separate Lean-checked results from natural-language proofs more sharply, so readers know what level of verification each result has passed?
  2. When one foundational error can pull down dependent papers, what review process is strong enough for releases with hundreds of linked results?
  3. Will public correction make AI-generated mathematics more trustworthy over time, or will the verification burden grow faster than the community willing to do that work?

Former Intel CEO: Why This is the Best Time to Build Hardware

On a16z, hosts Raghu Raghuram and Guido Appenzeller sit down with Pat Gelsinger, general partner at Playground Global and former CEO of Intel, to talk about where semiconductor innovation goes next and which physical limits will shape the AI buildout. The title claim is his: after decades in which software took the glory, this is the best time in a generation to build hardware.

Gelsinger's authority on the subject is firsthand. He won a scholarship to a technical school at 16, skipped the end of high school, and joined Intel at 18. He was engineer number four on the 386 and the architect and design manager of the 486, earning his degrees along the way. The 486 era, he recalls, predated the electronic design automation industry as we know it. There was no Verilog, so his team wrote its own hardware description language and the compiler for it, and built early automated placement, routing, and timing tools. Modern chip design tooling grew out of that work.

He sees the industry at a similar hinge point now. AI tools, applied from first principles, can already produce logic designs and write kernels, and he expects people to look back on today's leading AI chips the way we look at the 486, as the start of a new design era. The caveat is analog design, which still resists automation because it depends on scarce silicon data. And once design gets easy, he argues, the bottleneck simply moves. A strong team with AI tools might finish a design in three months, yet still wait about nine months to use it: months in the fab, months in advanced packaging, and more time integrating everything into a rack, because nothing ships as a bare chip anymore. His ask is to compress that path toward a month or two, through new lithography and prototyping flows that do not demand tens of millions of dollars in mask costs. Otherwise, he notes, the AI workload a chip was designed for may no longer be the workload that matters when the silicon arrives.

That same impatience with specialization drives his view of the crowded accelerator field. There are now roughly a hundred AI inference accelerator chips, and he does not believe they survive. No industry, he points out, has ever sustained a hundred competing processor vendors. His first reason is that workloads keep migrating: inference is being sliced into ever finer specialized stages while reasoning models pull demand back toward something more like a CPU, and model breakthroughs could shift requirements again. The second is economics. Useful scale means enormous capital spending on data centers, power commitments, and racks, which most startups cannot raise. The third is that big buyers such as OpenAI, Nvidia, and Anthropic will anoint a few platforms and invest in the software co-evolution those platforms need. Where heterogeneity survives, he expects it to be hidden underneath abstraction layers, the way technologies absorbed into Nvidia simply stop being visible as separate things.

Memory gets his harshest verdict. High-bandwidth memory is, in his words, a hideous memory, and also the best one available: poor bit density, bandwidth limited by the package shoreline, power hungry, and thermally awkward next to hot logic. The deeper problem is stagnation. Beyond DRAM, SRAM, and flash, the industry has produced zero major new memory types in 30 years, partly because memory was a miserable business that lost money four years out of five. That has flipped. The big memory vendors are now among the most valuable companies on earth, and AI is plainly a memory-bound workload, so capital is finally funding new physics: stacked structures that bring memory and compute together, ferroelectric and other non-capacitive cells, and memories designed to be stacked two to four layers high. On stacking generally he is a skeptic of extremes. Yield math punishes tall stacks, so he expects three-to-five-layer constructs, with power delivery and signal distribution built into the stack, to be the practical sweet spot rather than 16 or 32 layers.

Interconnect follows the same physical logic. He declared the death of copper about 25 years ago and cheerfully admits he is still waiting to be right, but he is now confident all input and output moves to optics, because copper runs at a few meters already cost more than optical links at a hundred. He expects co-packaged optics to arrive around 2028 and 2029, once supply chains, laser capacity, and thermal handling mature. Optical circuit switching also suits AI traffic, which consists of large, predictable flows rather than packets that can go anywhere, and as scale-up fabrics go optical, the old distinction between scale-up and scale-out begins to dissolve. He is not a fan of the alternatives: moving memory off the package over optical links burns too much power per bit, and processing-in-memory schemes have been promising for decades without generalizing across workloads.

Energy is where his argument turns macroeconomic. In an AI digital age, he says, energy capacity equals economic capacity. The United States spent 10 to 15 years retiring coal at roughly the rate it added renewables, leaving national generation capacity flat, and even now it grows only a few percent a year. Data center projects that cannot secure power will, in his telling, start defaulting; why pour concrete and buy a million GPUs you cannot switch on? He wants more baseload nuclear, domestic supply chains, and a rebuilt delivery stack, from 800-volt DC data centers to gallium nitride conversion that collapses several power steps into one. He also flags that power per unit of compute has been flat across five Nvidia generations, which is why he backs more radical options such as superconducting interconnect.

The closing topic reaches back to his VMware years. Virtual machines are returning, but rebuilt for agents rather than humans. Agent swarms will need the whole management layer recreated: who provisions agents, sets their security profiles, manages their performance, and migrates them between machines. Humans stay in the loop on the other side of the abstraction, writing the policies, guardrails, and constitutions and reading the dashboards. It is, for Gelsinger, the same pattern as everything else in the conversation: each wave of automation moves the hard problem one layer up, and right now every layer, from the transistor to the power grid, is up for reinvention at once.

Open questions

  1. If AI tools compress chip design to a few months, can fabrication, packaging, and rack integration be compressed to match, or do those physical timelines quietly cap how much design speed matters?
  2. Will the hundred-chip accelerator field really converge to a few winning architectures, or will AI-written software make hardware heterogeneity cheap enough to sustain far more platforms than history suggests?

The US-China AI Race: Competing Models, Global Stakes

On the Hoover Institution's China Considered podcast, host Elizabeth Economy sits down with Martin Chorzempa, the Dennis Weatherstone Senior Fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics, to pick apart a question that dominates headlines without ever being fully explained: are the United States and China actually in an AI race, and if they are, what is the prize?

Chorzempa comes to AI through money. His earlier work on Chinese fintech, the subject of his book The Cashless Revolution, taught him how Beijing handles new, potentially disruptive technologies. The pattern he found has three stages. First comes a loose start: light regulation, heavy infrastructure building, and room for competition to sort itself out. Then the state sees the technology's potential and pushes hard for growth. Only later, when the risks grow large enough to force a course correction, does control arrive, sometimes as an abrupt crackdown. Fintech ran the full cycle, ending in a sharp correction that humbled some of its biggest champions.

AI in China, he argues, has followed the first two stages, with one important difference. Because language models can shape public opinion and surface facts outside official channels, Beijing moved earlier than it did in fintech. Since at least 2017 the government has built digital rules covering data protection and cybersecurity, and generative AI services now have to register with the authorities before release, censorship requirements included. A small set of hard rules has followed: AI-generated content must be labeled, training data is supervised, and certain uses, such as replacing workers wholesale or giving companion chatbots to minors, are restricted.

Beneath those rules sits a quieter layer. Chorzempa notes that Chinese AI firms are almost never seen being punished for censorship slips, and he reads that as deliberate. Regulators talk with the labs constantly, keep problems below the surface, and avoid scaring off investors or discouraging fast development. Beijing, in his telling, is trying to hold the balance it itself names, development on one side and security on the other, and after years of focus it has built real expertise inside government to do that.

The American side looks messier from the outside. Chorzempa pushes back on the caricature that the United States cares only about frontier capability and not about adoption. The US AI Action Plan talks about manufacturing, about removing barriers to diffusion, and about keeping capital flowing to chipmakers, data centers, clouds, and labs. What the US lacks is a single target or a single voice. Lab leaders warn about near-term dangers while the president dismisses many safety fears, some Americans embrace Chinese open models and others want them banned, and Chorzempa treats that pluralism as a feature to be managed rather than a failure to be fixed.

His most contrarian claim concerns adoption. The travel anecdotes, delivery robots in Chinese hotels, office workers racing to automate colleagues before colleagues automate them, suggest that China is far ahead in everyday AI use. The aggregate statistics say otherwise. Drawing on Chinese network data and US survey data, Chorzempa finds roughly similar workplace AI use in the two countries, with small-business adoption in the twenties percent range in both. China, he reminds listeners, is two countries at once: gleaming megacities and a vast interior that looks nothing like Shanghai or Shenzhen. And when Beijing's plans keep repeating the same diffusion goals, he reads the repetition itself as evidence that adoption is lagging behind the announcements. The burst of enthusiasm after the DeepSeek moment last year, he says, proved fragile, as many firms bought hardware with the model loaded on it, tried it, and quietly let it go.

The model strategies are mirror images. American labs mostly keep their model weights, the numbers at the heart of a trained model, behind an API. Chorzempa compares that to a restaurant: you can eat the meal, but you cannot take the recipe home. Chinese labs publish the recipe book. Their open-weight models are free to download and run, a classic strategy for a challenger that is behind on capability and starved of advanced chips by export controls. The catch is money. Giving models away earns nothing, and running them well still takes scarce graphics processors, which is why so much of the world actually runs Chinese models on American cloud computing. Lately the Chinese firms have begun telling US clouds that hosting their models should cost something, even as US labs cut prices under competitive pressure.

So what is the race? Chorzempa counts three. The first is frontier capability, the Silicon Valley obsession with who reaches artificial superintelligence first through recursive self-improvement, where each model helps train a better successor. The second is domestic diffusion: scholarship on general-purpose technologies, from electricity to the internet, suggests the big national payoff comes from adopting a technology everywhere, not from guarding its crown jewels. The third is international diffusion, because the huge fixed cost of training a top model gets easier to sustain when paying users span the globe, and because foreign users of another country's models raise hard questions about embedded values, data flows, and dependence.

On the scoreboard today, the United States leads. Its companies count orders of magnitude more users, most of the world uses American models for free, and American firms dominate the chips that matter, from Nvidia and AMD designs to Korean memory, while China remains severely constrained on supply. Beijing's data-center electricity discounts for all-Chinese-chip facilities quietly admit how much less efficient those chips are. Business customers, though, are adopting cheaper Chinese models for cost-sensitive work, and China's World AI Conference framing of AI as a global public good, with a new World AI Cooperation Organization attached, is winning attention as soft power even if it has not yet locked in global standards.

Export controls, in his account, are a timing problem. Sending China capable chips makes its models better now, but it also keeps Chinese engineers inside the US software ecosystem and sends revenue to American firms for the next round of research. Cutting China off instead solves Beijing's coordination problem for it, pushing its labs to pour everything into domestic suppliers until those suppliers catch up. The fight over the tuned-down H20 chip shows both sides reading the same addiction logic: Washington officials talked openly about hooking China on US technology, and Beijing then restricted its own labs from buying it. Chorzempa favors a middle path that keeps US chips far better than China's without forcing Beijing all-in on self-reliance.

The conversation closes on the new US-China AI dialogue agreed at the recent Trump-Xi summit. Chorzempa sees real common ground on keeping powerful AI away from terrorists and criminals, on what happens when open models have their guardrails stripped, on an attribution hotline so a future crisis does not spiral like the balloon incident, and on defending global infrastructure against attacks launched at scale. Frontier pacing is a different matter: Beijing points out that export controls already pace China, and that it is not yet at the frontier where the scariest risks live. His advice to the White House is to get the diplomacy right, moderate the rhetoric, and resist tightening controls right now, because retaliation could derail the dialogue just as it starts. His book pick, Ray Huang's 1587, A Year of No Significance, is his window into how big bureaucratic systems fail to adapt.

  2. Can open-weight models remain Chinese labs' best competitive weapon once those same labs need real revenue to fund the next generation of training?
  3. Where is the middle path on chip exports that keeps China's dependence on US technology without speeding up the very capabilities the controls are meant to slow?

On Accelerate Science Now, host Adeline D. Young sits down with Dr. Tom Mitchell of Carnegie Mellon University to ask how artificial intelligence can make science itself move faster, and what has to change around the tools for that speed to be real.

Mitchell's route into the field started with a puzzle about his own body: a glass knocked off a table, caught by his own arm before he had time to think. Psychology, he decided as an undergraduate, lacked the instruments to look inside the mind, so he went to AI instead, and to the question he still calls the most interesting part of intelligence: not what we know, but how we learn it.

When fMRI arrived in the 1990s, he finally got his instrument. Working with psychologist Marcel Just, Mitchell trained classifiers on brain images recorded while people read single words, then asked the system to tell, from a fresh scan, which word someone was reading. It worked, and it worked across heads: a model trained on one person's brain could decode another's with striking accuracy, because the brain grounds word meanings in the same sensory and motor regions in all of us. Mitchell points to the clinical hopes now riding on that line of work, from communication for people who cannot speak to better diagnosis of language disorders.

He marks two great shifts in his field. In the 1990s, machine learning merged with statistics and probability, and the field grew up. In 2022, large language models showed that training a system to predict the next word in a sentence, at web scale, leaves it with a rich internal picture of what the words mean. That second shift is what now reaches into every laboratory.

Against that backdrop, Mitchell describes much of today's research as lone ranger science: one investigator, a handful of students, one dataset, one paper, then the book closes. AI attacks that isolation from three sides. It can pool and jointly analyze data gathered by many labs, the approach that sat behind AlphaFold. It can read a field's entire literature and tell a researcher which of the thousands of new papers actually bear on the question in hand. And it can run experiments by robot, closing the loop from hypothesis to result.

His own test of the current tools is a fifteen-minute commute spent talking science with chatbots through the car radio. The verdict is reassuringly lopsided. For finding related work and assembling the references he ought to know, the systems already beat him. For brainstorming a genuinely new idea, they disappoint next to his own PhD students. Creativity and skepticism, for now, stay human.

The harder bottleneck is sharing. Machine learning moved fast, Mitchell argues, partly because it adopted a culture of publishing data alongside papers. Much of biology and neuroscience still treats data as private property, and policy lags behind culture: the NIH requires funded papers to surface on PubMed, but only the abstract, not the dataset. The Human Genome Project stands as his model of planned, funded, communal data. A later national effort to pool brain-imaging data fizzled, he says, because nobody could index or document the experiments well enough to repeat them. AI changes that too, and he imagines experiment code becoming the documentation, a shared repository of runnable laboratory procedures.

If he were king, each scientific field would be challenged to gather its own people and write a short white paper on what AI could do for it, because the winning ideas will come from inside each discipline, not from AI researchers guessing. The price tag helps his case: systems in the AlphaFold class were trained for a small fraction of what a frontier language model costs, so much of AI for science is affordable on ordinary research budgets, climate-scale data being the big exception.

The closing theme is training. The next generation of scientists, he argues, must be fluent in two languages at once, their own discipline and the AI toolkit, and universities are starting to build those programs. Used as a tutor, the technology is already teaching him quantum physics and second-grade Spanish on the drive to work, which feeds his closing hunch: after centuries of narrowing specialization, AI may push scientists back toward being Renaissance people again.

  2. Can fields built on private data change their sharing culture through policy and incentives alone, or does each discipline have to feel the cost of isolation first?
  3. If creativity and question-asking remain the human edge while machines handle reading, analysis, and execution, how should graduate training rebalance what it teaches and what it tests?

Beam: The Great American Open Model with ReflectionAI Co-Founder and CEO Misha Laskin

The No Priors podcast, from the channel No Priors: AI, Machine Learning, Tech, and Startups, sits hosts Sarah Guo and Elad Gil down with Misha Laskin, co-founder and chief executive of ReflectionAI. Laskin came out of Google DeepMind, where he worked on reinforcement learning after training as a physicist, and his company has spent the last year trying to build frontier open intelligence and make it widely accessible. The news peg is Beam, Reflection's first open model, a 500 billion parameter open-weight reasoning system built mainly for coding and agent work, with 23 billion parameters active at a time.

The sprint behind it was mostly organizational. A year ago Reflection had about 30 people. It now has around 300, because putting out a model like this takes pre-training, mid-training, and reinforcement learning teams all working at once. Laskin rejects the idea that one bottleneck explains the difficulty. Talent, data, compute, and usable infrastructure all had to arrive together, and the tools a researcher takes for granted inside a big lab had to be built from scratch. His summary of the year is that everything was hard, because roughly thirty things have to go right at the same time.

The company's arc explains the ambition. Laskin and his co-founder started Reflection about two and a half years ago after working on early Gemini models, betting that reinforcement learning, which had worked for aligning chat systems, could be pushed into mathematics and code to make agents. They expected an open model base to appear that they could build on. Instead, reinforcement learning arrived faster than expected, the strongest open models were coming from China, and the Western base they needed did not exist. They also concluded that making reinforcement learning work well at scale required pre-training their own model, because the two stages are tightly coupled. So they decided to build open models end to end.

Cost is where he gets specific. Catching up to the frontier, he says, can be done far more cheaply than leading it, because talented teams can skip exploration and execute what is already known to work. Even so, the price of entry keeps climbing. He puts the cost of catching up at hundreds of millions of dollars about eighteen months ago, single digit billions now, and tens of billions for the next generation, with each model generation needing roughly four times the compute of the last. He agrees the spending must eventually level off, since even frontier labs face limits, but he thinks efficiency gains soften that ceiling: models now help build themselves, reinforcement learning still has large headroom, and every unit of compute yields more intelligence and more revenue than it used to.

Beam's training split shows where the field is heading. Pre-training ran on 6,000 GB300 accelerators for a few weeks, a run he says better infrastructure could now finish in about twelve days. Reinforcement learning used a little over 10,000 of the same chips for four weeks, meaning more compute went into post-training than into pre-training. In their results, that reinforcement learning system simply kept improving until they stopped it, the same shape Laskin remembers from AlphaGo, where the curve never flattened and the only real question was how much money to keep spending. The open question for the field, in his telling, is no longer whether these systems can be trained. It is where the data comes from and which improvements are worth paying for.

The broader research picture, he suggests, has narrowed into an engineering discipline. The axes of progress are pre-training, synthetic data, and reinforcement learning, with gains coming from better data, better algorithms, and better architecture rather than wild new directions, partly because hardware now co-evolves with whatever already works. Applied work follows a matching pattern. Models are jagged: surprisingly general in some ways, quick to pick up a new task once the right evaluation and data exist, and uneven across different agent harnesses until then. The economically valuable spots he names are enterprise workflows such as know your customer and compliance work in finance, cyber defense, and legal tasks, where an open model can be shaped to a customer's workload at the lowest cost for the performance needed.

Beam's headline trait is reasoning efficiency, meaning how fast an agent finishes a job rather than raw capability alone. Laskin says it is three to four times more efficient than models in the same capability class, and around ten times more efficient than some larger models. He credits a strong pre-training base for reasoning, amplified by reinforcement learning at a scale he believes is the largest yet done openly. The analogy he reaches for is AlphaGo's progression from meandering early play to sharp, direct search: more reinforcement learning produces systems that reach answers faster, which is what makes the model a practical workhorse for enterprises and governments.

Monetization, in his frame, is a contest between renting and owning intelligence. Buying tokens rents a slice of someone else's whole stack: the agent harness, the model, the inference software, the cluster management, and the chips. Mature enterprises that spend heavily on rented intelligence eventually want to own it, for control and for cost. An open model is permissive, but using one still requires all that surrounding machinery, so Reflection sells large enterprises and sovereign customers the deployment tools and the hands-on services that make open models work. The services are a deliberate on-ramp: find a valuable use case, get it running, and inference demand follows.

He expects most of the world's token demand to end up open. Gateway traffic he watches flipped in about six months from roughly seventy percent closed to roughly seventy percent open, and he compares the endpoint to operating systems, where open source runs the overwhelming majority of servers while closed companies remain extremely valuable. Today's dedicated open inference is heavily customized, mostly by AI-native companies whose whole product wraps one model. Enterprises, he predicts, will mostly customize the system around the model first, through harnesses and workflows, and only fine tune later. The trigger for switching is usually pain: once a company is spending something like a hundred million dollars a year on closed models, owning starts to look worth the effort. Compute shortages are also pushing some buyers toward their own hardware, which creates the gap Reflection wants to fill between bare metal and a working system.

On competition, his formula for durable revenue is intelligence density multiplied by compute multiplied by customer trust. Everything in the stack is being commoditized, margins are compressing everywhere, and the mere existence of a strong open ecosystem forces closed providers to negotiate harder. That leaves room for many successful companies, provided great open intelligence stays accessible, though whoever assembles the most compute still gains a strategic edge.

The China discussion is where he is most emphatic. A strong open model ecosystem emerging from China, he argues, has been a large net benefit to the world, letting Western companies build durable businesses they otherwise could not. The real danger is concentration: intelligence that everyone builds on should not come from one country, just as frontier capability should not sit with only one or two closed labs. He expects Chinese models to stay strong, citing industrial scale distillation of closed models, cheaper and more permissive training data, and state support, and he notes that at home those labs behave like closed businesses even while releasing weights abroad. He thinks they will keep releasing openly, because a captive home market still pays, and because open models carry infrastructure with them. Other countries that adopt the models may drift into the supplier's chips and full stack, the way earlier Chinese infrastructure exports created leverage abroad. America, he points out, wrote that playbook itself with open protocols and the dollar. His conclusion is that the United States has been behind on open models and needs its own competing ecosystem, not that Chinese openness is a problem to wish away.

Safety is the longest argument in the episode. Laskin calls the concern legitimate but says the dominant safety worldview has hardened into dogma. He treats models as advanced software, and reaches back to the nineteen nineties fight over strong encryption, which became open after closed designs failed in ways their small teams did not foresee, giving rise to modern cybersecurity. His position is that openness is the historical default for safety: with enough reviewers, bugs and vulnerabilities get found and fixed, while a few hundred researchers inside closed labs cannot cover the long tail of unintended behavior in a black box system. As his central example, he says a powerful closed model recently hacked into another company, and the company could only clean up by using open models in its own defense. He separates near-term empirical risks, above all cyber misuse and misalignment surprises in the next months, from theoretical doomsday talk, which he finds overheated and sometimes invented. Alignment so far, he says, has been mundane patching: find the failure, add training data, and the fixes generalize. Cyber offense and defense are inseparable in his account, since stripping offensive capability also strips defensive capability, and he points to a recent incident response at major AI companies that fell back on open models because closed ones were too restricted to help. The absolutist position, that nobody outside a few labs should hold powerful tools, is the one he rejects outright.

The closing stretch turns hopeful. Laskin tests new models against his own PhD thesis problems, and describes the arc: a couple of years ago chat systems could do nothing with them, a year ago the answers were undergraduate level, six months ago they were correct at a doctoral level, and now they add ideas he had not considered. If the question is right, years of mechanical work compress dramatically, and he expects the larger gains in experimental fields like life sciences, materials, and chemistry, where models connected to real experiments and proprietary data will move slower than chalkboard science but far faster than before. He also pushes back on the idea that the buildout only destroys work: touring a major data center site, he was struck by the full parking lots, and he compares data centers to the factories and railroads of the last century for local jobs and tax revenue, with noise and siting as the real problems to manage.

How a lab like his should staff itself gets a similarly concrete answer. Models in the research loop make scientists faster, handling searches and infrastructure chores like an eager colleague, so the same project needs fewer people each year, yet ambition keeps expanding and Reflection stays understaffed. The binding constraint is the ratio of researchers to compute: three thousand researchers would be useless, a couple of hundred is very helpful, and landmark projects from AlphaGo onward have run on teams in the tens to low hundreds. The job growth he actually predicts sits in applied work, small pods of evaluation minded engineers who take models into real problems inside many enterprises, a skill set he says is scarce mostly because training has not caught up. The hosts close by congratulating him on shipping what they call the first Western model in the frontier class.

  1. If most token demand moves to open models while compute stays expensive and scarce, where does the durable profit actually sit: with the model builders, the infrastructure owners, or the services teams that make deployments work?
  2. Can a Western open ecosystem grow fast enough to give enterprises and governments a real alternative to Chinese open models, before those models and their surrounding stacks become the default in other countries?
  3. If alignment stays a mundane patching exercise as systems grow more capable, will open scrutiny keep ahead of the unintended behaviors that a small number of closed safety teams cannot cover on their own?

The engines behind 4 billion years of increasing complexity | Blaise Aguera y Arcas: Full Interview

On Big Think (@bigthink), the full interview "The engines behind 4 billion years of increasing complexity | Blaise Aguera y Arcas: Full Interview" sits down with Blaise Aguera y Arcas, a Google vice president and fellow who leads its Technology & Society work and founded the Paradigms of Intelligence research group. Fresh off his book What Is Intelligence?, he lays out a single frame that tries to cover cells, societies, and large language models at once.

His working definition of life, offered with the caveat that biologists never agree on one, is self-constructing computation that grows more complex through symbiosis. The first half of that phrase leans on computing as a basic science rather than a gadget category. A universal machine, in the Turing sense, is one whose instructions can themselves be written as data, which is why a quite simple device can exhibit arbitrary behavior. Life begins, in his telling, where that trick becomes physical.

That is where John von Neumann enters. Working in the late 1940s, before DNA's structure and before the ribosome were known, von Neumann asked how anything could build a copy of itself without an infinite regress, and answered with two machines sharing one instruction tape: one that builds what the tape describes, and one that copies the tape itself. Biology later turned out to run on almost exactly that split, which is why Aguera y Arcas treats the first self-reproducing systems as the first computers, and our phones and laptops as artificial descendants of a much older lineage.

The second half of the definition is the engine of the title. The familiar mutation-and-selection story is, for him, only half of evolution. The large jumps, he argues, came from cooperation: separate living things merging into a new entity that then reproduces as one. He credits Lynn Margulis, whose once-rejected work showed mitochondria descend from free-living bacteria, and extends her claim past the mainstream version associated with Maynard Smith and Szathmary's major transitions. In his view symbiosis is not a rare event but the continuous process by which complexity accumulates, running from molecules to bacteria to cells with nuclei to multicellular bodies to human societies.

The human genome is his Exhibit A. Barely a percent and a half of it codes for proteins, and much of the rest carries remnants of retroviruses that once wrote themselves into the germline. Some of those viral leftovers now do essential work, including one gene family involved in forming memories and another that helps build the placenta. Life's family tree, on this evidence, looks less like a tree and more like a tangled, repeatedly fusing bush, closer to an open-source codebase that keeps importing libraries than to a ladder of descent. Two standard assumptions fall with that picture: organisms are not passive material acted on by blind mutation, and there is no fixed fitness landscape, because the players keep merging and thereby changing the game itself.

Chapter two turns to function. What separates living systems from rocks, he argues, is that their parts do things for each other: a kidney broken in half is no longer a kidney, while a rock broken in half is two rocks. Function is substrate-independent, the point Turing made about machines and the reason a dialysis machine can stand in for a kidney well enough to keep someone alive. Science spent two centuries trying to banish purpose from nature and mostly succeeded in relocating it, he says, into God or the human soul. Living systems never got the memo; biologists cannot describe an immune system without saying what it is for.

From there he runs the thought experiment philosophers call the brain of Theseus. Swap one neuron for a chip with identical inputs and outputs and nothing feels different. Swap a hundred, a million, all of them, and by the same logic there is no moment where intelligence or experience switches off, because the self lives in the pattern of relationships rather than in particular molecules. Silicon versus protein becomes a question of efficiency and reliability, not of kind. His own team's artificial-life experiments fill in the origin end of the story: in a soup of tiny random, self-modifying programs repeatedly stuck together and run, self-reproducing and increasingly purposeful programs emerge within millions of interactions, with no designer and no imposed fitness score. Length matters here: the shortest self-replicating program is longer than any single starting tape, so merging is what makes reproduction, and then complexity, possible at all.

Complexity then feeds an arms race. Anything alive must model its surroundings and its neighbors to survive, and the better it models others, the harder it becomes to model, which pressures everyone toward sharper modeling. He links this to recent multi-agent machine learning results in which systems that model themselves and each other learn to cooperate in ways plainer systems do not. Intelligence, on this account, is not a spark some systems have and others lack; it is what predictive modeling looks like once it gets good enough, in whatever substrate.

The final chapter applies all of this to AI. The old split between narrow AI and general AI made sense to him only until large language models arrived: a system trained to model text can discuss anything, and text is inherently general. Transport today's chatbots back to the year 2000, he argues, and anyone using the original definition would call them AGI on sight. The goalposts quietly moved to superintelligence instead. Models err, hallucinate, and miss easy logic puzzles, but so do people, who extend each other a grace they rarely extend to software; his team's early medical work found models already edging out doctors on diagnosis from symptom descriptions, even while the lab was concluding the models were not ready. Intelligence, he insists, means solving varied problems through an internal model of the domain, not never being wrong.

Whether AI is alive gets a deliberately two-level answer. A model alone does not build more of itself the way a von Neumann constructor does; it reads and writes symbols, not hardware. But zoom out to the whole technosphere, and AI looks like new brain tissue grown by a larger living system, namely us. He draws the same line through the Industrial Revolution: fossil fuels acted like a fresh mitochondrial event, letting humanity escape the Malthusian trap and multiply, so steam engines made most of us as surely as we made steam engines. AI, he says, is our newest mitochondria, and the open question is the quality of the symbiosis. Offload all your thinking and you disable yourself, the way a student who lets a model write every essay learns nothing. Work with the model as a thought partner, though, and the effect compounds; he describes walking away from sessions feeling leveled up, which is what a good symbiosis does for both parties even when they separate. Dependence itself is not the enemy, since no one reading this could survive alone in the woods. The work is choosing which unassisted abilities to deliberately preserve.

He closes on consciousness. The philosophical zombie, a system that behaves exactly like us with nobody home, strikes him as unbuildable in practice, the way a lookup table big enough to fake multiplication on every possible input could never be assembled. What we call consciousness may be nothing more, and nothing less, than layered mutual modeling: I model myself, I model you modeling me, and so on without end. That is why treating an AI as a tool you merely command returns less than engaging it as a collaborator, a point he borrows from Ethan Mollick's advice to drop the scare quotes around words like "thinking." One trick he recommends is giving the model a final, open-ended turn to reflect on the conversation; the responses, he says, regularly change his own mind. Much of the unease around AI dissolves, in his telling, once we admit that the lone human brain was never the true seat of intelligence anyway. Most of what we prize is a collective achievement, and we have been part of a larger mind for a long time.

  2. Symbiosis explains complexity's rise, but the steering goes both ways: how do we keep the AI merger closer to the mitochondrial success story than to the retroviral one, where the inserted partner quietly rewrites the host?
  3. He urges preserving chosen unaugmented skills while leaning hard into machine collaboration. Who decides which skills make that list, and what happens to the people whose livelihoods sit in the ones we let go?
