MTS · Cheryl Wu · Elasticity Institute

Why AI Math Breakthroughs Upgrade RSI Timelines | Cheryl Wu

MTS sat down live with Cheryl Wu, president of the Elasticity Institute, the day after OpenAI announced 722 new mathematics results. The question on the table: does a breakthrough like that move the timeline for recursive self-improvement?

Wu's answer starts with a definition. Her institute uses RSI to mean self-sustaining acceleration: a regime where AI progress speeds itself up while other inputs hold roughly steady.

That is a much weaker claim than an intelligence explosion. Wu argues the two ideas get lazily confused in public debate, and the confusion matters because it shapes how society and policymakers respond.

On the math results themselves, her read is measured. If AI systems excel at mathematics, they are likely to get better at AI research and development too. That raises the probability of self-sustaining acceleration in her view, without being strong evidence of an imminent fast takeoff.

She points to a second signal from the same news cycle: reports that AI systems are improving at designing AI research experiments and developing better judgment about which experiments are worth running, in some accounts rivaling human researchers on taste. Models that write code, design experiments, and pick promising directions still leave humans one job: deciding which peaks are worth climbing.

The host pushes the other direction at one point. The math results made him slightly less of a believer in general intelligence, because the models show a knowledge profile unlike ours: extraordinary in some areas, yet the broader economy has not visibly sped up.

Wu agrees this is the narrow versus broad capabilities problem. A narrow capability can accelerate itself without generalizing into economy-wide effects, a distinction her institute's memo also makes.

Much of the conversation walks through her institute's framework of eight things labs should measure to make RSI observable rather than vibes-based. The core asks: diverse, unsaturated capability benchmarks; published measurements from internal model checkpoints; and attribution work that separates capability gains driven by training compute from gains driven by algorithmic progress.

Attribution goes one level deeper. If algorithmic progress comes from a surge of compute spent testing more ideas, or from a few exceptional human researchers, that is less concerning. If it comes from models generating the ideas, that points toward RSI.

Headcount and pay are indicators too. If researcher headcount stays fixed while algorithmic progress accelerates sharply, cognitive work is being replaced, which should worry observers more. Wu expects compensation for the remaining researchers to rise, not fall, because they become the bottleneck and their marginal productivity climbs.

She treats OpenAI's disclosed coding-agent spend, with senior researcher spending reportedly doubling on roughly a monthly cadence, as suggestive but ambiguous. People may refuse to give up AI assistance at almost any price without that proving an equal productivity gain, a demand elasticity caveat she flags herself.

On takeoff speed, Wu is direct that nobody knows yet. Her intuition: once AI research is fully automated, the feedback loop is far stronger than in other technologies, and a generation twice as capable could plausibly double the acceleration. Training time and how parallelizable the work is remain real bottlenecks.

Open questions

  1. Which of the eight proposed indicators would labs actually agree to publish, and would checkpoint measurements from internal models survive competitive pressure to keep them private?
  2. If coding-agent spend keeps doubling, how would anyone separate genuine productivity gains from researchers simply becoming unwilling to work without AI assistance?
  3. Under full automation of AI research, do training time and parallelizability bottlenecks cap the acceleration, or does each more capable generation route around them?

About OpenAI's "Breakthrough" on 377 Math problems...

On Easy Riders, the new video takes OpenAI's latest mathematics drop as its starting point. The company has released a large batch of solutions to open mathematics problems, described by the host as around 370, with the video's own title putting the count at 377. According to him, the work was produced by an internal frontier model working through a set of agents. Instead of presenting the results, OpenAI uploaded them to a GitHub repository as a collection of files, a move the host treats as the real story.

His first complaint is about format. Rather than walk through what was actually done, the company left a folder of files and expects readers to dig through them. He argues this turns mathematics into a benchmarking exercise: another set of problems ticked off, another demonstration of model strength, with little attempt to give readers understanding. He contrasts this with his own approach on the channel, where he solves problems himself and talks through them. In his view, having mathematicians use AI and then explain what was found is a different thing from a large company solving everything and leaving the output as raw text.

What struck him next is the breadth. Scrolling the repository, he passes conformal universality, conformal field theory, random matrix theory, Gaussian orthogonal ensemble universality for random regular graphs, and Gaussian free field results, until he gets to Navier-Stokes. He studied the AdS-CFT correspondence in his master's and even visited the grave of Henri Navier, and he says several of these areas are things he cannot pretend to understand. The breadth is his evidence that this cannot be the work of a small team of human mathematicians guiding the model. After the Navier-Stokes step, he says, the work appears to have spanned discipline after discipline.

He had a practical hope for the repository. On his channel he has been trying for a long time to solve mathematics problems with agent swarms, and he admits he has not really managed it. One solution he produced was marginal, and he says he matched the same result by simply asking GPT-6 Pro, without any swarm at all. So he wanted to learn from the structure of OpenAI's method: how the research folders are organized, how the problems are split up, how the agents are prompted. He says he would rather read a book written by a person, but the method was the lesson he wanted. Instead the repository offers only the finished LaTeX, laid out as a publication and proof artifact collection, with his own tooling telling him there is no useful additional information in it about how the agents were run.

That leads into a broader argument about what mathematics is for. He asks whether the field is about everything getting solved, or about people who can talk to each other and gradually make a picture simpler and more intuitive to see. Some, he concedes, are happy to have the answers regardless of where they came from. But he points out that the models can produce documents they claim are true, and can cross-verify each other, while being much weaker at producing something interesting to read that leaves the reader having learned something.

He also raises a case he has been following. Tristan Buckmaster, working with Levin Apoggi, solved an Euler equation on their way toward an attempt at the Navier-Stokes problem, a Millennium Prize problem. The host says OpenAI then intercepted it because they needed an article for their website. He links that move to the company's need to justify raising capital while ordinary people are priced out. His conclusion is that the company does not really want to improve mathematics. He would watch a talk, and then perhaps read the files. What he would prefer is that they share how they prompt the models, the structure of the folders they use, or ship an improved tool for solving mathematics with the models a subscriber can already access.

Still, he is honest about the work itself. He says he is not arguing the mathematics is not legitimate. He thinks it almost certainly is, that the models are insanely good at mathematics, better than most humans will be, and that they span more fields than any one human could. He names people on the company's team, including Ed Witten and Timothy Gowers, and calls them heavyweights. He just does not understand why the best use of all that is a big folder of files that reads like a listing pulled from a preprint archive.

Open questions

  1. If a claim is produced by one model and verified by another, what counts as a human understanding it?
  2. Would OpenAI be stronger, or just less cautious, if it shared the prompt structure and folder organization behind its agent swarms?
  3. When a field gets answers from systems that cannot present them, who carries the job of turning those answers into something readable?

a16z · The a16z Show

Building the Cloud for AI Agents | AWS CEO Matt Garman

The a16z Show brought Raghu Raghuram together with Matt Garman, who leads AWS, for a wide conversation about how the cloud is being rebuilt around software agents rather than only around people.

Garman frames startups as the original center of AWS. He traces that back to his own early work on EC2, and argues that young companies still push the platform hardest, surface new requirements first, and often grow into the large customers of the next decade.

What has changed, in his telling, is scale at birth. New AI companies can arrive heavily funded and need serious compute immediately, while still asking familiar questions about architecture, security, identity, and how a small team avoids painting itself into a corner.

The agent shift is where Garman sees the deepest redesign. Coding assistants now choose databases, read documentation, and deploy applications, so AWS is adapting account setup, permissions, sandboxes, gateways, and data access so machines can work quickly without inheriting a person's full authority.

He gives account creation as an example. The traditional path forced new users through networking and identity setup before they could build. The newer path lets someone start fast with sensible defaults, then grow into the full controls later without migrating to a different kind of account.

A recurring tension is durability. Production databases need strong guarantees, but an agent may only need a database briefly. Garman says AWS is trying to support fast, disposable use without quietly offering a fragile option that later turns out to matter.

Scarce accelerators drive the next part of the discussion. Garman describes enormous demand from frontier labs, large enterprises, and startups at the same time, with Amazon planning very large purchases and a capital program he puts at about two hundred twenty billion dollars for 2026.

His answer on allocation is deliberate restraint. AWS could sell far more capacity to the biggest labs, he says, but it reserves room for startups and a broader base because a healthier ecosystem and less customer concentration reduce risk over time.

He also pushes back on bubble talk. In his view, much current spending supports core compute, storage, and inference tied to workloads customers already value, which makes the demand look more durable than a single speculative training race.

The bottleneck, though, keeps moving. Power one month can become memory, chip supply, networking parts, construction labor, or simple geography the next. Garman says planning now stretches years ahead and deep into the supply chain, including energy projects AWS helps bring onto the grid.

On data centers and public skepticism, he concedes the industry has explained itself poorly. He points to renewable energy, limited water use, local jobs, and tax revenue as benefits that often stay invisible to nearby communities.

The chip story follows a similar pattern of starting narrow. An offload card first reduced virtualization overhead, then became the seed for Graviton servers, and later for the Trainium family now used heavily for both training and inference, including much of the traffic behind Bedrock.

For enterprises, Garman says the agents in production today are mostly simple and still supervised. The bigger unlock, he argues, is not copying a human workflow step by step. It is redesigning the work so an agent can explore in parallel and reach the goal by a different route.

Trust is the brake. Companies worry about permissions, sandboxing, guardrails, and evaluation, especially the risk of an automated system damaging production data. AWS is answering with forward deployed teams that work beside customers for a short sprint, teach the evaluation and data habits, and then leave the customer able to run the system itself.

Data control sits underneath that pitch. Garman stresses that Bedrock was designed so customer data stays inside the customer's environment instead of flowing back to a model provider, and he sees growing interest in open weight models that enterprises can adapt on their own data inside SageMaker.

Security gets the same treatment in reverse. Beyond defending against stronger models, AWS is using them in a service called Continuum to hunt for weaknesses, rank what matters using knowledge of the customer's environment, and move protection closer to machine speed.

Inside Amazon, he says, agents now show up in software development, security work, and ordinary business teams through Amazon Quick. The organizational effect he describes is smaller teams moving faster from project to project, though he is candid that the right long term structure is still being experimented with.

Open questions

  1. If accelerators stay scarce, how will reserved capacity for startups be judged when the biggest labs can pay more and commit earlier?
  2. Can evaluation, permissions, and sandboxing mature fast enough for enterprises to let agents run unsupervised on consequential workflows?
  3. As bottlenecks rotate through power, memory, chips, and people, which constraint will actually set the pace of cloud expansion in 2027 and 2028?

Sam Witteveen

Microsoft Joins the Local AI Push

On Sam Witteveen, the creator picks through the Microsoft Windows and Surface event in San Francisco and finds the local AI story more interesting than the new laptop headlines. The opening demo matters most to him: a coding agent session that sent 1.6 million tokens into a model and another 10 thousand or so out, a ratio of about 150 to 1, with the bill for that work coming to zero dollars because the model was running on the laptop itself. It was not a staged toy either. The agent was digging through GitHub issues on the Windows Terminal code base, and the decision to run locally was made by the tool, not by the developer.

That routing idea is what Microsoft is calling hybrid intelligence. The Windows team lead described it as intelligent routing: models, a runtime, and the chipset all working together. In the GitHub Copilot demo the model picker was set to auto, and the new version of auto no longer just picks between cloud models. It can also pick a local one. A simple cleanup task on the code base went to the Microsoft AI Code Flash model running on the machine, with the GPU visibly ramping up. The presenter even pointed out that the laptop could stay in airplane mode and keep going. For a harder job, to go through issues tagged Needs Repo, a cloud model from OpenAI did the planning and then handed off to three sub-agents running locally. The bulk of the reading stayed on the device. That split, with the expensive model doing the thinking and the cheap local models doing the bulk reading, is a pattern he recognises from agent work everywhere.

The release timing is part of the interest. That Copilot app with local context, local actions, and local models is set to ship on October 15th, with more following over the next few months. The question he keeps coming back to is what happens when the router gets it wrong. If the local model cannot handle the task it was sent, does it notice and send it back to the cloud, or do you just get a weaker answer and never quite know what failed? He notes that no one showed that hand back at the event, and that every router lives or dies on exactly that decision. NVIDIA's PAIR and other routing projects have chased the same problem.

Then there is what actually runs locally. The first model is the Microsoft AI Code 1.1 Flash model, the coding model introduced at Build. Microsoft has cut it down to 3 bits, which shrinks its size by close to 80 percent, and now runs it on these devices with a 256K context window fully on machine. He is on the fence about a 3-bit coding model and says time will tell whether Microsoft has genuinely worked something out or whether the system will end up calling the cloud for anything remotely difficult.

The second model is a new Nemotron running at 2 bits in about 20 gigs of memory, pitched as a good fit for the RTX Spark. He reminds viewers that the RTX Spark will not only come in a 128 gig version. DGX Spark already includes a 64 gig version, and the RTX Spark might even go down to a 24 gig version. The third model is the one he is most suspicious of: DeepSeek V4 running at 1.6 bits in about 60 gigs of memory. The line on stage was that a year ago that capability was only available from frontier cloud models. The 1.6 bits is an average, meaning some layers stay at higher precision and the rest are pushed very low. His three checks are whether the coding pass rate holds up against a higher precision version of the same model, whether it falls apart at long context, and whether structured output still produces clean tool calls, because a broken tool call is generally a failed task for any agent. Long context is not free either. At 256K the KV cache takes a lot of memory on top of the weights, so his guess is that you will need one of the bigger memory machines to run it properly. It also feeds a debate he raises himself: do you go for a bigger model at a lower quantization, or a smaller model at a higher one? His own anecdotal experience is that, as long as you do not go below 4-bit, the bigger model usually wins.

The runtime piece is what he calls a clear win. Microsoft is bringing llama.cpp inside Windows ML, the runtime for deploying models across GPUs, NPUs, and CPUs. That buys a lot of compatibility: when a new model drops and a GGUF version shows up a few hours later, you are not waiting for Microsoft to package it. That includes fine-tuned or uncensored versions Microsoft would never convert for their own system. For anyone building a Windows app that ships with a local model, it is now one runtime that handles whatever hardware the user actually has, as long as that hardware is reasonably recent. What he does not yet know is whether you can plug your own model into the Copilot router or whether it only ever uses Microsoft's picks. If any model can plug in, you could decide you want a Qwen mixture of experts model inside your operating system and have a bunch of apps use it automatically. He thinks that alone could be a huge win for Microsoft.

Agents running locally also need guardrails. Microsoft's version is Microsoft Execution Containers, or MXC: a runtime sandbox where organizations set policies for what an agent can access and Windows itself enforces them while the agent runs. It reminded him of NVIDIA's OpenShell and of Docker sandboxes. The part he finds most interesting is agent identity: every action the agent takes is attributed to the agent, not to you. That helps with tracing, and it also means you can block an agent from an action and have it lose access while you, the logged in user, still keep yours.

Finally there is the hardware itself. These first RTX Spark machines go up to 128 gigs of unified memory with full CUDA support, on a Windows laptop, with no need to jump into Linux. Coverage he read puts the GPU at around an RTX 5070 level, with 20 CPU cores and a draw of no more than 80 watts. Prices started around $2,600, and the more memory you put in, the more the price goes up, with the current memory and RAM costs pushing all of this hardware higher anyway. Alongside the laptop there is a desktop tier, and Microsoft teased a Windows version of the DGX Station coming later this year. He hopes to get his hands on an RTX Spark and test what these quantized models actually perform like, and for which groups of people any of this is a viable alternative to a DGX Spark.

His overall take is that Microsoft just planted a flag. You are going to need to run models locally, not just use the cloud. Until now local AI has been pushed by enthusiasts, ML people, startups, and by NVIDIA this year. Having Microsoft join that bandwagon is, for him, a step toward local AI becoming the norm rather than the exception.

Open questions

  1. When the local model cannot handle what the Copilot router sent it, does anything notice and hand the task back to the cloud, or do you just get a weaker answer?
  2. How much coding quality actually survives at 3-bit, 2-bit, and 1.6-bit quantization when you test the same model at higher precision?
  3. Will you be able to plug your own model into the Copilot router, or will it only ever use the models Microsoft picks?

Forbes

Companies No Longer Run Monday Through Friday: How AI Is Changing The Norm

Forbes opens with a simple claim in its title: the five day workweek is giving way to something always on. The conversation is a fireside chat from the 2026 Forbes Future of Work Summit. Maneet Ahuja interviews Alexa von Tobel, who built LearnVest, later ran innovation inside a large legacy financial firm, and now leads Inspired Capital.

Von Tobel frames the moment as a speed contest. Her image is that fast organizations beat slow ones, and she says the pace of required adaptation is unlike anything in the careers of people in the room. She ties that pressure to a basic shift: high level reasoning help is now available to almost everyone, around the clock, at very low cost.

From the investor seat, she says AI is doing two things at once for large companies. It can lift revenue, and it can cut costs. That double effect explains both the optimism and the anxiety. Leaders may talk about productivity, but she argues the more interesting goal is making customers happier, not just answering support questions faster.

Her favorite example is IKEA. When AI took over much of routine customer support, the company did not simply remove those workers. It moved people who already knew the product catalog into design help for customers. The point is retooling talent toward revenue and judgment, rather than treating every automation gain as a headcount cut.

She gives the same advice to portfolio companies: use AI everywhere, especially where it supports people. Inspired Capital backs workplace and fintech tools, and she points to Windmill as the clearest case. Windmill plugs into tools such as Slack, GitHub, and Google Workspace, pulls work signals into one place, helps prepare reviews, gives employees a private coaching style assistant, and keeps daily work aligned to company goals. She says employees like it because it helps them get better at their jobs, not because it watches them harder.

The talk also stresses how hard change feels. Von Tobel notes that software cycles have collapsed from two week sprints to weekly pushes to hourly releases, and soon to agents writing code directly. Humans, by contrast, adapt slowly. Her warning is blunt: if you are not using AI across your work, you are falling behind. The practical consequence is that companies now run continuously, not Monday through Friday, and she expects that norm to reach even the largest firms.

She closes by arguing that automation raises the value of human strengths. As narrow tasks disappear, workers move up to supervising broader systems, and managers can oversee more than one operation. The skills that matter most are strategy, context, sales, communication, emotional intelligence, and conviction when a hard call has incomplete information. In her lightning round, she says data entry will fade, teaching soft skills will grow, and the best career advice is to treat work as the real thing, not a rehearsal.

Open questions

  1. If always on AI operations become normal, what new boundaries will workers and regulators demand around rest, attention, and accountability?
  2. Can tools like Windmill improve coaching and alignment without turning everyday work into constant measurement that employees learn to game?
  3. Which human skills should companies train first when entry level tasks vanish, and how will they know the training is working?

Claude · Mikhail Parakhin · Shopify

Inside Shopify's AI strategy with CTO Mikhail Parakhin

The Claude channel sat down with Mikhail Parakhin, Shopify's chief technology officer, for a conversation about how one of the largest commerce platforms uses AI internally, from unlimited model access for engineers to a simulated copy of an entire company.

Parakhin opens with a distinction he returns to throughout. Most AI adoption talk is about raising the floor, doing existing work faster. He cares more about raising the ceiling, doing work that was previously out of reach at any budget, and he argues the ceiling is where the real impact sits.

His own example is personal. He describes working for seven years on a machine learning problem involving a Wasserstein loss function, without solving it, and then cracking it in collaboration with a frontier model. Neither side could have done it alone, he says. The pairing behaved like the centaur teams from chess, where a human and a machine together outperform either one.

On measurement, he claims Shopify is unusually rigorous about the floor side of the ledger. Beyond counting pull requests, the company estimates the complexity of projects and tracks how much more teams execute, normalized for that complexity, which he says makes productivity gains measurable in precise percentages.

The ceiling side resists tidy metrics, so his advice to other technology leaders is behavioral. Always start with the largest model available, because a smaller model hides what you are missing. Shopify gives every employee unlimited tokens and runs the heaviest models it can get, backed by serious spending on inference and training capacity. He adds that anyone making decisions about AI has to actually use the product, or the returns will never become obvious to them.

His proudest example is a prediction system built on a simple analogy. Text is a sequence of tokens a model learns to continue, and a company, he argues, is also a sequence: opening a credit line, shipping a product, accepting cards, taking a loan, plus the countless small actions of every employee. Feed that sequence to a model and you get a digital twin of the business, a simulated merchant you can experiment on without risking anything real.

The point of the twin is counterfactuals. What happens if this merchant gets a loan, ships a day faster, or starts an advertising campaign? Shopify searches for the sequence of interventions most likely to grow the business, then acts on the answer in production, offering the loan or sending the nudge. Parakhin says the system has direct impact on the bottom line, and that it simply could not have been built before, because the talent concentration and foresight it required were not humanly feasible.

He also describes changing his mind about management. He once assumed language models could not help him run a team, then built a system that watches everything happening across his organization and flags trouble early: projects about to slip, deadlines at risk, even signs of unhappiness, so problems surface before they land.

The role of the engineer shifts accordingly. Because a model tends to give you what your question implies, asking for the wrong thing now produces the wrong thing almost immediately, with no meeting in between to catch it. Technical judgment about what is actually needed matters more, and engineers increasingly supervise models rather than type code, dispatching work across agents, keeping track of what each is doing, and preventing conflicts between them.

His closing advice for other CTOs is a barbell. On the development side, never economize: use the largest model for coding, testing, and automated research, with circuit breakers only to catch runaway processes. On the production side, be frugal: run the right sized model, often a fine tuned cheaper one, and let automated research tune the balance of cost, throughput, and quality. The money saved in production funds the frontier models in development, where he believes each generation, Fable 5 included, is a bigger step than people expect. Companies that invert the barbell, pinching on development tokens while burning large models in production, will be outcompeted, he predicts.

The conversation ends on a joke at his own expense. Parakhin had long planned to spend his retirement deciphering Linear A, the Bronze Age script of Minoan Crete, until Boris pointed out that someone had already done it with Claude.

Open questions

  1. The digital twin is trained on Shopify's own merchant history. How much of the approach transfers to companies that lack a platform's worth of behavioral data?
  2. Unlimited tokens and the largest models for everyone is an expensive default. What does the discipline actually cost at Shopify's scale, and where do the circuit breakers bite in practice?
  3. If engineers increasingly manage agents rather than write code, how do junior engineers build the judgment that the new role demands?

USV’s New $900M Fund: Fred Wilson, Rebecca Kaden & Mike Mignano

Sourcery with Molly O'Shea sits down at Union Square Ventures' New York office with general partners Fred Wilson, Rebecca Kaden, and Michael Mignano. The occasion is USV's new $900 million fund cycle, by far the largest the firm has ever raised, and the conversation uses the announcement to lay out how USV thinks the AI era should be funded.

  3. Can an open "Rebel Alliance" stack really hold its own against the integrated offerings of the established AI winners?

Data Centers Aren't Killing the Grid. They Could SAVE It.

This Week in AI, hosted by Jason Calacanis, leads with a reversal. Data centers have gone from courted tenants to local villains, mostly over fears about electricity bills, and the first guest argues the facilities could become an asset to the grid instead of a strain on it.

Varun Sivaram, founder and chief executive of Emerald AI, says the goal is not for AI to use less energy. AI will use far more. The goal is to connect far more computing to the grid we already have while keeping bills affordable and reliability intact, and he says the only route to all three is flexibility.

His software, Emerald Conductor, treats a data center as a controllable load. On the rare hours when a grid is stressed, a facility can slow some jobs, pause others, shift work to another city, or lean on batteries. The stresses are local and brief: a heat spike, a cold snap, an evening surge, even a halftime rush of kettles in London, where Emerald has already run a live trial with the national utility.

The scale claim rests on spare capacity. Sivaram cites Duke University research putting roughly 100 gigawatts within reach if large users trim demand slightly for a couple of hundred hours a year, about 2 percent of the time or less. He compares the grid to a highway built for a rush hour that comes twice a month, and notes that American grid utilization averages around half.

That leads to what he calls a grand bargain. Utilities would offer a new, interruptible class of service, and flexible data centers would get faster, larger connections in return, jumping the interconnection line instead of waiting years for new lines and substations. On the computing side, customers would accept service agreements in which some workloads can be gracefully slowed, as long as mission critical jobs are protected or moved elsewhere without users noticing.

The stakes, in his telling, are competitive and local. The United States wants to plug in far more AI capacity by 2028 than it is on pace to connect, while China is adding generation at a pace he says America cannot match this decade. He argues against the escape routes now in fashion, off grid private power and orbital data centers, because they decouple AI revenue from the public grid that households depend on. A flexible facility in Virginia, built with NVIDIA and Digital Realty at 100 megawatts, is offered as the reference design, backed by a $150M Series A and a new industry alliance with Google, NVIDIA, and Anthropic.
