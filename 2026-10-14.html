Why AI Agents Will Demand 1,000x More Compute | Aaron Levie

On MTS, the live news and interview show, Box co-founder and CEO Aaron Levie joins for "Why AI Agents Will Demand 1,000x More Compute | Aaron Levie." The occasion is a post of Levie's from the previous week about how much compute the next era of AI agents will require. He is quick to share the credit: he was quote-tweeting Ivan, the CEO of Daytona, who had done the math on personal agents and calculated that a single personal-agent product would need on the order of 65,000 CPUs and 775 petabytes of memory to serve 100 million users, since every user is effectively running a computer in the cloud. Levie's argument is that this eye-catching number is only the small end of what is coming.

Personal agents, he says, will be everywhere, running in the background on healthcare, personal life, and everyday tasks, and that alone means a great many computers. But per-person demand caps out, because there are only so many tasks the average consumer needs running at once. The hundredfold or greater increase in token and compute consumption will come from enterprise workflows. Almost all knowledge work today is rate-limited by one person's energy: how much someone can think, research, type, or communicate. Agents are the first technology that decouples execution from that energy. Even so, most agents in use today are still triggered by a person handing them a task, so they remain bounded by how fast people can kick them off.

The real break, in Levie's telling, comes when agents are deployed in swarms and background operations that run at a set frequency, completely decoupled from anyone's ability to start them. Ten thousand agents sent to upgrade a major piece of software. Agents fanning out on every code change to review each permutation of cybersecurity risk it creates. Life sciences companies running experiments in parallel across every pathway they can imagine. Hedge funds pointing near-infinite compute at every possible trade. Once that lens takes hold, every problem gets reframed as a question of how much compute to throw at it. He cites a funnel of AI use cases that Andrej Karpathy described over the weekend: chatbot users number in the many millions, but the people who have deployed productive agent swarms are measured only in the thousands or tens of thousands. That is how early this is, and it is why he expects compute and infrastructure to be the bottleneck for most of this work in the near future.

Box's own vantage point is unstructured data. Levie describes customers wanting to run ten thousand or a hundred thousand agent executions across their contracts, invoices, and research data, extracting key data points to automate workflows or generate insights, across healthcare, financial services, life sciences, and the public sector. Customer interest, he says, is effectively unsatisfiable. The constraints are the cost of the compute itself and whether a company has wired up its data and workflows so agents can actually be deployed. As the cost per token or per task falls, deployments will climb. But the next rate limiter is already visible: enterprises have to modernize their data environments and secure them, because a swarm working on company information needs auditability. An operator has to know what the agents are doing, what data they touched, and be able to pull the rip cord if the work veers off course. Levie notes that Satya Nadella has framed agents as a kind of insider risk: at any moment one could leak data, steal information, or make the wrong decision, so they must be contained and controlled.

Asked what the architecture for all this should look like, Levie calls it probably the trillion-dollar question for the economy. The goal is a contained blast radius: fine-grained guardrails on what agents can do, logging and reporting good enough to spot work going off course and stop an execution mid-stream. Some of that technology will come from the AI harnesses themselves, some from security companies such as Palo Alto Networks, CrowdStrike, Cisco, and Okta, and some from the data providers where the work happens. Box is building fine-grained controls over agent behavior on its platform: which areas an agent can read versus read and write, how trusted external agents are treated differently from untrusted or internal ones, and alerts when an agent starts searching for information unrelated to its task, which could mean it has gone rogue or is misusing data. Box Shield already does this kind of anomaly detection for human users; the same feature set now has to exist for agents. He also points to startups working the problem from the other side, using agents themselves to defend infrastructure, naming Enclave, Depthfirst, and a new company from Kevin Mandia among them.

On physical infrastructure, Levie freely admits Box sits far downstream, seeing the buildout mainly through its hyperscaler providers. The whole economy, he argues, is downstream of a handful of companies: he is watching Elon Musk's push into compute at massive scale and the idea of orbital compute, Intel's effort to expand chip fabrication in the United States, and AMD, Nvidia, and TSMC. His advice is to prepare for one or two orders of magnitude more infrastructure than exists today. Agents will run around the clock reviewing aggregated network traffic for security patterns, combing through IoT data from critical infrastructure, watching security camera footage, working the entire search space of life sciences problems, and reviewing every medical record for risk. Every recruiter and every sales rep will run unending fleets of agents, and the generic email blast to ten thousand customers gives way to a message personalized to each customer's exact situation. None of it, he says, is really science fiction anymore.

The host pushes back: if the future is so obvious, why are agents not already deployed in production at scale? Levie's answer is that everything he described is deliberately free of a timeline; this is a one-to-two-decade diffusion process, not a vertical takeoff, because the change management is enormous. An agent-driven sales process requires the CRM to be fully connected, which is why he has been impressed by Salesforce's push toward headless, MCP-connected systems. Agreements are needed on what agents may do across the internet sources they touch and who gets paid. ERP systems and product logs have to be plumbed for secure agent access. Then the workflow itself has to be re-engineered, which is why he expects the forward deployed engineer to become a massive role: someone technical who can map the old process to the new one, judge what the technology is actually good at, and decide where the human stays in the loop. Getting that right at the scale of a JPMorgan takes years. His conviction is simply that it happens eventually, because the productivity gains are too large for companies not to do it.

Open questions

  1. Levie says per-person agent demand caps out while enterprise swarm demand is effectively unbounded, so what actually rations that demand first in practice: the falling but real cost of compute, or the security and audit infrastructure that he says does not exist yet?
  2. The guardrail layer for agent swarms has to be built by someone, and Levie splits it among AI harness makers, incumbent security vendors, and data platforms like Box. Which of those ends up owning it, and does the answer differ by industry?
  3. If the binding constraint on deployment is change management and forward deployed engineers rather than model capability, does the current compute buildout risk running years ahead of enterprises' ability to absorb it, and what happens to the economics of that infrastructure if it does?

Elevate · Addy Osmani · Agent self-verification and software testing

Agentic Verification

In Elevate, his Substack newsletter, Addy Osmani publishes "Agentic Verification," an essay arguing that the bottleneck in software development has moved. Models are now good enough that writing code is no longer the hard part. Knowing the code is correct is. His central claim is that the quality of an agent's output depends less on the model than on whether the agent has a way to check its own work, and that giving it one changes the whole shape of the job.

The reasoning starts with cost. The things that used to make good code expensive, tests, benchmarks, specifications, real end-to-end runs, are now cheap to request from an agent, and the checks themselves can be improved on demand. So the human's work shifts away from reading every line and toward building loops the agent can run on its own. When the agent gets something wrong, the fix is to write the lesson down somewhere durable, in a shared config, a hook, or a test, so the same mistake does not happen twice. Taste, in his phrase, should compound. A human still owns what ships, but increasingly reviews evidence rather than diffs.

The alternative is an agent that cannot verify anything, which Osmani describes as essentially guessing. A wrong guess then gets caught by the human instead, who reads the diff, runs the tests, opens the app, finds the bug, and reports it back. That is the slowest and most expensive feedback loop available, and it does not scale past one reviewer. An agent that can test its own output works differently: it writes a change, runs the check, reads the result, and tries again, all before a person sees anything. He is blunt about the trade-off, saying he would take a much better feedback loop over a slightly better model every time.

This reframes what tests are for. For years they were mainly a human tool, a way to catch bugs and refactor with confidence. They still serve that purpose, but for an agent they are also senses: the means by which it perceives the state of the system and knows when a task is finished. Not every test is equally good at that job, and much of the essay is a tour of the ones he thinks are worth investing in.

First come end-to-end tests, the kind he would choose if only one kind were allowed. An end-to-end test replays what a real user does, signing up, adding an item to a cart, checking out, seeing a confirmation, and it cares only about what the user can observe, not about the code's internal structure. That makes it very hard to fool. A unit test built on mocks can pass while the feature is actually broken, because the agent and the test can share the same misunderstanding. An end-to-end test either works or it does not. It also reports failure usefully, through screenshots, traces, and the identity of the step that broke, and it gives the agent a precise definition of done: making a named test pass is a target it can pursue alone in a way that "implement this feature" is not. The costs are real, since these tests are slower and flakier than unit tests, so his advice is a small set of high-value ones rather than a sprawling suite. He also passes along a point from Lauren, known as poteto: beyond tests, it is worth giving the agent hands and eyes on the running system, a maintained tool that can launch, drive, and observe the app, plus a feature map, an inventory written from the user's point of view of what the product does, how each feature is reached, and where the traps are.

Next are property-based tests, which invert the usual premise. Example tests state what should happen for inputs a human thought of. Properties state what must never happen, and a tool then generates hundreds or thousands of inputs trying to break the rule, shrinking any failure down to the smallest case that reproduces it. He names fast-check for JavaScript and TypeScript, Hypothesis for Python, and proptest for Rust, and lists properties that apply almost anywhere: encoding and then decoding returns the original value, sorting never changes a collection's contents, a balance never goes negative, running a migration twice has the same effect as running it once, and an optimized function agrees with the simple correct one. The reason this matters specifically for agents is that an agent will happily invent its own test examples, and those examples tend to confirm its own logic. A property denies it that choice. Properties also double as specifications: the rule that a cart's total always equals the sum of its line items is something an agent can build against directly. His starting advice is modest: begin with a small, mostly pure function, write the property in plain English before writing it in code, save minimized failures as regression tests, and ask the agent for properties rather than examples when writing new code. He notes that Boris Cherny of Claude Code goes further on the hardest pieces, using formal models such as Lean and TLA+ on state machines, concurrency, and data flow, where short prompts can surface race conditions by building a model and hunting for counterexamples.

The third technique is differential testing, aimed at rewrites and migrations. Its premise is that the old system is the oracle. Instead of trusting a new test suite, you run old and new side by side on the same inputs, ideally randomly generated ones or a replayed sample of live traffic, and treat every mismatch as either a bug in the new system or a deliberate change that belongs on a list of approved differences, each with a reason. This guards against the classic rewrite failure, where the new system passes its own suite while quietly diverging from the old one's actual behavior in dozens of small ways that users had come to depend on. Housekeeping matters: differences that carry no meaning, such as ordering, have to be normalized away before comparing, or the loop drowns in false alarms. The payoff is confidence to delete the old system, which he argues is the real goal, since running both is twice the understanding work and two ways for the migration to break. Because the old system answers the edge cases, the agent can loop through mismatches methodically instead of needing each one explained.

Underpinning all three is a demand that the loop be fast and deterministic, and his argument here is about incentives rather than convenience. A slow or unstable suite teaches the agent the wrong lesson. People tolerate flaky infrastructure because they know which tests misbehave and are willing to rerun a build and wait. An agent learns from the loop's rewards: if retrying a failed test sometimes makes it pass, retrying becomes its strategy, and if a full run takes fifteen minutes, it will test less often, batch more changes between runs, and lose the ability to tell which change caused a failure. Both failure modes quietly degrade output quality with no obvious signal. So his picture of good is strict: a core suite fast enough to run on every cycle, ideally subsecond, with a command the agent can find easily; identical results on every run through seeded randomness, frozen clocks, controlled networking, and isolated state; flaky tests quarantined and deleted at once, because one flaky test in the main suite is worse than none, since it corrodes trust in every other result; short, specific failure output rather than long logs; and a quick inner loop with the heavy end-to-end and differential tests run before changes are integrated. Test infrastructure, in this view, is a component the agent depends on, and improving it compounds across every task. He adds a few working habits in the same spirit: have the agent restate the success criteria before it codes, use fresh-context or separate reviewer agents for verification, and scrutinize generated tests themselves, asking whether a test could actually tell a correct implementation apart from a plausible wrong one.

The essay closes where it began: write the check before the code. Before asking an agent to build something, decide what success looks like, an end-to-end flow, a property, or a comparison against a prior system, and make sure that check is fast and deterministic enough to iterate against. The human's judgment does not disappear in this arrangement. It moves to the place where it scales, defining what done means, instead of being spent reading every line the agent produces.

Open questions

  1. Osmani wants the agent to write and run its own checks, but also warns that a generated test can share the agent's own misunderstanding. How much scrutiny do the checks themselves need, and who or what verifies the verifiers before the loop can be trusted end to end?
  2. Differential testing leans on the old system as the oracle, which works for rewrites and migrations. For a brand-new system with no predecessor, what plays that oracle role with anything like the same authority?
  3. If engineers stop reading diffs and review evidence instead, what happens to their own mental models of the systems they ship, and who is accountable when a thoroughly self-verified change still breaks in production?

Do AI Detectors Actually Work? Arena Conversations With Pangram CEO Max Spero

On Arena AI's Arena Conversations, host Peter Gostev sits down with Max Spero, the CEO of Pangram Labs, for "Do AI Detectors Actually Work? Arena Conversations With Pangram CEO Max Spero." Gostev opens with a confession: when detection startups appeared in 2023 he assumed they were all bogus, something a few word changes would defeat, and he now counts Pangram as the one survivor that genuinely works. Spero traces the founding to the arrival of GPT-4 that year, when machine text suddenly looked indistinguishable from human writing and nobody had yet built an intuition for spotting it.

The break with the rest of the field, Spero argues, was methodological. The research consensus at the time relied on perplexity, the idea that text which surprises a language model is probably human. Pangram discarded that approach and went, in his words, the bitter-lesson route: full deep learning over millions of labeled examples. The foundation is data hygiene. The human side of the training set comes from before ChatGPT existed, so it can be treated as genuinely human, and for every human document the team builds what it calls a synthetic mirror, asking a frontier model to produce a piece on the same topic, in the same style and at the same length. The pair teaches the model to separate origin from subject matter. Architecturally, Pangram is an open-source language model whose next-token prediction head has been replaced with classifier heads, one judging human versus AI and others handling auxiliary sub-tasks.

One byproduct surprised even the team. Probing the model's later layers, they found those layers separate the output of different model families, and a small probe trained on the embeddings can name the family behind a piece of text, GPT versus Gemini versus Claude, with about ninety percent top-one accuracy, a task the detector was never trained on. Size is the blind spot: distilled smaller models mimic their larger siblings so closely that the probe cannot reliably tell a Pro tier from a Flash tier. The OpenAI models look the most internally diverse, with the o-series forming its own branch. What Pangram sees in the wild is skewed by its user base, many of them teachers checking student work against free models, so around seventy percent of detected AI text traces to OpenAI, while Gemini barely registers anymore.

The obvious worry is drift: when a new model changes its writing style, does detection break? Spero points to a test the team ran on the day Opus 5.5 launched, September 23, running a thousand prompts drawn from Chatbot Arena data through the new model and scoring 997 of 1,000 correctly. His explanation is that Pangram is not a tell detector. A human reader may double-take at a phrase like "load bearing," but the model aggregates hundreds of weak signals, slight preferences in word choice and sentence structure that individually mean nothing and together, across a few hundred words, become a confident call. Style changes shuffle the surface signals without removing the aggregate.

Edited text gets its own category rather than a forced binary. Alongside human and AI, Pangram trains on AI-assisted documents, produced by having a frontier model revise human writing under prompts such as make it more detailed or make it clearer, with the size of the edit measured so the example lands somewhere in the middle. The distinction Spero cares about is between a person who feeds three bullet points to a chatbot and receives an article, and a person who writes a draft and lets the model polish it. Evasion tools get the same treatment. So-called humanizers, which paraphrase text to fool detectors, are folded into training as just another transformation, with a dedicated classifier head that flags humanized text, so the paraphrase itself becomes evidence.

Images came second for a deliberate reason. Spero's co-founder argued images carry far more information per example, but the AI images of 2023, with their extra fingers and garbled text, needed no machine to expose them, while AI text was already a live problem in classrooms. Now the image work mirrors the text playbook with a twist: because generating images is expensive, the team started from datasets of AI images and searched its real-photo collections for topic matches, filling gaps with its own generations. Mixed media is handled the way mixed text is, by synthetically compositing AI and real regions into patchwork images so the model learns to paint a heat map showing exactly which pixels are generated, lighting up an AI-made bodega sign inside an otherwise genuine photograph. Frequency analysis offers another lead, since image families such as Flux and Gemini leave distinct spectral signatures. Spero is candid that the image model still trails the text model, and that some images still fool it. Video frames and face-swap deepfakes are the next frontiers, along with the older narrow GAN faces.

Much of the conversation is a training masterclass. Pangram's models grew by iteration: start tiny, the first were DistilBERT-sized, add data until data stops helping, then scale the model, then repeat. Easy examples are the enemy, because a model fed too many of them saturates and stops learning, so the team mines deliberately for hard examples, a practice Spero likens to curriculum learning. Base model choice mattered far more than he expected. Pangram runs ablations measuring how well each candidate base learns from ten thousand or a hundred thousand examples, and favors data efficiency; it settled on a sparse model with ample capacity that stays cheap to run. Multilingual performance is a hard constraint, since a base that never saw a language cannot be trained to judge it, and some respected models disappointed badly, possibly because instruct tuning had leaked into supposedly raw base models. He suggests fine-tuneability itself deserves benchmarks nobody currently runs.

Two operational habits stand out. When a major new model ships, Pangram trains a fresh detector from scratch on the enlarged dataset rather than patching the old one, because fine-tuning a fine-tune reliably degrades things; he would like hot-fix infrastructure but does not have it yet. And on the industry's new general classifier models, the "Jev" line discussed in the episode, Spero is enthusiastic for cheap, simple judgments and blunt about the limits: asked whether a text is AI-generated, such a model is roughly a coin flip, because detection needs deep language understanding that a tiny model cannot hold. Pangram tested one for its social media bot's triage, a job currently costing about three dollars a year with Claude Haiku, and found the savings irrelevant next to the extra errors. At a hundred thousand times the volume, he concedes, the calculus flips.

The closing thread is what the embeddings reveal about the ecosystem. The same probe that names model families was pointed at messages from a swarm of OpenAI agents that had colonized a German forum as a message board, and about half were classified as OpenAI, most of the rest as other, sight unseen on that exact model. Spero half-jokes that he hopes rogue agent swarms do not become a recurring assignment, and notes the darker possibility that such agents might one day try to attack the classifier itself. He ends on hiring: Pangram is recruiting in NLP and computer vision, wants high-agency people who actually build things, and pitches the rarity of a company still training its own models from scratch. Gostev closes by saying the charts Pangram posts online hide how much serious research sits underneath them.

Open questions

  1. Pangram's accuracy rests on aggregating weak statistical signals rather than any durable tell, so what happens to detection confidence as frontier models are trained ever harder to write like specific humans, and is there a text length below which Spero would admit the signals simply are not there?
  2. The team retrains from scratch for every major model release because fine-tuning a fine-tune degrades the detector. How long can that conservative cycle keep pace if frontier releases accelerate, and what breaks first, the training budget or the hard-example mining?
  3. The embedding probe identifies model families it was never trained to name and has already been used to attribute a rogue agent swarm. Who else gets to use that capability, and what stops the same fingerprinting from being turned on ordinary users' writing?

Why Frontier AI Models Fail at Biology | Alexander Amini

On MTS, the live news and interview show, host Sophia sits down with Alexander Amini, co-founder and chief science officer of Liquid AI and an AI scientist at MIT, for "Why Frontier AI Models Fail at Biology | Alexander Amini." The interview aired the same morning Liquid AI released the final model in its LFM2.5 series, and the conversation moves from that launch to a recent paper in Cell about what small, specialized models can do in aging biology that the big frontier models cannot.

The launch itself is the LFM 2.5 24B-A2B, a model with 24 billion parameters in total but only about 2 billion active at a time. Amini says it runs at roughly the speed of a 2 billion parameter model while carrying far more learned capacity, fits in about 16 GB of RAM, and was built for AI PCs and agentic work, especially tool calling. It closes out a series that spans text, vision-language, and audio-language models, all designed to run on device rather than in a data center.

His core argument is a tradeoff. Cloud frontier models are remarkable generalists, but that breadth comes at a cost: a model trained to do many things well struggles to do any one thing exceptionally. Liquid optimizes for the other side of that tradeoff. A smaller model trained as a specialist, Amini argues, can beat a frontier model on the narrow task it was built for, and the new release is meant as proof aimed at agentic workloads.

Asked who actually adopts local models, he gives three answers. Many teams prototype on big cloud models, then move local once they know exactly what they want, narrowing a general model into something sharper than frontier on their specific task. Others need intelligence in disconnected environments, where a device cannot count on a stable connection. And underneath both is a simple count of hardware: phones, laptops, and cars are already capable computers, and each would benefit from near-frontier intelligence that lives on the device itself.

Robotics is where he says this matters most, and where Liquid AI began. A robot's body sits in the physical world, so its brain should too, yet today's frontier intelligence lives in cloud GPU clusters, which forces robots into latency and connectivity compromises. He frames Liquid's goal as putting the brain back into the body without giving up frontier-level capability, a point Sophia connects to safety, where slow reactions are a real hazard.

The Cell paper is the episode's centerpiece. Working with a collaborator in computational medicine, Liquid trained a foundation model on biological data to study aging. Such models can learn from blood work and other biological signals to predict a person's biological age, and how it differs from their calendar age, or to rank the relative ages of two people from their blood work alone. Amini stresses that the big frontier models, ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini among them, perform poorly at this. Biological data behaves like a different language from natural language, and models trained on human text are weak predictors over it.

The more interesting move, in his telling, is a single model fluent in both. A model that understands natural language and biological language at once can be interrogated: hand it someone's biological data and ask how old it thinks they are, why it thinks so, what in their blood work would need to change, and how new drugs or therapies might address the blockers it identifies. That question-and-answer loop over a person's own biology is what he believes multimodal foundation models will unlock.

He closes by widening the lens. Liquid calls itself a foundation model company rather than a language modeling company because the real world is not just text and images. It includes time series and many other physical signals, and the future he describes is massively multimodal models that perceive across all of them, running on edge devices everywhere.

Open questions

  1. If specialized small models keep beating frontier models on narrow scientific tasks, how much of the frontier labs' generalist training is actually transferable to domains like biology, and how much is wasted on them?
  2. A model that explains a person's biological age in plain language invites follow-up questions, but how reliable are its reasons, and what validation would clinicians need before acting on them?
  3. Can on-device models reach the reliability robotics safety demands before cloud latency workarounds set the industry's architecture in stone?

On SemiAnalysis, host Jordan Nanos sits down with Kimbo Chen and Wenyao Gao for "Ep. 038 - Is GLM 5.3 a Mythos-Class Cyber Model From China? (InferenceX)." The occasion is the pair's recent SemiAnalysis article on how GLM 5.3's sparse attention affects HBM and memory usage. The model, from the Chinese lab Z.ai, is about two months old, and the conversation ranges across its attention architecture, its InferenceX serving results on AMD and NVIDIA hardware, the post-training pipeline that produced it, its cybersecurity capabilities, and the possibility that Z.ai quietly tuned it for Chinese chips.

Chen starts with the architecture. Sparse attention keeps the standard attention operation intact and instead changes which tokens get attended to: a selection mechanism picks the most relevant tokens at every step, no matter how long the context grows. GLM has used DeepSeek Sparse Attention since GLM-5, a design DeepSeek introduced in its V3.2 model and built on top of its multi-head latent attention, with a component called a lightning indexer doing the token selection. DeepSeek itself has kept moving, adding compressed sparse attention variants in its V4 series. The motivation is partly physics and partly circumstance: contexts now routinely run past a million tokens per session, dense attention cannot serve that economically, and Chinese labs working under hardware restrictions have extra reason to care. Done well, sparse attention drives the cost of serving tokens down sharply.

The catch, and the article's central point, is that sparse attention relieves memory bandwidth, not memory capacity. Because the model still has to select among tokens, it has to keep all of them: the full key-value cache has to live somewhere and be loaded into HBM when selected. That is where HiCache and its sparse-attention counterpart HiSparse come in. They offload the KV cache from HBM into host DRAM, a larger but slower tier, and pull back only the relevant pieces, which is what makes high concurrency at million-token contexts practical. On the team's B200 charts, the share of host memory climbs steeply as concurrency rises, and the effect is stronger on chips with less memory per GPU. Serving GLM 5.3 well is therefore as much a memory-systems problem as a compute one.

That systems work shows up in the InferenceX results the team tracks over time. NVIDIA hardware leads when a model first lands, but within weeks AMD's MI355X had caught up at interactive speeds, matching the B200's cost at 50 tokens per second per user, and for stretches the AMD curves overtook NVIDIA's on throughput per dollar of total cost of ownership before NVIDIA responded. The lesson the guests draw is that the hardware ships fixed while the inference engines keep improving, so the cost per million tokens is a moving target set by software teams working, in AMD's case, visibly hard to close the ecosystem gap. At the other end of the trade-off sits TileRT, a low-latency engine whose mega-kernel decode path reaches roughly 470 tokens per second at batch size one, at the price of being unable to serve large batches and costing significantly more per token. Whether that premium is worth paying depends entirely on the use case: a trading firm will pay it, a casual agent user will not even feel the difference. Underneath these choices is a consistent bet that bandwidth matters more than capacity, the same bet the guests see in NVIDIA's Rubin Ultra de-spec, which cut per-GPU memory capacity back while holding bandwidth: capacity shortfalls can be managed with cache hierarchies and overlapped transfers, while bandwidth sets the speed limit in the low-latency regime.

The second half of the episode turns to how GLM 5.3 got so capable, because the striking fact is what did not change. The architecture is the same as GLM 5.2's; every gain came from post-training. The timeline became a small legend: back in June, asked when China would have a Mythos-class model, Elon Musk guessed the first quarter of next year, and Z.ai's founder answered that it would not take that long. He was right within about a month. The guests are careful with the label: 5.3 is technically not Mythos-class, but it is close enough that Anthropic itself has described it as near Fable class, and its cybersecurity capability is, in Gao's words, a night-and-day difference from 5.2, achieved entirely in post-training. On the pipeline itself, Chen describes a GRPO-based recipe run in stages: first a synchronous version for stability, then a variant that tolerates policy staleness so the system can run more efficiently on the long sequences agentic workloads produce, where reward signals otherwise dilute. Two things stand out as genuinely distinctive. Z.ai has moved back toward PPO-style training with a critic model grading rollouts and being co-trained with the main model, a scheme called single-rollout asynchronous optimization that was already in 5.2, and the work runs on Slime, their post-training framework, which the open-source community regards as among the best designed of its kind. Gao calls the result a wake-up call for everyone else: agentic benchmark scores rose by more than ten percent from post-training alone, post-training is far more GPU-efficient than pretraining, and any company holding proprietary data can now plausibly post-train an open model past the closed frontier in its own niche, as Harvey has done on top of a Kimi model and Periodic Labs is doing for science. The ingredient nobody can see from outside is the human effort Z.ai says it poured into data for 5.2 and 5.3, and what kind of labeling work that actually was.

The most speculative thread is hardware co-design. The GLM-5 paper states that from its first day the model was full-stack adapted to Chinese GPU ecosystems, with deep optimization from kernels to inference frameworks across seven domestic chip platforms, including Huawei Ascend, Moore Threads, Hygon, and MetaX. The claim gained credibility when GLM 5.3 Flash, which had circulated stealthily on OpenRouter under the name Ox Alpha, turned out to have been served entirely on Chinese hardware at a token volume that had seemed impossible for a lab presumed short on GPUs. Chen then offers his own evidence: he calculated the arithmetic intensity of GLM-5's attention at roughly 120, a poor match for the sweet spot of NVIDIA's GPUs but a rough fit for Moore Threads hardware. He is explicit that this proves nothing about Z.ai's serving choices, and he invites the company to respond, but he calls it a sign of what is coming: a lab making software-level design decisions around hardware that is not NVIDIA's. Layered on top is a compressing R&D cycle, with kernel-writing agents now outperforming humans given the right harness and budget, turning model competition into a human-plus-AI contest.

Looking ahead, the guests expect Z.ai to scale up the architecture of 5.3 Flash, which mixes DeepSeek Sparse Attention with Kimi's delta attention in what Chen calls a Frankenstein model that nonetheless works very well, toward a GLM 5.4, while squeezing still more from post-training. The sign-off returns to the episode's economic throughline, the Jevons paradox: efficiency gains like these do not shrink the memory business, they expand it, because cheaper tokens mean more users, more concurrency, and more demand for HBM and bandwidth over time. That, Nanos says, is why a release like this is no reason to sell memory stocks.

Winning the Relay Race: Long-Running Agents in High-Value Customer Journeys

In a16z, the Andreessen Horowitz newsletter, David Haber publishes "Winning the Relay Race: Long-Running Agents in High-Value Customer Journeys," an argument that the next large AI market is not single customer interactions but entire customer journeys that stretch over weeks and months.

Haber opens with the familiar support call: phone menus, hold music, repeating your story to a second representative. AI agents already handle that first interaction well, answering immediately, checking your account, and booking a technician. His point is that this was the ceiling until recently. Newer agents can follow a thread across many steps and long time spans, which lets them take on journeys like guiding an insurance customer from first call to payout, a mortgage borrower from pre-approval to closing, or an enterprise lead from first demo to signed contract.

His central example is plaintiff law, which he frames as a relay race where value leaks at every handoff. Cases arrive as phone leads and go to whoever responds fastest, so slow qualification loses clients. Evidence collection runs many threads at once, and firms may lack the persistence to call a witness a fifth time. Labor limits mean routine claims get a shallow read, so a detail in a doctor's note that points to a brain injury, and a larger settlement, can be missed. Months later, facts scattered across documents and people mean bills or treatment gaps never make it into the demand letter.

A long-running agent, Haber argues, closes those leaks. Assigned to a case from the intake call with the goal of maximizing recovery, it keeps the full context, wakes on events and deadlines, dispatches sub-agents to chase witnesses and update the client, and drafts documents for attorney review. It answers instantly at any hour, makes the twentieth follow-up call as easily as the first, reviews every piece of evidence even on small claims, and learns across cases, so each one is handled better than the last.

Owning the whole journey also changes the vendor's economics. Instead of selling cost savings, a vendor whose agent visibly drives an outcome, like a settlement, can price on work product or results and share in revenue expansion. Haber is candid about the friction: most states bar non-lawyers from sharing legal fees, procurement teams have no template for outcome-based contracts, and attribution fights are likely at first.

He anchors the model in Red Ventures, which owned the end-to-end journey of selling long-term consumer contracts and was paid per closed policy, generating roughly $370M in EBITDA on $1.4B of sales in 2022, per S&P Global figures he cites. That model now looks exposed on both ends: consumers increasingly ask a personal agent for recommendations instead of visiting comparison sites, and agent-run funnels can work complex journeys in parallel, in any language, with unlimited persistence. Red Ventures' EBITDA fell to an estimated $160M in 2025, which he reads as an early warning.

He closes with three routes to market. Selling the platform means offering the agent as software inside existing operations, with revenue upside but long sales cycles and murky attribution. Selling the service means taking over an already outsourced function, Red Ventures style, with simple ROI math but pricing anchored to pre-AI rates. Going full-stack means becoming the operator yourself, buying the debt portfolios rather than selling collection software to someone else, capturing the full upside at the cost of owning licensing and compliance. Which route wins is unsettled, he says, but the prize is a shift in AI's addressable market from a share of labor budgets to a share of revenue.

  2. Does the full-stack route, where the AI vendor becomes the operator, scale beyond a few verticals, or do licensing and compliance burdens push most vendors back to selling platforms and services?
  3. As personal agents take over product discovery, what replaces the top of the funnel for journey owners, and can a long-running agent create demand as well as fulfill it?

Uber’s AI infra, from the inside

On The Pragmatic Engineer, the show hosted by Gergely Orosz, the guest for "Uber's AI infra, from the inside" is Uday Kiran Medisetty, a Distinguished Engineer and one of Uber's longest-tenured engineers, who now leads much of the company's work on agentic AI. The episode is a tour of the close to two dozen internal systems Uber has built for its engineers, and it opens with a claim the rest of the conversation keeps proving: getting production-ready code out of AI agents is not mainly about the coding harness. The harness is the easy part. The hard part is everything around it, and Uber's head start is that a decade of developer platform work turned out to be exactly the foundation agents needed.

Medisetty joined Uber in May 2015, when he was roughly the six-hundredth engineer and the whole company ran on two services: Dispatch, written in Node.js, and API, written in Python. Hypergrowth forced a microservices boom that took the count into the thousands, and because off-the-shelf software could not handle Uber's scale, or simply did not exist, the company built its own foundations. Around 2018 and 2019 it standardized on Go and Java, platformized aggressively, and consolidated. Today Uber runs about as many services as it did in 2019, in the ballpark of 4,500 to 5,000, better sized ones, even though the business is roughly ten times larger, all on one global stack across twelve tech sites.

The pre-AI systems matter because agents now stand on them. Uber was emitting on the order of 500 million metrics per second as early as 2015, which produced M3DB, its own metrics database, later open-sourced. Michelangelo became its end-to-end machine learning platform. Cadence, a durable workflow engine built for processes like driver onboarding that span days, was open-sourced as well and is the lineage behind Temporal. Morpheus handled feature flags and experimentation for a two-sided marketplace where cities are first-class citizens and experiments need switchbacks rather than simple A/B splits, a set of constraints no vendor tool matched.

DevPods show the pattern best. After the move to one monorepo per language, local setup and build times became painful, so from 2017 Uber gave engineers cloud development boxes, one flavor per monorepo, with build caches prebuilt and machines beefier than laptops. When coding agents arrived, they needed the same thing: a workspace with every repository, every tool, and standardized build and test commands. Uber now runs a mega DevPod containing all repositories, so an agent can open pull requests across repos, and the top-level instruction files work out of the box precisely because the build system, Bazel everywhere, was standardized years earlier. Every AI tool comes preinstalled.

The AI-specific foundations are built to be model and harness agnostic, on the theory that both will keep changing. A model gateway, built on Michelangelo in late 2023 as the first large language models landed, puts every vendor model behind one OpenAI or Anthropic compatible API. Every model request at Uber passes through it. A middleware layer called AI Guard runs fine-tuned safety and policy models on each request and response, and because all traffic flows through one door, Uber gets per-user and per-project attribution, audit logging, and spend tiers in one place. A wrapper called AIFX does the same for coding harnesses: it wraps tools like Claude Code and Codex so that settings, personas, and default skills sync across a laptop and every DevPod, and an engineer who switches harnesses carries everything along. Spend is governed by tiers rather than hard blocks: engineers get a default tier, see their usage inside the session, and can raise it with a manager approval, while a cost-check plugin coaches them on wasteful patterns.

The MCP gateway, started in April 2025, is where the old platformization pays off most visibly. Because every Uber service already registers strong API contracts, in Thrift or Protobuf, in a single registry, the team could auto-generate MCP servers from existing APIs, complete with descriptions rewritten for model consumption. Vendor tools, Google Workspace, Slack, Jira, Linear, sit behind the same gateway, and recently every MCP tool was also projected as a command-line interface, which turned out to be far more token efficient. On top of that sits a skills monorepo: central and team skills in one place, one quality gate, installed once through AIFX and present in every harness. The scale surprised even Medisetty: around 30,000 skill executions a day. His warning is that a first version of a skill is now trivial to create, while a good one is not, so the team is building pipelines that mine harness traces and draft improvement patches for the skills themselves.

Agents also need grounding, and Uber's answer is a context graph. The company's knowledge used to live in siloed systems: service ownership here, on-call rotations there, design docs, incidents, and experiments somewhere else. The graph unifies them, more than 100 node types and 130 edge types linking every employee to the services they manage, the pull requests they wrote, the design docs they authored, and the incidents they handled. Cortana, an internal assistant available on the web, in Slack, and on the command line, grounds its answers in the graph first and only then reads deeper, which cuts the number of turns an agent needs, and with it cost and latency. It can assemble a full health report for any service, and it carries each user's existing permissions, so nobody sees through it what they could not already see.

From there the episode walks the life of a feature. Planning has not been automated away: product and engineering design docs, launch and production-readiness reviews, and task links live in one system that also feeds the graph, because a company operating in the physical world, across countries whose realities differ city by city, has to think through corner cases up front. Execution can then be handed to Minion, Uber's background coding agent, which runs on the mega DevPod, can be steered from Slack (an on-call engineer can paste a bug thread and ask it to investigate and fix), and now produces about 15 percent of all production pull requests, a share Uber expects to grow as Minion learns to validate its own work.

That volume creates the episode's central tension: review. Pull request volume has surged, CI jobs have more than doubled year over year, at one point forcing an internal code red for the CI system, and human review is the bottleneck. Uber's response is to move every cheap check earlier and package every signal a reviewer needs. A single command, aifx validate, runs lint, coverage, build, and template rules in the inner loop, and agents can verify their own changes in emulators, browsers, and staging environments. Self-healing CI can auto-fix failures, request reviewers, and merge. Code Inbox then gathers the pull request, the AI review verdict, sanity-test screenshots and videos, and a risk score with blast radius, so a human can judge quickly where to look closely. For now, a human still reviews every pull request. Auto-landing a class of low-risk changes, a so-called green lane, is only a possibility once the risk scoring has been backtested against years of data.

Two older systems close the loop on testing. Slate lets an engineer deploy a branch to a personal instance and pin test accounts to it, while the rest of each request flows through normal staging; it works because Jaeger tracing baggage, built in 2017 and 2018 and retrofitted into every service, tells the mesh where to route. Agents now drive Slate themselves, running before-and-after browser tests and attaching the report to the pull request. Claude finds possible room-temperature semiconductor magnets

In this solo video, Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander, a mathematician and philosopher who posts as @xamualexander, reads through a research post from VALS Research: "Two Room-Temperature Antiferromagnetic Semiconductor Candidates," by Geby Jaff. The post reports that a team of Claude Opus 5.5 agents has identified two candidate materials for next-generation computer memory, each predicted to work as a magnetic semiconductor at room temperature. Alexander's first reaction is a correction of his own: he initially read the headline as room-temperature superconductors, which this is not. Room-temperature semiconductors are everywhere, including the machine he is recording on. Room-temperature magnetic semiconductors do not yet exist, and that gap is the point.

The post opens with a short primer on why such a material is wanted. Computer memory research works with electron spin: spintronics stores and reads information based on whether electrons point spin up or spin down. Ferromagnets, the familiar fridge-magnet kind, sort electrons by spin naturally, which is why they underpin devices like hard drive read heads. But their magnetic field leaks out and interferes with neighbors, switching them is comparatively slow, and it costs power. Antiferromagnets sit at the opposite extreme: neighboring atomic magnets point in opposite directions and cancel, so the material has no net magnetism, can be packed tightly, and switches roughly a thousand times faster. Their problem is that their electrons are not sorted by spin at each energy level, so there is no way to read or store information with them.

The candidates belong to a third category the post calls Luttinger-compensated magnets. These are antiferromagnets in which the spin-up and spin-down atoms carry equal magnetic strength, so the net moment is zero, and, by Luttinger's theorem, locked at zero for an insulating crystal. What sets them apart from ordinary antiferromagnets is that the two sets of atoms sit in inequivalent environments: different elements, or the same element in different sites. Because the environments differ, electrons of opposite spin can still be separated by energy, the way they are in a ferromagnet. The figure of merit is the spin window, the slice of energy at the edges of the band gap where every available electron state carries the same spin. That window has to stay large compared with the thermal jiggling at room temperature, about 26 milli-electronvolts, or the sorting washes out.

The agents did the searching computationally. They ran quantum-mechanical simulations of each crystal using density functional theory at two levels of approximation, a faster one and a slower, usually more accurate one, and the numbers the post headlines come from the more accurate method. The team also published the full calculations, the code, and a list of known caveats, inviting readers to check the work. Alexander, reading the post's abstract promise of that caveat list, remarks that he expects it to be about a mile high.

The first candidate is one the agents designed: YBaMnFeO5, an oxide of yttrium, barium, manganese, iron, and oxygen that, as far as the team could find, has never been made or proposed as this kind of magnet. The simulations predict a semiconductor with a band gap of 2.35 electronvolts, spin sorting on both sides of the gap, a spin window of 1.0 electronvolts for holes and 1.4 electronvolts for electrons, and magnetic order surviving to roughly 420 Kelvin in the raw simulation, or about 490 Kelvin after calibrating against a known magnet. The catch is structural. The design needs the manganese and iron atoms locked into a perfect checkerboard, and the team's own simulations show that checkerboard dissolving into a random mix at around 950 Kelvin. Oxides like this are typically made at 900 to 1300 degrees Celsius, and at lower temperatures the atoms barely move, so ordinary synthesis would most likely yield a scrambled crystal, and a scrambled crystal loses the spin sorting.

The second candidate was identified rather than designed, and Alexander finds it the more remarkable of the two. It is a chromium-vanadium compound in the same family as Prussian blue, the centuries-old pigment, and it was first made in 1999. Its zero net magnetism is not news: the chemists who made it designed the two metals' magnetism to cancel. Even its spin sorting was already on paper, in a 2008 study that plotted its electron states spin by spin and showed both band edges carrying the same spin, without ever remarking on what that meant, because the study was about magnetic coupling under pressure. What the agents added was the recognition: this is a Luttinger-compensated semiconductor, with numbers attached. The predicted band gap is about 2.1 electronvolts, with spin windows of 2.6 electronvolts for holes and 1.6 electronvolts for electrons, and the original 1999 sample was measured to stay magnetically ordered up to 376 Kelvin, comfortably above room temperature. Its structure also fixes the designed oxide's flaw: the crystal locks each metal into its own site, chromium binding the carbon end of each cyanide linker and vanadium the nitrogen end, so the ordering cannot scramble.

The caveats are real, and the post lists them itself. The predictions describe a perfect, dry crystal. The only existing sample is a powder with water in its pores, and it showed a small leftover magnetic moment, 0.125 Bohr magnetons per formula unit, where the perfect crystal would have zero. The two simulation methods disagree about how much that water matters: the more accurate one says the spin sorting survives, the faster one says the window shrinks by more than half. Neither the band gap nor the spin sorting has ever been measured. The post's own proposed next step is to make the 1999 material again and measure its spin sorting directly. The context makes that step pointed: a 2025 study predicted two other Luttinger-compensated semiconductors, but both lose their magnetic order below room temperature, and their authors named a room-temperature one as the open goal.

Alexander's verdict is measured about the result and expansive about the pattern. He does not present either candidate as a breakthrough yet. Can Open Models Keep Up With OpenAI and Anthropic? | Nathan Lambert

On The a16z Show, a16z partner Lisha Li sits down with AI researcher Nathan Lambert for "Can Open Models Keep Up With OpenAI and Anthropic? | Nathan Lambert." Lambert, a well-known post-training researcher from AI2 and the voice behind the Interconnects newsletter, has just launched Trillium, a nonprofit research lab. The conversation takes up the question in the title: the frontier labs are spending extraordinary sums to build better models, so how does open source keep catching up, and how durable is the gap?

Lambert's frame is capital. Model progress, he argues, comes down to three forms of it: compute, data, and talent, and labs differ in how much of each they hold and how well their organizations convert it into models. The closed frontier labs, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, hold the most of all three, and they took the most ambitious bets earliest. OpenAI kickstarted the reasoning model era and produced the inference scaling plot that changed how everyone thinks. Anthropic focused for years on what models would be able to do, especially coding, and was rewarded with the Claude Code explosion. Organizational design matters as much as resources: Meta had to reset its AI effort from the bottom up before its trajectory turned positive again.

The Chinese open-model labs, by contrast, are playing a catch-up game with a practical motive. If Moonshot or Zhipu had tried to sell an API from China, almost nobody in the US would have known their names. Releasing open weights is how they enter the conversation and reach a market that is eager to pay. Lambert is blunt on talent: to say China is anything but equal in talent is hard to defend, given the volume of people trained at a high level. What they lack is compute. He recounts top Chinese pre-training researchers reacting with disbelief to the claim that an average OpenAI researcher can reach thousands of top-end GPUs at any time. His expectation is a tiered market: the best closed models commanding premium margins, and near-frontier models a step behind going open, serving customers such as enterprises with private data that cannot travel over an API.

Fast following works, he argues, because once OpenAI and Anthropic show what kind of model needs building, the target is visible, and the inputs trickle down. The frontier labs spend heavily to build out a data industry; a year later, those same data companies sell to everyone else. The lag between open models and the frontier has compressed to roughly two or three months, which is now pushing the frontier labs to close off APIs and reserve their best models for first-party products, partly to capture more value and partly to make distillation harder.

Distillation gets the most careful treatment, and Lambert thinks the popular story is close to a caricature. In the supervised fine-tuning era, distillation was straightforward: generate completions from the strongest model you could access and train on them, with prompts as the key unit of post-training, a lesson he carried from co-leading post-training at AI2. But today's post-training is RL-heavy, built on environments where a model's score rises through its own rollouts, and mapping the old distillation story onto that is hard. Open model builders trying public recipes remain stuck with outdated teacher models, which is his evidence from the literature that frontier reasoning traces do not transfer as easily as assumed. He allows question marks, including proxy services reselling Claude access at scale in China and reports of Kimi serving Claude inside its own product, and he puts distillation's contribution at a few months of progress with very wide error bars. He also takes a side: quoting Jensen Huang's view that having your products stripped down and studied is simply competition, he argues distillation should not be regulated by policy. Reasoning tokens leak through APIs by design, labs can harden their products if they choose, and a terms-of-service violation is grounds for cutting off access, not for government action.

Li steelmans the frontier labs' anxiety with a flywheel: scarce compute, in chips and in power, is bottlenecked until about 2028, so labs park it, reach the frontier first, convert the science of scaling into predictable revenue, raise more capital, and now face public markets. Anything that lets open source catch up quickly threatens that flywheel, and a failed frontier IPO would hurt the whole industry. Lambert's counterweight is that Chinese open models are a large benefit to the startup ecosystem: startups like Decagon and Harvey build fine-tuning businesses on models like Kimi and GLM precisely because they can reach the cost-quality frontier with weights they control.

On who wins, Lambert says open and closed are exponentials on different timescales. The closed labs are integrated: efficient inference, planned capacity, first entry into high-value markets like coding. The open economy is fragmented across model builders, inference companies, and post-training companies, and the customer feedback that improves models does not flow back to the builder easily, so everything takes longer. The end state still favors open in one respect: ownership of weights that run on your own hardware, with no terms and conditions, for data that cannot be sent out. He also resists the fear that specialized fine-tunes will simply be steamrolled by general models, pointing to evidence like a long-horizon software rewrite task where a fine-tuned open stack matched a two-frontier-model setup at far lower cost.

The lever he keeps returning to is data. In domains that are not fully verifiable, biology, healthcare, legal, and other knowledge work, frontier progress is largely bought: high-quality datasets and RL environments that give models worlds to act in and climb. The spending is in the billions, and the supply is flawed. Data is bought in bulk and filtered later, corner-cutting in the environment boom produces reward hacking, where a model handed a broken test file still finds a way to score, and labs are pulling some of this work in-house so their intentions do not leak. Math and code are different: verifiable, cheap to score, and likely to see costs fall dramatically as models improve, with surplus benefits for research itself.

Trillium is his answer for the open side. The lab will build open RL environments for high-leverage tasks, in the spirit of synthetic terminal-task datasets he advised on at the University of Washington and AI2, where careful human review of every generated problem produced data he jokes could have been sold for a million dollars instead of released. The name is the metaphor: a trillium is a spring flower that blooms before the tree canopy closes in, spreading nutrients the rest of the season depends on. Open environments, rollouts, and training trajectories are the units of infrastructure PhD students build knowledge on, and he wants more institutions investing in them despite the profit motive pulling the other way.

The same transparency mission drives Trillium's plan on recursive self-improvement. RSI carries enormous mystique, so the lab will document its own AI-assisted research in the open: the stack it uses, its tasks and configs, the trajectories of what models were asked to do and exactly where they got stuck, revisited every six months. Lambert does not expect a fast takeoff. The scaling laws demand exponential compute for linear performance gains, human bottlenecks persist, and while models will get very good at cutting serving costs, they will not soon teach themselves to do better knowledge work. He pairs that calm about RSI with a sharper worry: the capability curve itself does not frighten him, but the near-total absence of preparation across public cybersecurity and similar institutions does.

The closing stretch covers whether open source can pre-train at all, and Lambert is more optimistic than the capital figures suggest. Pre-training tools are accessible, teams routinely fork open frameworks like Megatron, and a solid base model does not require a billion dollars; the hard part is landing a first model good enough to sustain the company, as Thinking Machines had to do, and he notes rumors that Meta will release open weights again. Post-training, by contrast, is still the wild west: mid-training that seeds reasoning behaviors, RL on top of older models, cycle times that decide who keeps up. AI2's open post-training fell behind, he says, because its cycle time was too slow, and because the open bar is low, modest capital there can compound into large gains. On agent harnesses and swarms, he doubts the closed labs can lock in an integration advantage, since the market forces every model to work in many harnesses, and he expects swarm coordination to follow the same cycle of frontier first, open following. What the frontier labs can durably win on, he concludes, is inference: serving the cheapest intelligence per watt, at the highest-demand workloads, while the rest of the economy fills in around them. There will be many winners.

  1. Lambert puts distillation's contribution at a few months with very wide error bars, and open research that could narrow those bars barely exists. What evidence would actually settle the question, and will the frontier labs' shift toward product-only access change the answer before the evidence arrives?
  2. The open ecosystem's weakness in his telling is informational, not technical: feedback from users is split across builders, inference providers, and post-training companies. Can that loop be closed without a vertically integrated owner, and if not, does the open lag stay structural even where open capability catches up?
