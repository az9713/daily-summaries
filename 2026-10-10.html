Interview with Ronald L. Rivest about Cryptography and Quantum Information Theory

Post Quantum Cryptography Inquiry published "Interview with Ronald L. Rivest about Cryptography and Quantum Information Theory," a conversation with Ronald L. Rivest recorded in his MIT office. The description identifies Rivest as a founder of MIT's Cryptography and Information Security Group, a professor in MIT's Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science, and a co-inventor of RSA with Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman. It also says the interview was made in June 2025 in Boston, as part of a video survey on cryptography under the quantum threat.

Rivest begins with the origin of RSA. The immediate prompt was the Diffie and Hellman paper that laid out public-key cryptography as an idea without supplying a working system. For Rivest and his co-inventors, the attraction was the open problem itself: find a hard mathematical problem and build a public-key system around it. He treats the later revelation that British intelligence had explored similar ideas earlier as mostly beside the point, because classified work could not be examined, tested, or used by the public research community.

The conversation keeps returning to the fact that modern infrastructure rests on assumptions that have worked for decades but remain unproved. Rivest notes his role in the early cryptography community and in hash functions, which now sit underneath systems far beyond the original setting, including blockchains and cryptocurrencies. That success does not remove the underlying risk. If P were equal to NP, he says, ordinary cryptography would effectively collapse, with one-time pads as the exception, because the hardness assumptions used across finance, communications, and public infrastructure would no longer hold.

On quantum computing, Rivest is calm and skeptical. He argues that quantum machines are often misunderstood as general accelerators, when the clearest famous use is narrower: Peter Shor's algorithm turns factoring into an engineering challenge if a large enough quantum computer can be built. That would threaten RSA directly, but Rivest stresses that quantum hardware still has much to prove. He is also wary of the larger promise attached to quantum computing, including claims about drug discovery, climate problems, and broad scientific transformation. In his view, the field may produce something durable, or it may join the list of ideas that looked revolutionary before engineering reality caught up.

A key distinction runs through the interview: the danger is not only quantum. A better classical factoring algorithm could arrive first, and it could change the situation just as suddenly. Rivest describes the crank messages every cryptographer receives from people claiming to have solved factoring, and his standard reply is to ask them to factor a concrete number. But if a real breakthrough appeared, he would expect months of checking before anyone knew how practical it was, how fast it ran, what key sizes it threatened, and whether the break was limited to factoring or damaged a wider class of assumptions.

That is why he praises NIST's post-quantum cryptography work. The point, as he frames it, is not to wait for panic. It is to gather the community early, compare proposals in public, standardize replacements, and let implementers prepare before a crisis forces the issue. Quantum cryptography is not necessary just to resist quantum attacks; the practical defense is quantum-resistant classical cryptography that can be deployed through software, standards, and careful migration. The old RSA story also carries a lesson here: an algorithm alone is not enough. Implementations need review, interoperability needs standards, and users need systems that can actually talk to one another.

Rivest also defends the ethics of trying to break cryptosystems. Secrecy, in his account, is a weak shield because knowledge held by one party tends to spread. Publishing RSA was partly a way to expose the idea to attack and learn whether it deserved trust. The same logic supports open scrutiny now: side channels, implementation flaws, and bad assumptions are found by people who are allowed to look hard at the system. If factoring fell, the response would not be his alone. It would depend on the shape of the break, involve the stakeholders, and aim at an orderly phase-out rather than pretending the old system could be saved by embarrassment or denial.

Open questions

  1. how much migration should happen now, while quantum hardware is still uncertain, without wasting effort or creating a false sense of safety.
  2. if factoring falls classically rather than through a quantum machine, how quickly could standards bodies and vendors separate a narrow factoring break from a broader collapse of assumptions.
  3. what useful work will large quantum computers actually prove good for beyond threatening public-key cryptography.

Interview with Kristin E. Lauter about Cryptography and Quantum Information Theory

Post Quantum Cryptography Inquiry published "Interview with Kristin E. Lauter about Cryptography and Quantum Information Theory," a conversation with Kristin E. Lauter recorded in San Francisco in May 2025. Lauter, a mathematician trained at the University of Chicago, spent two years on the University of Michigan faculty, then led cryptography and privacy research at Microsoft Research for 22 years before moving to Facebook AI Research, now Meta AI. She opens by placing her career between pure number theory and deployment: she went into industry to help elliptic curve cryptography work at scale, then helped invent a new hard problem for the post-quantum era.

The interview treats quantum computing less as a machine deadline and more as a mathematical change in what attackers can do. Shor's algorithm already gives a polynomial-time route against RSA and elliptic curve systems once a large, high-fidelity quantum computer exists. Lauter says her Microsoft work with quantum algorithms researchers even implemented the elliptic curve attack path in a compiler setting, estimating gates and runtime under assumed architectures. The uncertainty is not whether the math points at RSA and ECC, but when hardware reaches the scale and fidelity needed. That is why parameter choices have already been moving defensively: RSA 2048 has long been the baseline, while NSA guidance pushed elliptic curve keys from 256 toward 384 bits because ECC can require fewer quantum resources to attack.

Replacement is harder than swapping one signature for another. Blockchains and anonymous credential systems lean on elliptic curve signatures and pairing-based constructions such as BLS signatures, whose useful feature is that signatures can be combined and checked in flexible ways. A post-quantum signature therefore has to do more than resist quantum attack. It also needs properties that current systems use for credentials, smart contracts, and aggregation. Lauter expects new signatures across blockchains and Bitcoin-like systems, but she is frank that migration may mean re-signing, coordination, and something close to a hard fork, with users still needing to be convinced.

Her answer on access is reassuring in a specific way. Public-key cryptography has a strong open standards culture, shaped by academic, government, and industry researchers and by a bias against patent-encumbered standards. Once standards exist, procurement rules, compliance expectations, certification services, and interoperability pressure tend to spread them broadly. A company can try to wall off a proprietary secure network, but systems that do not interoperate get pushed aside. Internationally, she points to ISO as the established meeting point, while noting real differences in trust models. South Korea's government-run public key infrastructure interests her precisely because most countries leave that trusted directory role to companies.

The sharpest turn in the interview is her move from post-quantum cryptography to what she calls post-AI cryptography. At Meta, her team uses machine learning to attack lattice-based systems built on learning with errors, a problem whose hardness depends on learning a secret inner product after noise is added. Machine learning begins from almost the opposite instinct: noisy data can still be learned from. Her group has shown proof-of-concept secret recovery and is now trying to widen the range of secrets and parameters. The point is not to break the new standards for sport. It is to learn, before deployment hardens, whether AI attacks scale in a way that parameter choices must absorb. Lauter sleeps fine, she says, because cryptography has historically set parameters conservatively against known attacks. The open risk is that AI capability is improving faster than the field understands its scaling.

That AI thread also runs backward into mathematics itself. Lauter argues that models are still surprisingly uneven at math, even as math problems become a proxy for reasoning in benchmarks. Better AI attacks on hard problems could teach researchers both where new cryptosystems are fragile and how to make general models reason better. Her own favorite construction remains the supersingular isogeny graph work she co-invented: a vast graph whose nodes are supersingular elliptic curves and whose edges are maps between them, where security rests on finding a path between distant nodes in a graph far too large to search naively. The key-exchange line from that family was later broken, and she is careful not to overclaim. The original hash-function proposal, she says, has not been broken. For the standardized future, she points mainly to lattice-based and code-based systems selected by NIST in 2022, both tied at bottom to high-dimensional decoding problems.

The closing portion widens from algorithms to who gets to do mathematics. As president of the Association for Women in Mathematics from 2015 to 2017, Lauter helped build research networks that began with Women in Numbers and grew to more than 25 communities, supported by an NSF grant. She describes years of number theory conferences with few or no women speakers, top journals without women editors, and good work struggling for recognition. Industry, paradoxically, gave her a clearer argument for value: when the work is elliptic curve cryptography that needs to ship in products, the audience and stakes are easier to explain. The networks were her way of making that recognition less dependent on gatekeepers.

Open questions

  1. will AI attacks on learning-with-errors systems scale far enough, fast enough, to force larger parameters or different lattice assumptions after post-quantum deployment is already underway?
  2. can post-quantum signatures recover the pairing and aggregation features that blockchains and anonymous credentials now get from elliptic curves, without a migration so painful that users resist it?
  3. will open standards, certification, and interoperability keep post-quantum protection broadly available across countries, or will different trust models for public key infrastructure split the world into incompatible security regimes?

The Lab Data Proves the Intelligence Explosion Is a Myth | Ramez Naam

On MTS, investor and computer scientist Ramez Naam argues that the current evidence does not support an intelligence explosion. His target is the familiar recursive self-improvement story: a better AI designs better algorithms and chips, which produce a still better AI, which then accelerates away. Naam's objection is that the loop only runs away if each turn returns more than it costs. Most hard problems, he says, are worse than linear, so the loop meets diminishing returns before it becomes self-sustaining.

The core of the case is lab data rather than vibes. Naam points to OpenAI and Anthropic statements and system cards that describe AI helping researchers, but with weak conversion from input to research output. In the OpenAI figures he cites, researchers use far more tokens and far more compute, while the gains show up as much smaller increases in code written and experiments run. Anthropic's system cards, as he reads them, keep setting the important threshold at a large acceleration in AI development and keep reporting that current models are not there yet.

That gap explains why he thinks confident talk about recursive self-improvement can run ahead of the evidence. The old thought experiment, from I. J. Good and Vernor Vinge onward, makes the feedback loop sound automatic once machines start improving themselves. Naam says people often skip the coefficients: how much capability is gained, how much productivity follows, and whether the next round is easier or harder than the last. He assumes the labs are sincere, but he also notes that some of today's AI gains are being spent just to keep progress moving as problems get harder.

The constraints are not only mathematical. Naam argues that the pool of human-written training data is close to exhausted, so more gains have to come from synthetic data and reinforcement learning. Those methods are strongest where success is formal and checkable, such as math, code, and games. They are weaker where feedback is slow, messy, expensive, or dependent on human trials. Compute has a parallel problem: AI capital spending has grown beyond the free cash flow of the big technology companies, debt is filling the gap, and the revenue needed to justify the buildout rises toward a scale where wider economic limits start to matter.

None of that makes Naam an AI pessimist. He calls AI transformative and says adoption is still low among consumers and businesses, so even today's capabilities have room to spread. His forecast is continued striking progress in domains that can generate abundant data and judge results quickly. Biology is harder because the data are imperfect and experiments can be slow. Claims about curing aging run into human trials. Claims about machine hyper-persuasion run into the fact that influence is social, uneven, and hard to verify. The messier the feedback, the less the current scaling recipe looks like a shortcut.

The possible escape from diminishing returns is not more of the same loop. It is a change in what models can originate. Naam says current systems look useful for incremental research and experimentation, but much weaker at producing genuinely new ideas that are not already in the literature. That is why he watches efforts to test research taste and novel idea generation, and why rare mathematical breakthroughs matter to him. If a model could reliably create new concepts rather than only optimize inside known ones, the feedback loop he criticizes could start to behave differently.

Agent swarms do not solve the problem by themselves. Naam argues that test-time compute and multi-agent scaling show steep diminishing returns, in some readings worse than pre-training. Parallel agents help when a problem can be split into many independent tries, such as search or data aggregation. They help much less when the bottleneck is one deep insight. His analogy is blunt: putting hundreds of ordinary people in a room would not recreate Einstein's route to general relativity. What he wants from the labs is transparency: regular data on experiment rates, internal models, scaling coefficients, and whether those coefficients are improving. Without that, outsiders are arguing over a feedback loop whose key numbers are mostly private.

Open questions

  1. What internal lab data would show whether benchmark gains are actually converting into faster AI research over time?
  2. Can models move from incremental experimentation to reliable generation of novel, field-changing ideas?
  3. Which domains can be made verifiable enough for rapid AI progress, and which will stay limited by slow real-world feedback?

Why General LLMs Will Control Physical Robots | Thijs Simonian

MTS (@mtsituation) published "Why General LLMs Will Control Physical Robots | Thijs Simonian," a conversation with Thijs Simonian, a Stanford junior whose path runs from viral Discord bots to OpenAI's robotics team. The episode is less a product announcement than a field note from a young builder who has watched general models cross from screens into physical action.

Simonian's background matters because it explains his confidence. In high school he built a Discord bot that grew to roughly 1.6 million servers before modern coding assistants made that kind of project easier to start. At Ella Marina he worked on video model evaluation, which led to conversations with OpenAI's Sora circle. After two rejections through the normal intern pipeline, he entered through relationships and a portfolio of specific ideas, then shifted to robotics when Sora was folded into other work.

The center of the conversation is the robot painting demo. Using Astra access before public release, Simonian and collaborators connected a general model to a robot arm and camera so it could plan strokes, look at the result, and improve across attempts. The point he keeps returning to is not that the robot was polished. It was slow and imperfect, much like early computer use, but it completed physical tasks often enough to show a trainable path.

His larger claim is that robotics may follow the same arc as computer use. Once a model can perceive a scene, choose actions, notice mistakes, and retry, the interface stops being a specialized robotics stack and starts looking like a general agent loop. That would lower the barrier for builders who can buy inexpensive hardware and experiment, while raising hard questions about speed, reliability, safety, and cost per attempt.

The interview also doubles as advice for students. Simonian argues that public proof of work now beats a polished resume: build things, show the process, develop design judgment, and keep a short project focus each term. He treats staying at Stanford as a reversible choice rather than a badge, valuable because it leaves time and community for independent exploration while the field is moving quickly.

Open questions

  1. How much of robot control can remain in a general model before latency, safety, and contact physics demand specialized control layers underneath it?
  2. When cheap arms and public models make demos easy, what evidence will separate a charming viral build from a dependable physical worker?
  3. If portfolios and public demos keep beating formal pipelines, how should universities and labs change recruiting so talented builders are not filtered out early?

How to Build a Sales Machine | Max Freeman, Ramp | 20VC with Harry Stebbings

20VC's 20Sales show published "How to Build a Sales Machine | Max Freeman, Ramp | 20VC with Harry Stebbings," a conversation between host Harry Stebbings and Max Freeman, Ramp's SVP of Sales. Freeman was Ramp's first sales hire, joining as an SDR after quitting a mid-market account executive role, and by 2025 he was leading more than 300 sellers. The episode is a granular walkthrough of how Ramp built its revenue engine from scratch.

The core of Freeman's hiring philosophy is treating go-to-market recruiting like moneyball, hunting for mispriced assets whose resumes understate their ability. His best-known version of this is hiring investment bankers into sales: they have no concept of nine to five, they bring real business acumen and horsepower, and a cold call to a CFO that opens with "I just quit Goldman to join this credit card business" carried weight before Ramp had any brand. The bet is explicit: pay double the market rate for the talent, demand four to six times the standard output. His SDRs were expected to book 40 to 60 qualified meetings a month, and commercial account executives take 10 to 12 calls a day, with some on 15 or more.

In interviews, he decomposes every candidate's number: how much was inbound, outbound, expansion, founder-sourced, inherited. He asks candidates to reconstruct two deals chronologically, looking for texture about how they advanced the opportunity and articulated business value, and to explain a deal they lost, checking whether they can name the real cause rather than blaming a connected decision maker. The final screen is a business case where the candidate runs a discovery call as a Ramp seller, and the hiring test he states plainly is whether he would buy a product from this person.

Freeman's bigger argument is that outbound is no longer a writing and calling problem but an engineering problem. In late 2020 and early 2021, before the current AI wave, Ramp built internal automation he calls OATS, the outbound automation team, to turn manual signal detection (new hires, shared universities, overlapping cap-table investors) into automated list building and outreach. He says sellers should be talking to customers, not scraping lists. His growth engineering team runs half a dozen engineers building tools for go-to-market, and the metric he watches is meeting response rate, not raw response rate, with just under 1 percent converting to meetings being a strong bar even as response rates regress.

On compressing sales cycles, the lever is upstream qualification with more rigor: confirm the contact can actually drive change and is committed to deciding within about a year, then test that by asking them to bring their boss to the next call. He refuses to engage on discounting early in a deal, insisting on co-authoring a business value assessment instead, and notes that most lost deals are lost to the status quo, which he treats as a failure to manufacture urgency rather than a product gap.

Compensation design starts with no comp plan at all: he advises 100 percent of target earnings guaranteed for the first two quarters, because founders do not yet know what is attainable and a mis-set quota destroys confidence that is hard to regain. He advises never hiring one seller at a time, because there is no benchmark, and describes Ramp's ratios running seven to twelve times salary-to-quota by segment, which he sees as healthy for a performance sales team.

Onboarding is a 60 to 90 day boot camp covering the product, buyer personas, competitive landscape, and internal systems, gated by disco and demo certifications. Sellers who do not pass do not get turned on in the router. He expects first calls within two to four weeks, frontline leaders riding shotgun, and says about 70 percent of new hires impress while the rest get direct intervention. His signal for future greatness is being pleasantly annoying: the new hires who ask questions around the clock correlate strongly with becoming great.

Forecasting accuracy is part of the performance culture, reviewed in one-on-ones and QBRs, with Ramp's finance counterpart building models that land within a 5 percent delta. He killed forecast calls entirely as performative work theater, replacing them with weekly pipeline reviews focused on deals at risk and deals already lost. Expansion is treated as an economic engine for net revenue retention, with customer success built as a technical team of CPAs and ERP specialists rather than a professional services line.

On leadership, his stated weakness is the instinct to jump into every important deal himself. He frames the leader's job as building more "barrels" inside the organization and staying a dual threat: able to operate in the clouds with executives while still in the trenches, making cold calls and sending cold emails every day.

Open questions

  1. Banker hiring worked when Ramp had no brand; now that Ramp has enormous brand heat, does the banker premium still pay, or does it become an expensive habit?
  2. If AI keeps pushing outbound response rates down, does the engineering-driven outbound machine keep its edge, or does everyone converge on the same automation and erase the advantage?
  3. How much of the 7 to 12 times quota ratios is a replicable system versus the product of a generational market window and a once-in-a-decade talent pool?

Can AI Really Break Cryptography? Experts Weigh In

a16z crypto convened cryptographers and investors to answer a panic that had been building on social media. The episode opens with Liz Harkavy, an investment partner at the firm, describing a warning issued the day before by Justin Drake, a security researcher at the Ethereum Foundation, who told the industry to prepare for "bunker mode," the possibility that AI could break elliptic curve cryptography faster than a quantum computer could. Vitalik Buterin followed with a toned down but still unsettling tweet speculating that AI might find better attacks on crypto systems. Sonal Chokshi, editor in chief, joins as a guest, along with Dan Boneh, a Stanford professor who heads the applied cryptography group and co directs the computer security lab, and Eddy Lazzarin and Justin Thaler from the a16z crypto team.

The spark for the panic was a massive OpenAI release: roughly 700 papers claiming enormous progress in mathematics and computer science. Boneh says he was genuinely impressed by the results and that his inbox blew up with requests for his opinion. But as he worked through the list of problems solved, one thing stood out. There was not a single question about cryptography, not even a theoretical one. He stresses that this tells us nothing by itself. Perhaps the models are simply bad at cryptography, which would itself be interesting. Perhaps the labs tried, failed, and said nothing. Or perhaps they tried, found something, and had reasons not to share. His opening message is blunt: everybody calm down. The world today is the same as it was a year or two ago, because no new information about cryptography was actually released.

The panel spends the core of the conversation disentangling two different threats that got mixed together online. The long running quantum conversation, which centers on machines running Shor's algorithm against elliptic curves and RSA, is a known quantity with known mitigations. The new AI threat is different and broader. AI does not just threaten elliptic curves. It could in principle also weaken the post quantum systems, lattices, code based schemes, and the rest, that the industry is migrating toward. That is exactly why the panel wants to know whether the latest models have been thrown at those post quantum candidates and failed, a result they would love to hear.

Boneh makes a point about how cryptography actually works that shapes the whole discussion. All of public key cryptography rests on complexity assumptions: elliptic curve discrete log, shortest vector in lattices, and so on. Nobody can prove these problems are hard, because that would require resolving P versus NP. So confidence comes from a slower process. Experts try to break assumptions, fail publicly, and confidence accumulates over a decade or more. AI changes this tempo. A lab could spend tens of millions of compute dollars telling its most capable model to go insane on a new scheme for a month, and if it fails, that failure would arguably be more confidence building than a decade of the old process. In this sense the group sees AI as a tool that could give us more confidence in our security than we have ever had, if the labs share what they tried.

Thaler adds a nuance worth keeping. The working belief is not that an efficient algorithm exists and humans are too dumb to find it. It is that no such algorithm exists at all, in which case no amount of AI cleverness can find a nonexistent algorithm. The panic, then, is really about the timeline collapsing if such an algorithm does exist.

For everyday users, the panel's advice is refreshingly plain. Nothing has changed, and nothing needs to change. Do not move funds, do not panic, keep using the systems as they are. For institutions, the panel says the "bunker mode" recommendations are mostly good hygiene they should already follow, like not exposing public keys on chain where that matters. Coinbase already said publicly that it does most of this. One panelist notes that a broken elliptic curve scheme would be a cataclysm for all digital infrastructure, home banking, healthcare, government communications, not just blockchains, which makes the narrow focus on wallet hygiene in the original tweet seem discordant. If worst comes to worst, the plan is known: move to post quantum systems that are already being standardized and in some cases already deployed, like the hybrid elliptic curve plus lattice key exchange in TLS and migrations like iMessage and Signal.

A substantial middle section compares hash based and lattice based signatures, a live debate in the blockchain world. Hash based signatures are seen as more agile: if one hash function weakens, you swap in another and the framework still holds, and no new assumptions beyond hashing get added to the chain. But they are larger, harder to use, and lack the algebraic properties, like threshold signatures, that cryptographers value. The panel also warns that hash only paths relying on recursive proof aggregation carry their own poorly understood risks. Nobody claims a settled answer.

The episode closes on opportunity. Startups and software shops now have AI models that can scan their own cryptography and codebases for bugs before shipping, a tool that did not exist before. As intelligence per dollar keeps falling, the panel argues we may be approaching a world with asymptotically fewer software bugs, where AI helps defense more than offense. And if the labs ever do announce a break, the playbook is old and trusted: responsible disclosure to industry and government, rapid coordinated upgrades, and the crypto agility that deployments have been building toward for over a decade.

Open questions

  1. Have any frontier labs actually tried their best models against standardized cryptography or the post quantum candidates, and what would it take to get them to publish negative results?
  2. If AI can produce conviction about a scheme's security in a month that once took a decade, how should the industry redesign its process for standardizing and deploying new cryptography?
  3. Which post quantum path, hash based or lattice based, should blockchains bet on, and does the AI threat change that tradeoff?

Photonic Fabric, Custom Silicon and the ROI of the AI Buildout

Marvell Technology published "Photonic Fabric, Custom Silicon and the ROI of the AI Buildout," a conversation from Marvell's Investor Day in which Dave Lazovsky and Will Chu of Marvell join John Furrier on theCUBE. The piece centers on two Marvell announcements and the economic argument behind the AI infrastructure buildout.

The headline hardware is the Photonic Fabric Memory Module, which Marvell calls the first optically interconnected, high-capacity, high-bandwidth memory. The module shown carries two stacks of HBM and supports configurations of up to eight stacks of DDR5. Its point is to decouple memory capacity from compute for the first time. Today, the only sockets for HBM sit about a millimeter and a half away from the GPU, CPU, or XPU. Marvell integrates these modules into appliances that pool 32 to 33 terabytes of optically interconnected memory capacity, sitting up to 50 meters away from the compute. It is a memory farm: aggregated, disaggregated memory.

Lazovsky places the module in the full memory hierarchy. Inside the chip is custom SRAM, which Marvell says delivers 17 times the bandwidth at two thirds of the standby power of alternatives, because the KV cache needs information stored close and available immediately. Then come HBM, DRAM, board-level expansion like CXL, and finally the photonic fabric module, which pushes disaggregation outside the tray entirely into another box. All of it is needed, with flash and hard drives carrying the long tail of AI data.

Custom silicon is the second big story. Chu frames it as pure economics. Hyperscaler spending is north of a trillion dollars, and once a hyperscaler knows its workloads, it can design customized silicon to extract more performance per dollar: higher performance, lower power, better economics. The pattern is familiar. In the early days the hyperscalers bought off-the-shelf servers, then they built their own servers, Amazon's Nitro being the first major breakthrough, and then their own chips. AI supercharged that desire, because XPU complexity keeps rising fast and pulls along a whole set of XPU-attached semi-custom silicon.

Furrier and the Marvell team keep returning to the AI factory framing. These are not just data centers; they are industrial centers producing intelligence, and the economic metric is tokens per watt per second. Chu argues the real bottleneck in AI today is not compute or memory but connectivity: if AI is one big brain, everything has to be interconnected, and a brain whose parts cannot talk to each other is not a brain. Marvell calls this its connectivity-first strategy, with more than twenty years of investment in SerDes, optics, and switches.

The ROI argument rests on the shift from training to inference. Three years ago, about a third of data center infrastructure was focused on inference. Today it is north of 60 percent, and over the next two to three years it will approach 90 percent. Inference is the return on the ROI equation; training is the investment. The largest cloud providers, which were at sub-10 percent operating margins three years ago, are now approaching 40 percent, and they are reinvesting those profits into infrastructure: 1.2 trillion dollars of aggregate CapEx this calendar year, growing to north of 3 trillion by 2030. This, the Marvell team argues, is why fear about the buildout misses the point: the money is the inference, and the returns are already showing up in the hyperscalers' margins.

The second announcement is the world's first fully monolithic 100T switch, with 512 200-gig SerDes in a single monolithic device. Because it is monolithic, Lazovsky claims it delivers more than 20 percent lower latency and more than 20 percent lower power than any competitive alternative, serving both scale-out bandwidth and, equally importantly, scale-up latency.

The interview closes with Marvell's business performance. Data center group revenue is tracking to 12 billion dollars this year, 20 billion next year, and a midpoint of 80 billion in fiscal 2031, up sharply from the roughly 2 billion the business once did. During the conversation the closing bell rang with the stock up 15.71 at 286 dollars a share. The team's closing argument: this is the largest CapEx buildout in human history, Marvell is attached to the fastest-growing parts of it, and it has the customer credibility and execution record to grow faster than the market.

Jon Hernandez AI · Stephen Wolfram

The future with AI will be very different from what we're being sold - Stephen Wolfram

Jon Hernandez AI published "The future with AI will be very different from what we're being sold - Stephen Wolfram," a one hour and forty two minute conversation between host Jon Hernandez and Stephen Wolfram, the creator of Mathematica, Wolfram Language, and Wolfram Alpha. The episode ranges across Wolfram's five decades in computing, and its central message is a deliberate cooling of the hype around AI.

Wolfram frames AI as two very different enterprises. One branch uses computation to formalize and automate what we want done, and this is the branch he has worked on for forty years through Wolfram Language. The other branch imitates human minds, which is what neural networks and large language models do. He stresses that these are complements, not competitors. Neural nets supply a humanizing linguistic interface; precise computation supplies the solid layer underneath where correctness matters.

The history lesson is that neural nets were thought dead for five decades. Wolfram played with them in the early 1980s and they did nothing. The 2011 surprise was that deep nets, trained hard enough on GPUs, could recognize cats from dogs, and the harder tasks turned out to be easier than expected, because small nets get stuck while big ones have room to find their way. Nobody predicted this, including the people who built ChatGPT.

Wolfram offers a scientific hypothesis for why language models work at all. Trained on roughly a trillion words of human writing, they are next word predictors, but their fluent generalization reveals an unrecognized regularity in language, something like a semantic grammar of meaning sitting beneath syntax. In a sense, AI is not more complicated than we thought; we humans are simpler than we thought.

ChatGPT can do math, but not on its own. It calls out through tool interfaces to systems like Wolfram Language and Wolfram Alpha. That connection was the point of Wolfram Alpha all along. When it launched in 2009, leading AI researchers called question answering impossible; it worked because a precise computational language sat underneath the vague natural language on top. The emerging workflow is a loop: the user says something vague, the model drafts Wolfram Language code as a precise representation, a human checks it, and that checked brick becomes part of a tower of capability.

The episode's hard core is computational irreducibility, Wolfram's discovery from the 1980s. Most natural and mathematical systems have no shortcut: you must run every step to know the outcome. Neural nets do the opposite, they hunt for the pattern that gets the quick answer. That mismatch is the basis of his claim about the black box. There is a genuine choice. You can demand machines that are fully explainable, and then they can only do what explainable machines do. Or you can let them do everything a computational system can do, and accept that they will sometimes surprise you. He draws the same line for medicine, where 90 percent correct can be a disaster, and says the sane middle ground is AI for the interface plus precise computation for the hard layer.

Alignment, on this view, cannot be fully solved. You cannot define "the right thing" without a precise language for what you want, and even then irreducible surprises remain. His position is not despair but acceptance, the same bargain we struck with airplanes and turbulent flow, which we never fully understood either. The good news inside irreducibility is that there are infinite pockets of reducibility, places where we can say something reliable, and those are what we build technology on.

On whether AI can discover truly new knowledge, Wolfram is careful. Originality is cheap: it is trivial to find a program nobody has examined. The hard question is whether it connects to anything humans care about. Language models can recombine what humans have already made, which is useful for science, but they do not explore the computational universe on their own. He tells the story of a 120 step automated proof he generated in 2000 about the foundations of logic, which no human, and no LLM, has ever understood. It is a lump of irreducible computation, not understanding.

Math benchmarks impress him less than the press releases do. Competition math is a memorized bag of tricks, and benchmark chasing usually puts more work into the benchmark than the solution. He is similarly skeptical of scaling hype. Thresholds exist, small nets cannot do what big ones can, but much of the recent improvement is the harness around the models rather than the models themselves. For raw tasks like recognizing a cat, little has changed in years. He does expect a ChatGPT moment for humanoid robotics, and for new modalities like massive sensor arrays, but he sees current progress as continuous, not discontinuous.

Work is where the conversation gets practical. Every automation wave, from forklifts to databases, shifted what people do and expanded what was possible, with the real question always being what humans choose to do. Choice is by definition unautomatable. The premium on deep specialization is falling; the premium on breadth is rising, because there are infinite niches left to explore. He argues education, built in the industrial revolution for systematic factory work, now needs to teach thinking rather than mechanical skills, and tells the story of CEOs who prototype in Wolfram Language while their programmers spend a month on what could take half an hour.

He is calmly optimistic. The storybook version of technology says everything changed overnight; the real version took fifty years, as with the industrial revolution. The frenzy is also regional, concentrated in Silicon Valley, and much of the startup fundraising story is, in his dry telling, the old tale of the man paid to teach a horse to talk. In 2028, he says plainly, the world will not end.

Open questions

  1. If capability and explainability are truly a tradeoff, how should regulated fields decide how much unexplainable capability they will buy?
  2. As AI takes over the mechanical layers of work, will the value of human choice show up in wages, or only in who gets to do the choosing?
  3. If intelligence turns out to be simpler than we thought, what does that do to the idea that superintelligence is a coherent destination at all?
