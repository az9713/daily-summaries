Bg2 Pod · Jensen Huang · NVIDIA · AI revenue and the capital buildout

Jensen Huang | Does AI Revenue Justify Capex, '27 AI Bottlenecks, Singularity, Open vs Closed & more

On Bg2 Pod, the investing show hosted by Brad Gerstner and Bill Gurley, Gerstner and Altimeter partner Clark Tang sit down with Jensen Huang, the co-founder and chief executive of NVIDIA, for a conversation titled "Jensen Huang | Does AI Revenue Justify Capex, '27 AI Bottlenecks, Singularity, Open vs Closed & more." It is their third BG2 conversation with Huang, recorded days after an extraordinary week for him in Washington: a state dinner at the White House, the summit that produced a new accord on superintelligence, and a ceremony where the president presented him with the National Medal of Science in front of his parents, who had flown in on a day's notice. Gerstner notes that five of the six people honored on that stage were born outside the United States, and Huang turns the observation into the episode's first theme: America's superpower is that the world's best builders still want to come and build there, and keeping that flow of talent lawful, open, and alive is a strategic asset.

The hosts open by recapping Huang's earlier calls on the show: two years ago he said the compute buildout would reach at least two trillion dollars when most people expected one, he predicted inference demand would explode once models began to reason, and last year he said the age of agents was arriving. All three landed. Asked where things stand now, with lab leaders talking about entering the singularity, Huang explains why demand for compute keeps compounding. A chatbot mostly repeats what a model has memorized, but real work requires reasoning: laying out options, rolling them forward, reflecting, pruning, and trying again. On top of that, the models have gained what he calls a harness, the surrounding system that manages working memory, builds long term knowledge, and teaches the model to use the tools humans already rely on, from design software to simulators. Coding was the first easy on-ramp. The next wave, he argues, is design, creative work, and science, powered by agents that can turn a photograph into an editable CAD model and work inside a company's own information.

The conversation's emblematic example is a recent result on the Navier-Stokes equations, a famous open problem in mathematics. Huang is careful about the credit: the model did not start from nothing, it stood on decades of work by mathematicians and scientists, and then applied exhaustive exploration, with some ten thousand agents working the problem, to push past a point where human effort had stalled. The hosts put the compute cost at roughly four million dollars and note how fast that cost is falling. Huang's wider point is that many fields sit at the same kind of precipice, with the groundwork laid and only massive, cheap exploration missing. He expects the cost of intelligence to keep dropping, and demand to more than make up for it, because every answer opens new doors. Gerstner raises Keynes, who foresaw an eightfold richer economy and a fifteen hour week, and got the wealth right and the leisure wrong. Huang agrees: work is purpose, ambition expands to fill the opportunity, and he is working harder than ever by choice.

From there the talk turns to physical AI, intelligence that senses and acts in the real world: self-driving cars, warehouse robots, surgical systems, imaging machines, logistics networks. Huang says this business did about ten billion dollars in the first half of the year, up from five billion the year before, and that his earlier talk of a hundred billion dollar market was probably understated. Building it takes three computers, he explains: one to train the brain, one running a digital twin of the world, his Omniverse platform, where robots and agents can learn safely, and one inside the machine itself. NVIDIA builds all three, and he expects company after company, from FedEx and Walmart to the telecom carriers, to become technology companies in the process. He also concedes that AI's benefits are mostly invisible today: people already rely on it every time they search, shop, or get a package delivered, and would only notice its value if the data centers went dark for a weekend.

The middle of the episode is a long argument about safety, where Huang rejects the idea that perfect alignment is either possible or the right goal. Values differ across people and cultures, he says, so alignment will always be a work in progress. The practical answer is the one we use with children and new employees: start with zero trust, grant the minimum rights a job requires, and expand them only as confidence grows. Containment comes first, then monitoring that watches the system from outside its own view, with the ability to escalate or shut it down. NVIDIA's contribution is a framework he calls Open Shell, which he says more than a hundred companies have joined. His larger claim is that safety is not a brake on the technology but part of it, the way braking systems and airbags are part of a car: when the industry says accelerate, it should mean accelerating the safety and security technology fastest of all. He describes the new American framework as a three legged stool: Commerce Department review of every new frontier model, the White House accord the labs signed, with audits and board oversight, and a coordinating body for critical industry security. It is imperfect, he says, but it is real, it has teeth, and it is moving now, which beats arguing about perfection.

Winning, in his definition, is not NVIDIA or the labs winning. It is every American industry, state, and community winning, an economy many times larger, with more spent on health, education, and infrastructure. That frames the buildout discussion. Citing SemiAnalysis estimates, the hosts say something like forty three gigawatts of new AI compute may come online next year, more than all the AI compute that exists today, with a similar amount the year after. Huang says the demand is there and more. The obstacles are physical: land, power, the buildings themselves, skilled construction labor, and local moratoriums. Scarcity, he argues, actually helps NVIDIA, because when power is the constraint, buyers choose the hardware that produces the most intelligence per watt, and NVIDIA now sells far more into each gigawatt than it used to, on the order of forty billion dollars of systems, networking, processors, and software, up from under twenty. He is warm about Elon Musk's buildout, including the ambition of ten gigawatts by the end of 2027 and their joint work on servers designed for orbit, where he argues only the highest value computing earns its launch cost, and NVIDIA's is the most rentable and durable once it is up there.

The title question gets the episode's most sustained answer. The hosts do the skeptical math: the frontier labs and inference companies take in something like two hundred billion dollars this year, and to pay back trillions in capital spending that figure must double again and again. Huang calls the framing a misunderstanding of where the infrastructure actually goes, and splits demand into rough thirds. One third is ordinary computing that has nothing to do with chatbots: data processing, simulation, recommendation systems, fraud detection, growing at thirty to forty percent a year on its own. A second slice, a quarter to a third, is the labs, and there are more of them than the famous two: Meta's Muse effort, Grok, Gemini, and Anthropic all run on NVIDIA now. The final third is thousands of startups renting NVIDIA compute the way a previous generation rented AWS, most of them building on open models, which he says eighty percent of startups use, and specializing them into domain tools for fields like manufacturing, health, and biology. Add in the end of Moore's law, which means more computing now costs more money rather than arriving free with time, and he holds to his forecast of three to four trillion dollars a year of infrastructure buildout by the end of the decade, without needing any lab to reach a trillion in revenue. The same logic explains NVIDIA's valuation puzzle: analyst forecasts for the company have been revised up by one to two hundred percent in a year while the stock is up only about twenty percent, and Huang argues the moat is widening, because at fifty to a hundred billion dollars a site, buyers place sight unseen orders only with a supplier they trust to deliver, the way he trusts TSMC with all of his own business. General purpose hardware, he says, is what makes the investment safe: it stays useful across workloads, models, and customers, so it stays rentable, financeable, and cheap to own over its life.

He closes with NVIDIA's next act and his own outlook. Two new business models sit on top of the installed base: licensing NVIDIA's software stack to enterprises that need it operated for them, at a few thousand dollars per graphics processor per year across millions of units, and revenue sharing with regional cloud partners that rely on NVIDIA for its stack, its ecosystem, and help securing financing. The company is returning more than half its free cash flow to shareholders through buybacks, and its much watched investments in ecosystem companies will shrink as a share of cash generation. Asked whether he is more confident than a year ago, Huang says he is about as confident as a person can be that the outcome will be good, because too many smart people, most of them invisible to the commentators, are doing the work responsibly. Worry is healthy, he says. Predicting the end of the world underestimates everyone else.

Open questions

  1. Huang's payback case rests on demand being far broader than the famous labs, with baseline computing and thousands of startups absorbing much of the buildout. If the physical bottlenecks he names, land, power, and finished buildings, cap how much compute actually comes online in 2027, which of those demand segments gets rationed first, and does the revenue story still hold for the financiers funding the sites?
  2. His safety model, zero trust containment plus monitoring from outside the system, is an engineering answer that assumes operators keep tight boundaries as agents gain tools, funds, and responsibilities. As competitive pressure pushes companies to grant agents wider rights faster, what institution, if any, actually enforces the gradual expansion he describes, the accord, the Commerce review, or the market?
  3. NVIDIA's widening moat, in his telling, comes from trust at a scale where buyers cannot benchmark before committing a hundred billion dollars. If that trust becomes the main barrier to entry, does the industry's push toward open models and custom chips ever produce a real second source, or does the fungibility argument lock in a single supplier for the world's intelligence infrastructure?

No Priors · Greg Brockman · OpenAI

Revolutionizing Cybersecurity and AI-Driven Science with OpenAI President Greg Brockman

On No Priors, the podcast hosted by Sarah Guo and Elad Gil, the guest for "Revolutionizing Cybersecurity and AI-Driven Science with OpenAI President Greg Brockman" is Greg Brockman, co-founder and president of OpenAI. The conversation ranges across the company's latest math results, the push into AI-driven drug discovery, a proactive agent called Dots, and a long closing argument that the physics of cybersecurity have changed, that defenders have a window they need to use, and that security spending should one day vastly outstrip software spending.

The episode opens with math. OpenAI has rolled out GPT-6 to over a billion people, and the show's notes report that its AI agents have solved nearly 400 century-old math problems. Brockman treats this as a settled milestone: the question of whether AI can produce genuinely new knowledge, he says, is now impossible to deny. The systems are not yet inventing whole new theories, but they are resolving problems that stood for a hundred years. What excites him is less the answers than the leverage: what a human equipped with these tools can now attempt in any field where similar conditions hold.

Guo and Gil press on a problem that comes with that power. Advanced proofs can only be adjudicated by a handful of specialists, and a natural language proof is far harder to check than a formal one. Brockman says OpenAI trains its models with that in mind, shaping outputs so that people can follow the reasoning and learn from it, because keeping humans in the driver's seat is a core value for the company, not an afterthought. He came to the field from mathematics himself, inspired by figures like Galois and Gauss who worked on hundred-year horizons.

The core insight of the science discussion is about data. Mathematics and theoretical physics are data-rich, which is a large part of why progress there came fast. Biology is the opposite: the data is scarce and hard to generate. Drug discovery is still the prize Brockman ranks first, the application most likely to lift everyone's quality of life, but it is a long pipeline, roughly 15 years and about 1.5 billion dollars per drug, with most of the cost sitting in clinical trials rather than in finding the molecule. His first-principles argument is that humans are simply badly suited to biology, while a neural network has no particular reason to be better at natural language than at the language of biology, so the breakthroughs should transfer once the data problem is worked on. Some steps will fall quickly, like the year-long regulatory filings that models can now draft, and sometimes the cure already exists in an obscure paper and the real job is finding it. Other steps need new wet labs and better simulation.

That leads to regulation. If a model ever becomes more predictive of a drug's efficacy or toxicity than a clinical trial, Brockman argues, the rules should change around it, the same way road systems would be rethought if self-driving cars proved ten or a hundred times safer than human drivers. Trials are already migrating to jurisdictions whose regulatory environment fits the new reality better, and he expects most of what is possible to arrive quickly, with long-term outcome validation as the slow part.

The middle of the conversation turns to Dots, OpenAI's proactive, always-on agent. Brockman's own Dots tracks work he has promised to do and messages he still owes, and hands him a morning list, often flagging things he never asked it to watch, like a calendar conflict he needs to leave early for. He expects work assistants and consumer assistants to converge into one thing, with the real distinction being economics: a consumer product must scale to free or nearly free, and bringing memory to every free ChatGPT user was an enormous engineering effort, while a workplace can justify peak intelligence at a much higher cost per person. OpenAI's position, with what he puts at 1.2 billion ChatGPT consumers alongside a leading coding and enterprise business, is an argument for unifying rather than splitting. A visible side effect is a renaissance of founders: people quitting jobs to start companies they say they never would have attempted without an AI they can rely on.

On interface, his line is that the best UI is no UI: you talk to the agent by voice or reach it inside Slack or iMessage, which Dots already does. Yet the same release wave added generative UI, where the model builds the interface on the fly, a rich view when you are comparing flights, or a one-off app to help you assemble a bicycle that nobody would ever hire a developer to write. The enterprise difference, in his telling, is governance: admins, security teams, and controls over when new features appear.

The longest stretch is cybersecurity, and it carries the episode's title claim. Brockman describes a defender's window: frontier AI can help defenders secure themselves right now, before those capabilities diffuse widely, while the capability available to everyone ratchets upward with each model release from providers around the world. The leading edge is already visible, in cases like model-driven hacks of banks in South Korea. The vulnerabilities themselves are mostly old. Security was bolted onto an internet built for resilience rather than safety, the world of Heartbleed and of critical infrastructure resting on one volunteer maintainer's open source project. OpenAI's response is its Daybreak program, which gives defenders access to its most capable models to find and fix flaws, backed by a one billion dollar commitment for frontline defenders, plus work with the Defense Department and the security community on studying vulnerabilities and coordinating incident response.

His practical model is saturation. Each time a new model lands, apply it to your own systems: OpenAI at one point paused the projects of about a quarter of its production engineers and turned them to securing, had its peak model probe the whole infrastructure, fixed what it found, and kept going until that model could find nothing more. The same tools make formal verification of software realistic for the first time and let models be trained to write secure code from the start. He expects a transitional period with more attacks before defense pulls ahead, and he is blunt about the economics: as software creation becomes cheap, what you actually want is software that is reliable and secure, so security spending should vastly outstrip software spending. Almost nobody audits their open source supply chain today; third-party scanners are emerging, OpenAI is partnering with firms like CrowdStrike, and he frames the whole effort as a coordinated response that OpenAI, holding the frontier models, has a duty to lead.

The conversation closes where it began, with human agency. Even as models surpass people in narrow areas, Brockman insists humans stay firmly in the driver's seat, more like chief executives or board members directing teams of experts, judging outcomes by whether life actually got better. His image is the handle Steve Jobs put on the iMac, so its owner could always pick the machine up and throw it out the window.

Open questions

  1. How long does the defender's window stay open? Brockman defines it as the gap between frontier capability, which defenders can access through programs like Daybreak, and diffused capability, which ratchets up with every model release worldwide. The episode never says whether that gap should be measured in years or in release cycles, or what happens to the organizations that miss it.
  2. Who pays for security spending that vastly outstrips software spending? The parties he names as most exposed, volunteer open source maintainers and under-resourced critical infrastructure operators, are exactly the ones with the least money today. Whether a one billion dollar commitment and third-party tooling actually reach that long tail, or whether the shift mainly protects large companies that can fund it, is left unaddressed.
  3. How do you prove drug discovery's physics changed without running the old process? Brockman argues regulation should bend once models predict efficacy and toxicity better than trials do, but demonstrating that superiority seems to require the long trials and long-term outcome data he hopes to shorten, at least once. The episode does not resolve that bootstrap problem.

Schwartz Reisman Institute · Jacob Tsimerman · Fields Medal · Mathematics and AI safety

After the Fields Medal: Mathematics, AI and What Comes Next | Jacob Tsimerman & David Duvenaud

The Schwartz Reisman Institute for Technology and Society hosted this public talk and conversation on September 8, 2026. Jacob Tsimerman, a professor of mathematics at the University of Toronto, had received the 2026 Fields Medal only weeks earlier, honoured in part for his role in proving the André-Oort conjecture. He is the first scholar based at a Canadian institution to win the medal. After opening remarks and a video message from U of T president Melanie Woodin, Tsimerman gave a personal talk about how his life in mathematics has worked, and then sat down with David Duvenaud, the institute's Schwartz Reisman Chair in Technology and Society, for a conversation and audience questions that kept returning to one theme: Tsimerman is now turning from pure mathematics to AI safety, and he used the evening to launch a new institute devoted to it.

Theory building and problem solving

Tsimerman framed his whole career as an interplay between two activities. Problems are the goals he wants to reach, and theories are the collections of tools he builds or borrows to reach them. He traced the pattern through his own life. As a small child he learned from riddles his grandfather set him, including a bathtub that two taps fill at different rates, a problem that becomes simple the moment you invent the concept of speed. In high school, down the street at University of Toronto Schools, he sat for hours in contest rooms working on elegant problems, an experience he credits with teaching him to stay in the room and keep working without knowing the answer. At university, Galois theory showed him the mature version of the cycle: a theory crystallizes around a problem, the problem inspires deeper theory, and generations repeat the loop.

His research career followed the same shape. The André-Oort conjecture fell because the tools already existed: twentieth century work on tame functions had produced the theory of o-minimal structures, and he and his collaborators assembled those tools and applied them. The Griffiths conjecture was different, because the existing tools were not enough and he had to build a small piece of new theory himself. He also spoke candidly about the economics of a research life: rewards in mathematics are sparse, years can pass with nothing working, and a piece of advice he received on arriving at Princeton for his doctorate, to keep working even if you do not know what you are working towards, has stayed with him. In his mid thirties he made a deliberate, time-boxed decision to aim at the Fields Medal while he was still eligible, knowing he might fail, and he describes the first year of that push as miserable before things began to work.

A mathematics institute for AI safety

The next stage he announced is the Mathematical AI Safety Institute, MAISI, launched that day with Andrew Critch as executive director. Its premise is that AI safety is widely treated as an engineering problem, when many of its hardest questions are theoretical. How do we trust agents more powerful than we are? What is learning, as a theory, in the way we already have theories of information and complexity? What happens when agents model each other and themselves? Tsimerman argued that such problems are left out of the safety conversation because they are too hard, not because they are unimportant, and that mathematics has simply not yet built the tools. The institute plans to gather around a hundred mathematicians in one building in the Bay Area, import the collaborative culture of places like the Institute for Advanced Study and Toronto's own Fields Institute, and publish through living documents and online lectures rather than slow journal routes. It is philanthropic and fully independent: its first funding is a ten million dollar grant approved by Coefficient Giving, it is not governed by OpenAI or any lab, and it has no plans to run giant training runs, though it will use frontier models to help with theory.

Asked what actually worries him, Tsimerman pointed to the paper he wrote with Critch cataloguing omnicidal futures, scenarios in which at least half of humanity dies. The list includes AI agents pursuing their own goals, bad actors misusing systems, governments racing, and super-persuasion: he described a recent UK AI Safety Institute test in which a model tried to talk a human into accepting a malicious update. His broader point was that AI systems are not so much coded as grown, and we understand them no better than we understand frogs, so prediction and control need theoretical foundations, not just experiments. Among the more hopeful directions he named debate protocols, where a weaker party can check a stronger system's answers without trusting it, and ideas close to zero-knowledge proofs. He also endorsed calls to pace the frontier, spelling that out as a regulator with the power to assess models for safety before deployment, and he raised formal verification, mathematically proving that hardware and software can only behave in permitted ways, as an ambitious direction some agencies are now pursuing. Beyond loss of control, he flagged gradual disempowerment, a society where humans can no longer produce economic value, as a question for sociologists, humanists and policymakers, and urged every field to build its own institution for the AI era.

What happens to mathematics itself

The conversation kept circling back to his own discipline. Tsimerman said AI is about to become robustly superhuman at research mathematics as it is currently practised, pointing to the recently announced AI result on the Navier-Stokes problem, where the system produced a counterexample humans had sought for decades. He predicted the age of humans solving famous open conjectures without AI help will likely end by the end of 2026, imagined an ever-expanding library of machine-produced theorems tended by a tireless math computer, and guessed other Millennium problems could fall before the end of the decade, while cautioning that Navier-Stokes had a known strategy and problems like Riemann still need a new idea. Asked what the Fields Medal will even mean in four years, he said its significance will have to shift dramatically. For students, his advice was to hedge: use AI tools seriously, since refusing to is a guaranteed way to fail, but keep investing in yourself rather than going numb, because nobody yet knows how the pieces land.

Open questions

  1. Can mathematicians actually build the missing theories of learning, trust and agent interaction fast enough for them to matter before much more powerful systems arrive?
  2. If AI becomes robustly superhuman at research mathematics, what should the field reorganize itself around: verifying machine results, choosing questions, teaching, or something else entirely?
  3. Will governments create a body with real power to assess frontier models before deployment, and would the labs accept its verdicts when a release is at stake?

Arena AI · Dawid · Meta Muse Wonder · Mistral Le Chonk · Chinese labs

Meta Muse Wonder, Mistral Le Chonk, and What Chinese Labs Do Next

Arena AI's rapid-fire street interview, "Meta Muse Wonder, Mistral Le Chonk, and What Chinese Labs Do Next," puts a series of quick questions to Dawid, an Arena AI voice viewers will know from the channel's model coverage. In just over three minutes he gives blunt verdicts on Meta's new model, Mistral's return, and the two trends he thinks matter most next.

The first question is whether Meta's Muse Wonder really is on par with Opus 5.5, as Meta says. Dawid does not buy it, and he says the online consensus agrees with him. His sharpest point is about price: users are paying roughly what they would pay for Opus 5.5, but the quality, in his view, is not there.

Asked what Meta is trying to do, he says the play is a consumer one. Meta is focused on a strong consumer product and on long-horizon agents that do things for you, and it will keep shaping its models around those agentic uses. His advice on who should use Muse Wonder is narrow: people who will actually use the Muse app. He sees no reason to switch away from Claude or OpenAI models for it, though he finds Meta's optimization for agentic use worth watching.

The conversation then turns to Mistral's Large 4, nicknamed Le Chonk, the company's first general-purpose model in ten months. Dawid calls it genuinely good news for Europe that Mistral is still building models and not retreating to enterprise work alone. But he is clear that Mistral is behind the curve, roughly two to three months behind the Chinese models, and he notes that a much smaller Chinese model can match the large model's performance. The headline size, one trillion parameters, does not impress him either, since it is not much bigger than other open models already available.

On what to watch next, he names two trends. First, Chinese labs have not released a flagship model in two months, which he calls ages in the world of AI, and he is watching how they respond to the recent Opus 5.5 and Sol 6.1 releases. His bet is on a DeepSeek release within the next two weeks, especially if the rumors about a model called Fable prove true, and he says any Chinese lab in that position would be tempted to push the button now.

Second, he points to cache read prices as the quiet battleground. Opus 5.5 and the new Sol model have already seen massive price drops there, and because so much of total token usage is cache reads, he calls it the main lever labs can pull to keep cutting prices for consumers. That, he says, is a very good development for consumers, and one that makes sense for the labs too.

Open questions

  1. If Muse Wonder's quality trails its price, will Meta cut the price, improve the model fast, or lean harder on the Muse app experience to justify it?
  2. When the next Chinese flagship lands, will it reset the open-model race the way Dawid expects, and can Mistral close a two to three month gap while models keep shipping this fast?
  3. How far can cache read prices fall before the economics of serving long agent sessions stop working for the labs?

No.1 Neuroscientist Reveals How To Unlock Superhuman Focus & Perfect Memory! - Dr. Sahar Yousef

On The Diary Of A CEO, Steven Bartlett's guest for "No.1 Neuroscientist Reveals How To Unlock Superhuman Focus & Perfect Memory! - Dr. Sahar Yousef" is Dr. Sahar Yousef, a cognitive neuroscientist at UC Berkeley and the founder of the Becoming Superhuman Lab. Across two hours and sixteen minutes she lays out a single argument: the brain runs on biological laws as fixed as gravity, modern technology has been engineered to break those laws for profit, and the same neuroscience can be run in reverse to win back attention, memory, and time.

Her credentials are the frame for everything that follows. She has spent her entire academic career at UC Berkeley, her field is cognitive neuroplasticity, the study of how the brain changes and adapts, and her lab's stated goal is to help people get the most done in the least amount of time with the least amount of stress. Much of her evidence comes from shadowing executives. She describes following a Fortune-level technology boss who worked in a glass office with a second monitor open on email, two phones face up beside him, and an Apple Watch on his wrist, and who managed roughly thirty seconds of focus at a stretch. A study she cites puts the average knowledge worker's time on one screen at forty-seven seconds before something pulls them away.

The foundation, she insists, is sleep, a minimum of seven hours a night. Founders arrive averaging five hours and asking how to get one percent better, which she compares to bringing a mechanic a car that is on fire and asking him to soup up the engine. Until the fire is out, she says, there is no baseline performance to improve.

Attention and memory, in her account, cannot be separated: you cannot remember anything you did not attend to, so every distraction is also a memory loss. The problem is not weakness. The default settings of phones, email, and messaging software were built by behavioral neuroscientists to maximize engagement, because engagement is what renews the contracts, not the user's productivity. Her fixes start with refusing the defaults. She advocates VIP notifications, modeled on the mail: a package that truly matters gets a knock and a signature, everything else waits in the mailbox. Bartlett hands over his own phone to a lock box on camera to make the point.

Her most developed argument is that technology copied the processed food playbook almost exactly. Food companies hijacked a fundamental need by raising stimulation, adding sugar everywhere, cutting friction, making products instant and effortless, and cutting satiation, removing the protein and fiber that make you full. Platforms, she says, did the same three things to the needs for novelty and connection: color-saturated feeds and short-form video raise stimulation, variable rewards work like slot machine jackpots, timed to land just before you would leave, the infinite scroll removes friction by deleting the end, and social media cuts satiation by offering the illusion of connection while the data shows heavier users getting lonelier. Short-form content is candy; it cannot fill the need it pretends to meet.

The reversal follows the same three levers. Reduce stimulation: in her lab's study, healthy professionals who switched their phones to grayscale for three weeks spent on average one hour less per day on their phones, seven hours a week returned. Prefer long form over short form. Meet the underlying needs in real life, with real people, instead of with their simulations. And feel no shame about any of it: hundreds of experts designed these tools to addict a brain that is itself wired for shortcuts.

Her working method is the focus sprint. A sprint is scheduled and visible on a shared calendar, which also quiets the guilt about not replying instantly. The goal of a sprint must be an output, something finished and measurable, never an input like working on a project, and it must be broken down beforehand into a granular execution list, so that no moment inside the sprint is spent wondering what comes next. Knowing exactly what is next, she says, is what lets the brain's anticipatory networks and dopamine work for you instead of against you.

Priorities come from a refrigerator metaphor. From the full task list, each day you pick one to three MITs, most important tasks, the things your future self would thank you for, and you defend real time for them. Everything else is an LIT, a least important task, the five-minute emails and forms that deliver quick, cheap dopamine. LITs are not banned; they fill the gaps between meetings. The hard question the method raises is how to know what your MITs are if you do not know your goals, and her answer is a deathbed exercise: picture yourself at the end of your life and work out what you needed to have done and been. She cites the hospice nurse Bronnie Ware's finding that the most common dying regret was living a life untrue to oneself.

Breaks, on her account, are where scrolling fails most quietly. In a study she describes, after focused work, people who looked at nature recovered best, people who simply did nothing came next, and people who scrolled short-form video recovered worst, because scrolling is still exercise for the muscle you are trying to rest. Doing nothing is not wasted time: a mind allowed to loosen is the one that produces insights, since no problem is solved by the same state of mind that created it. Rumination is the dark twin of the same mechanism. Rehearsing a grievance deepens the neural network that holds it, so her prescription is to acknowledge the feeling and then redirect attention, quickly and repeatedly.

She also wants people to know their chronotype. About a quarter of people are morning-shifted, at their best early and fading all day; a small minority, around sixteen percent in her lab's data, are evening-shifted, rising in energy late; most people are biphasic, with a morning peak, a deep afternoon dip, and a second wind. Focus sprints belong at your peak, and she is frank that the world is built for morning people and evening people mostly have to adapt around it.

The closing argument turns to AI. Education spent a century expanding what she calls the thinking class, and she sees it shrinking again as algorithms and AI take thinking away, largely because people let them. The brain prefers shortcuts, which makes outsourcing thought feel natural. Her counsel is to measure first, the way a blood test precedes treatment: assess your attention and digital dependence honestly, then apply the tactics. She points listeners to free assessments offered through her institute.

The episode ends with a demonstration. Bartlett writes a sentence and a string of numbers on separate lines, quickly; then he alternates between them, letter, number, letter, number, and slows down by roughly sixty percent. That gap is the switch cost, the time and energy paid every time attention moves, and by her estimate the average knowledge worker now loses more than two hours a day to bouncing between screens and tasks. The remedy is cognitive closure: finish a thing, close the box, and do not carry its baggage into the next. Her answer to the closing question, how to become luckier, applies the same principle in reverse. Get clear on what you want, keep it in mind daily, and a brain built to find evidence for its beliefs will start spotting the doors that were always there.

  1. How far can individual countermeasures go against an industry? Grayscale phones, lock boxes, and VIP notifications are personal defenses against products engineered by hundreds of specialists and funded by engagement. The episode never takes up whether design standards, regulation, or platform incentives have to change for the defenses to scale beyond motivated individuals.
  2. What does chronotype scheduling offer people who do not control their calendars? Yousef concedes the working world is organized around morning-shifted people and mostly advises adapting. For employees whose meetings, shifts, and school runs are set by others, the practical room to sprint at a personal peak may be small, and the episode does not test that limit.
  3. Where exactly is the line between using AI well and letting it think for you? She warns that the thinking class is shrinking because people hand over their reasoning, yet her own framework celebrates removing deliberation from good habits through automaticity. The episode asserts the distinction more than it defines it, and listeners are left to draw it for themselves.

The company building underwater drones for $50,000

On Cheeky Pint, John Collison's interview show, Collison sits down over pints with investor Chris Sacca and their guest Will O'Brien, the cofounder and president of Ulysses, for "The company building underwater drones for $50,000." Ulysses builds autonomous robots that do physical work in the ocean, and O'Brien's argument runs through the whole conversation: the ocean is the last unexplored frontier on Earth, almost all work in it is still done by expensive crewed ships, and whoever makes ocean work cheap and routine unlocks industries that do not exist yet. His blunt version of the ambition arrives in the cold open: we need to be building millions of underwater drones.

The episode gets to the robots by an odd route, because O'Brien is first a student of Guinness. He walks through the brewer's forgotten career as a technology company: an in-house research team hiring brilliant people as early as the 1890s and pointing them at problems, William Sealy Gosset inventing small-sample statistics to judge hop quality and publishing it under a pseudonym, the Student of the Student's t-test, so competitors would not learn that Guinness employed researchers, and Project ACORN, the decades-long effort, renamed from Project Dynamite for obvious reasons in 1980s Ireland, that produced the widget inside every can of draught Guinness. O'Brien wrote an essay on all this, and it explains his taste: he grew up in an entrepreneurial Irish family by the sea, reveres technically excellent companies nobody talks about, and describes himself as an eco-pragmatist who wants a growing economy and a flourishing biosphere at the same time.

Ulysses started with seagrass. One of O'Brien's cofounders, who had built aerial delivery drones at Manna, met a marine biologist on a surf trip and heard her describe a job made of manual tasks: photographing the seabed, gathering water samples, planting seeds by hand. The company set out to automate ecosystem restoration, and seagrass turned out to be the right teacher. It covers about 2 percent of the seafloor yet holds roughly a fifth of the ocean's stored carbon, around a fifth of commercially important fish species depend on it as habitat, and humanity restores it at something like one hundred to one thousand times slower than it destroys it. Ulysses now runs restoration projects in Virginia, southern Florida, California, and the Great Barrier Reef, and O'Brien says the single biggest obstacle is not technology but permitting, the green tape that has stalled what could be the world's largest seagrass project in Australia.

Building for seagrass forced the real invention, a two-part platform designed from first principles. A surface vehicle called Leviathan, with a range of about 2,000 nautical miles, does the long transit where drag is low, then launches Mako, the underwater vehicle that finds the work site, scans, and physically manipulates things, a deliberate break from older underwater vehicles that only gathered data. Mako is assembled from modular blocks: extra battery packs buy another 150 nautical miles of endurance, a different rear module buys speed, and stereo cameras or a sonar module swap in at the front. Because the architecture was general from the start, O'Brien says, the same platform could pivot when the US Navy and offshore energy companies came knocking, with no new core research and development required for each new line of work.

His strategic frame is the standard story of how frontiers develop, applied to the sea. First comes an expedition era, a few high-value trips that justify extreme costs, the fur trade in the American West or spy satellites in space. Then someone collapses the cost of access, railroads or reusable rockets, and settlement and entirely new industries follow. Ulysses wants to be that cost collapse for the ocean. The contrast he draws is stark: ocean work today means chartering a ship for a few hundred thousand dollars a day, plus a tethered vehicle worked in shifts by a crew that labor rules multiply into captains, mates, cooks, and medics. A Ulysses base vehicle, sold to navies, starts at about 50,000 dollars, against roughly 5 million for the autonomous underwater vehicle the US Navy most commonly buys, and even the Leviathan surface vehicle costs under a million. Energy companies mostly buy services rather than vehicles, but the pitch is the same: persistent robotic work instead of episodic expeditions.

The hard part, he is clear, is not demand but making the things. The first Ulysses vehicle was about 80 percent bought-in parts; the company is now on its fifth generation and pulling the stack in-house, writing its software from scratch and hiring an acoustics team to build its own sonar, because the maritime supply chain cannot support its ambitions. One supplier, coaxed into honesty, makes 100 to 200 of a key component a year. Against roughly 20 million aerial drones built annually, the world produces perhaps 1,000 to 2,000 underwater drones. So Ulysses builds on electric vehicle, drone, and consumer electronics supply chains wherever it can, prototypes its own inertial navigation at a fraction of its supplier's price, and plans to open a factory next year that could be among the largest underwater drone plants in the world by units. At the moment, O'Brien says, the company turns away contracts: the constraint is production, not sales.

The engineering reality underneath is hostile. Saltwater corrodes, pressure crushes (Ulysses works down to about 1,500 meters and has crush-tested to 2,200), but O'Brien names communication as the deepest problem. Acoustic links carry perhaps ten kilobytes a second across five kilometers, a dial-up modem with worse manners, so the vehicle must think for itself. Mako carries on the order of a hundred times the onboard compute of other autonomous underwater vehicles, including a full desktop-class graphics processor, and its autonomy, still rudimentary today, runs entirely locally: draw a polygon, generate a survey pattern, steer around a rock. Leviathan closes the loop, recovering the vehicle, charging it wirelessly, and linking it to shore over Starlink for live diagnostics.

The customers so far are the US Navy, which hired Ulysses for mine countermeasures before the Strait of Hormuz made that fashionable, and offshore wind, where the vehicles patrol floating wind farm cables daily, checking their condition and cutting away the drifting fishing lines that foul them and trap dolphins and whales. Sacca, whose firm Lower Carbon Capital was Ulysses' first investor, argues these slow, sober institutional buyers are a feature: procurement takes years, but once a company is in, it is sticky and hard to displace. O'Brien distinguishes Ulysses from the new defense primes on intent. Surface shipbuilders have no underwater vehicle, and much of the sector works on weapons, which he dismisses as the easy problem. His claim is that a general-purpose underwater robot is what turns a 3 trillion dollar ocean economy into a 10 trillion dollar one. The darkest thread is undersea cables: around 200 of them carry the world's traffic, they are mostly cut by accident today, but they can be tapped or severed with little effort, and O'Brien wants persistent robotic patrols over chokepoints like the waters west of Ireland, through which nearly all US to Europe cables pass. He confirms Ulysses has work in that domain and declines, smiling, to say more.

Open questions

  1. Can the supply chain actually scale to the vision? The model needs hundreds of vehicles next year and millions eventually, yet core maritime suppliers produce components in the low hundreds annually. Ulysses is answering with vertical integration and borrowed supply chains, but whether one new factory and in-house sonar and navigation programs can carry that ramp, on venture timelines, is unproven.
  2. Which business is Ulysses, a product company or a services company? Navies buy vehicles outright while energy customers buy operated services, and services scale with people and field operations rather than with units shipped. The episode never resolves how those two models coexist as volumes grow, or which one sets the company's economics.
  3. Who pays for a standing guard over the ocean's chokepoints? O'Brien makes a strong case that undersea cables and waterways should be monitored around the clock, and that only robots make that affordable. But the cables belong to consortia and the waterways to nations, and the episode does not settle whether governments will fund persistent patrols, or whether the vulnerability stays unpriced until a crisis prices it.

AI Agents Are Now Executing Phone Attacks | Kevin Tian

On MTS, the show hosted by Sophia Dew, this episode features Kevin Tian, co-founder and CEO of Doppel, an AI-powered cybersecurity company that defends organizations against impersonation, phishing, and deepfakes. Tian says the years since have borne that out, from major enterprise attacks down to fake athletes and disinformation campaigns in sports leagues.

What has changed, in his telling, is that the attacks are now agentic and spread across channels. Doppel's own defensive agents, used to simulate attacks for clients, can call people on the phone and hold conversations that run nine minutes on average, and they can fire off text messages as well. An attack no longer has to arrive as one suspicious email: it can come by phone, by text, and by video call, where the person on screen may or may not be a deepfake. He adds a newer twist. Defenders no longer protect only humans, because agents increasingly sit on the receiving end too, picking up phones and reading and answering email, so the question becomes how to protect agents and humans alike from being engineered.

Risk signal ingestion is Doppel's attempt to spot the targets before the attack. It combines real-world security data with what employees actually do: sharing files from Google Drive or Microsoft 365 with public links instead of a limited audience, weak password hygiene, not using multi-factor authentication. Those behaviors are signals that a person is more susceptible to a social engineering attack, and the point of collecting them is to identify who inside a company is most at risk.

The 49ers partnership shows what the full platform looks like in practice. Doppel hunts down impersonators, such as someone pretending to be a player on Instagram, and gets them taken down. It also simulates attacks, having an AI agent pose as a player and call a coach to try to engineer him, and it has recently moved into email security to stop attacks in real time. Tian, a longtime NFL fan, says the team is a natural fit: Bay Area based and historically forward-leaning on technology.

Sophia presses him on why this is not the platforms' job, since Instagram would seem to have every incentive to stop impersonation. Tian concedes the platforms do a great deal, but says the scale they operate at makes the problem genuinely hard. Doppel's problem is more tractable because it works directly with the organization being targeted, in a white-glove relationship, and knows that organization's legitimate accounts and assets. That ground truth is what gives it confidence about which personas are fake.

The hardest part of the whole problem, he says, is scale. AI has massively increased the volume of attacks, so anyone still doing takedowns by hand, building security awareness training by hand, or filtering email with hard-coded rules will fall behind. His blunt version: if you are not using AI to combat AI attacks, you will be too slow and get compromised. His advice to regular people runs along the same lines, awareness of the problem plus the right technology, because an attacker can now set an agent loose to make a thousand phone calls and send highly targeted phishing emails pretending to be your mother or your father. Asked whether attackers or defenders are winning, he calls it a pendulum. Recent high-profile attacks show the goal of zero incidents is not met, but defenders are building technology that could not have existed six to twelve months ago and hardening quickly, and the real danger is complacency, since both sides keep innovating.

a16z · Terry Angelos · Personal agents and the fight over how they pay

Wallet Wars

In a16z, the Andreessen Horowitz newsletter, Terry Angelos publishes "Wallet Wars," an essay about the fight to control how personal AI agents will pay for things on a consumer's behalf. Personal agents, he notes, are moving into text messages and familiar apps, and their promise is shifting from better answers to getting things done. An assistant that can organize your kids' soccer schedule can also order their cleats, and one that can plan a trip can book the hotel. An assistant that understands your life, he argues, becomes the starting point for much of your spending.

But getting things done requires something intelligence alone cannot provide: permission to pay. Without your card, an agent's recommendation is just another item on your to-do list. With payment credentials in hand, it can finish the task. That gap, Angelos says, has set off a scramble among wallets and payment companies, and underneath it sit two different battles. One is over who can help an agent pay at all. The other, more consequential one is over who lets the agent transact as you. The difference matters for loyalty programs and for the economics of payments.

He roots the moment in history. Early internet shoppers feared typing a card number into a website, then learned to trust the process. A card number looks to a user like both the identity of the paying account and the authority to spend from it, but the industry spent decades separating those two things through payment tokenization. Tokens are why a subscription survives a card expiring. Agents, in his framing, are the next step in that same separation, building on years of precedent rather than starting from zero.

The first battle is the war to connect. The fastest way to give an agent spending power is to plug in a wallet that already holds the consumer's credentials: Stripe's Link claims 300 million users, Shopify's Shop Pay 250 million, and PayPal more than 400 million. Agent developers ask how many of their users already have a payment method stored and how many merchants accept it. Stripe and Shopify have moved quickly, powering payments for Muse and Instinct, while PayPal brings an established network of consumers and businesses. Agents, he suggests, could give PayPal a second act, because the winning wallet needs more than the prettiest checkout.

The second battle is reach. No wallet's checkout button works everywhere, so wallets find other paths to merchants. Link, for example, can hand an agent a single-use virtual card, so a merchant needs no Link integration at all. But that purchase is not the same as paying with your own card: two transactions happen, one between you and Link and one between Link and the merchant. The cracks show in ordinary moments. If Walmart invites you to join Walmart+ mid-purchase and you accept, the experience breaks, because the card on the transaction does not match the card Walmart expects.

Angelos calls Stripe's approach the AOL of agentic commerce. AOL once packaged a messy internet into a curated garden, and inside Stripe's ecosystem transactions never need to exchange credentials at all: at a Stripe merchant, the card on file can simply be charged. Outside those walls, Link buys the item on your behalf with its own virtual card rather than your agent acting as you. The payment succeeds, but the relationship changes. Book a United flight with your Chase MileagePlus card through Link, and Link may charge that account while paying United with a Stripe-issued card, so the miles you intended to earn may never arrive. Link's own terms warn that underlying card offers, benefits and rewards do not always carry through. He names the distinction payment interoperability versus relationship interoperability: Stripe can reproduce the payment without necessarily preserving the relationship.

A third battle is routing. An agent connected to Link, Shop Pay and PayPal, with several cards spread across them, has to choose which one pays. You might want the airline card for airfare, another card for groceries, rewards on one purchase and purchase protection on another. "Buy it," he writes, is not a complete payment instruction. The agent needs a payment policy, not just a payment credential, and when interests conflict, an open question is whose economics it should optimize: the consumer's, the merchant's, or the agent platform's.

The fourth battle is liability. Authorize an agent to spend $500 on a hotel and it may stay within budget while booking the wrong dates, with the hotel delivering exactly what was ordered. Who refunds you? The rules are being rewritten as each participant defines its role. Card networks have frameworks for scoped credentials that limit spending by amount and merchant while preserving chargeback rights. Most wallets treat an agent's transactions as authorized even when the agent misread the instructions. Authorization, correct execution and purchase protection, he argues, are three different problems, handled by tokens, the agent, and the wallet and issuer respectively. For consumers it reduces to one question: when my agent gets it wrong, who makes it right?

His bet is on open standards. The card networks have each announced frameworks for agentic commerce, Visa's Intelligent Commerce, Mastercard's AgentPay and American Express's ACE, designed so an agent can transact as you. In the model he sketches, you store your card in a trusted wallet; before a transaction, the wallet mints a scoped agentic token that can be handed safely to the agent and then to the merchant, unusable by anyone else and carrying safeguards that match your instruction, like a spending cap. The merchant charges that token, which maps back to your original card and preserves your loyalty and cardholder benefits. The best outcome, he writes, is an agent that can use your credit card without having your credit card. AOL's training wheels got everyone online, and then the open web won; he expects the same sequence here.

Open questions

  1. When an agent pays with a wallet's virtual card instead of the consumer's own card, who absorbs the losses in rewards, benefits and merchant relationships, and will consumers notice enough to demand relationship interoperability rather than mere payment success?
  2. A payment policy means an agent must weigh the consumer's, the merchant's and the platform's economics on every purchase; who writes that policy, and can a consumer ever see or change the rules their agent follows?
  3. If scoped tokens and network standards become the norm, what is left for wallets like Link, Shop Pay and PayPal to compete on, and will the open-standards future arrive before the walled gardens lock in agent developers and merchants?

Omar Waseem · Dhravya Shah · Supermemory and the future of AI memory

How to go from intern to $50M founder in 2 years | Founder of Supermemory, Dhravya Shah

On Omar Waseem's podcast, in a second conversation between the two, the guest is Dhravya Shah, the 21-year-old founder of Supermemory, an AI memory startup. The episode, titled "How to go from intern to $50M founder in 2 years," traces an unusual two years: Shah built the first version of Supermemory as a side project, interned at Cloudflare under its chief technology officer, turned down money from famous names, raised a seed round that he says closed at a 50 million dollar cap, and then, two months before this recording, shut down half his company's products on purpose.

An internship that ran like a founder's job

Shah's route into Cloudflare ran through the project itself. He had been building a voice-enabled personal assistant, an early Jarvis experiment, on Cloudflare's developer platform, using tools like Vectorize and D1 before that platform was a big thing. Cloudflare noticed the work and brought him in. Inside, he says, the experience confounded his expectations of corporate life. The CTO sat in the interns' room and could be approached at any time. There were dinners with chief executive Matthew Prince. Shah was moved between teams at his own request, working on AI, databases, developer relations, and marketing, sometimes on Saturdays, by choice.

He credits Cloudflare's culture for much of what followed. Teams there have real autonomy, and writing is treated as part of the work rather than documentation after the fact: if there is no blog post, internal or external, the work is not done. Product ideas move from a written proposal to a first version to Friday demo days, and the company's go-to-market motion is essentially the launch itself, with new products built on a small set of primitives like Workers, Durable Objects, and object storage. His perspective shifted there, he says, from building a product to building infrastructure that other people rely on.

A gifted domain, and the money he refused

The project that became Supermemory started under a different name. An open-source user began calling it Supermemory, Shah liked it, and within an hour of him retweeting the idea, Scott Kyle, a maintainer of React Native, messaged to say he owned the supermemory.ai domain and would hand it over, asking only to invest when Shah raised a seed. Shah describes that moment, together with encouragement from Julian at Solo Founders, as the first spark that a side project could be a company.

What followed was a string of refusals that he recounts with some amusement. In 2024 the project became one of the fastest growing repositories on GitHub and venture firms began calling. He turned down a Founders Inc residency after his Cloudflare mentor Dane advised him to either bootstrap or raise from tier-one firms and avoid the middle path. He negotiated with a16z's speedrun program for two months and walked away, saying its terms were far beyond a standard YC SAFE despite being described that way. He also declined a 30,000 dollar YC Summer Fellows grant, reasoning that it was meant for college students doing research in their free time and should go to one of them.

The round that did happen started with people rather than firms: a first check from Julian, help running the process from Sudarshan, an early believer who had cold-messaged him on Twitter, and a check from Dane. Sequoia and Khosla Ventures passed, arguing that retrieval and vector databases had become commodities. Shah says those rejections were largely right, and they pushed his pitch away from retrieval toward self-learning memory. Through Sudarshan he met a partner at Susa Ventures who put in 2,000 dollars of his own money on the spot, and the round landed at Susa. By Shah's telling, founder Chad Byers asked what number would make him happy, accepted his answer, and the round closed at a 30 million dollar cap before Shah raised a little more and ended it at 50 million. The whole thing, he says, took six months of pain and about five days of actual frenzy.

The epiphany, and shutting down half the company

After the raise, Supermemory spent roughly a year behaving like a product company, shipping memory retrieval, file systems, connectors, extractors, and profiles, and competing with product rivals like Mem0. Two months before this conversation, Shah had what he calls a big realization. He compares the company to a restaurant on Market Street: busy, making money, and fundamentally interchangeable. The frontier labs, he argues, would never build on a memory product from a company content to be one of many, because they want to be world-changing themselves. So he rebranded, and shut down Company Brain, the consumer personal app, and most of the business angle, giving up what he describes as a lot of revenue. He says he visited his investors' houses to tell them in person, and that the team, told they were killing products they had spent heavily on, was not disappointed.

The company now works on what he sees as the next inflection point in AI: continual learning, personalization, and personal agents. His stated goal is not a revenue milestone but, in his word, magic, something he insists cannot be quantified. The company takes in business mainly from publishing its research and thinking in public and converting inbound interest. Even so, he says the company makes a few million dollars a year and is currently profitable.

One research vehicle surprises the host: Shah is building an AI companion, a girlfriend in his framing, purely as a test bed. A long-horizon agent with no tools and no work to do, he argues, is the purest way to test whether memory actually works, through tone, empathy, and how it changes over time. He contrasts that with Character AI's single-thread character chats, predicts companions will become as normal and as privately unmentioned as dating apps, and says his team is also studying the darker side, including the AI psychosis harms that surfaced in the GPT-4 era, because he does not want to build that future carelessly.

What good memory looks like, and what scares him

Shah's technical view is that the industry has the read side of memory wrong. Most systems retrieve: the user says something, the agent searches files, and answers from what it finds, which he compares to excusing yourself mid-conversation to look through ten books. Real memory, in his picture, is simply present. Supermemory injects relevant tokens into whatever harness the agent runs in, keeps a profile that exists without being asked for, and pairs the main model with a separate smaller model that does the remembering and the injecting, so the working model never has to decide to remember. Underneath sit a component that maintains the information and a data structure with a sense of time, something like git, plus connectedness, something like Obsidian. Memory done this way, he argues, becomes continual learning: a coding agent that notices a command failing can remember that and take a different path next time. The system itself is deliberately light, self-hostable, and, he says, runs in about 200 megabytes of RAM.

The shutdown of Company Brain left its own lessons, chief among them permissioning: an agent that knows everything about a company can repeat private channel talk in the wrong place, and nobody has solved when an agent should speak up proactively rather than wait to be asked. Company Brain is now open source, maintained but no longer a business.

His sharpest warning concerns agent-to-agent systems. He describes a friend's Google login code being sent to him, unprompted, through a trusted-network feature of a personal agent product, and argues that many viral leak screenshots are actually harnesses hallucinating explanations, since a harness cannot know how it itself works and will invent a reassuring story when a user gets angry. He recounts reverse-engineering one such product by metaprompting it into remembering planted facts, then exporting the data to inspect its internal workers. Agent-to-agent communication, he concludes, is nowhere near figured out, and he would not use it yet except with best friends.

The conversation closes on temperament. Shah admits his internet persona, goofy and build-obsessed since his Dubsmash days, is basically who he is; a recent meeting with Indian billionaire Nikhil Kamath produced the line that he seems very juvenile, and his answer was simply that he has lived alone for years and is being himself. His closing philosophy is velocity: stress, he notes, is the same whether the problem is worth 10 million or 10 billion, time passes either way, and iteration speed combined with intellectual honesty course-corrects faster than stopping to reflect.

Open questions

  1. If memory only becomes foundational when it shades into continual learning, what evidence over the next year would show that Supermemory's injection approach beats the retrieval approach everyone else ships?
  2. Can a company that deliberately shut down revenue products and refuses revenue milestones keep its investors' patience if the frontier bet takes longer than expected?
  3. If agent-to-agent systems are as easy to break into as Shah demonstrates, what has to change, technically or socially, before personal agents can safely talk to each other?
