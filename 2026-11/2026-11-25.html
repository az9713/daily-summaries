Nate's Substack · Nate B. Jones on the memory shortage behind rising phone and laptop prices

Why the AI Boom Is Raising Laptop and Phone Prices

In Nate's Substack, Nate B. Jones's "Why the AI Boom Is Raising Laptop and Phone Prices" argues that the AI buildout now reaches people who never use AI at all, through the price of the phones and laptops they buy. A year ago, memory accounted for about 20 percent of the parts cost of a low-end smartphone. Now it accounts for 54 percent, according to Counterpoint Research, more than half the component budget going to RAM and storage before the manufacturer pays for the screen, cameras, processor, or battery. Worldwide average smartphone retail prices have risen 20 percent this year, by the same report.

Jones frames the result as a financial vise. Phone makers and laptop makers compete with data centers for constrained supplies of memory, so physical inputs keep getting more expensive. At the same time, the companies building those data centers face a second pressure: the market price of the intelligence they sell keeps falling, as competition pushes down what customers will pay for a given level of AI capability. Understanding who can survive that squeeze matters, he writes, when you are buying a computer, choosing an AI tool, or deciding where to work.

The first section explains how agents pulled ordinary memory into the boom. When an agent fixes a bug, the model reasons about the problem, and then the agent opens files, runs code, reads the result, launches a test, and tries again, perhaps opening a browser to inspect the page it changed. Each step needs somewhere to execute, somewhere to hold the working state, and memory for the programs doing the work. Multiplied by agents running across thousands of companies, sometimes for hours, the hardware story becomes much bigger than the GPU answering a prompt.

AI has always needed ordinary servers alongside specialized chips, but agents make those servers more central to the workload. Counterpoint's latest memory analysis identifies agentic AI as a major source of demand for conventional memory, including DDR and LPDDR, the broad families also used in personal computers and phones. Servers and laptops do not need identical modules to compete, because their manufacturers draw on overlapping production capacity. When cloud companies secure more of that capacity, consumer-device makers have less room to negotiate. And supply cannot respond quickly: expanding memory production takes years, and power has its own delays. Berkeley Lab found that, in regions with available data, new generation projects reaching operation in 2025 had spent a median of more than five years getting from a grid-connection request to operation. As Jones puts it, you cannot software-patch a memory fab into existence.

The second section turns to the squeeze on the AI providers themselves. Epoch AI estimates that the cost of achieving a given level of AI performance has fallen roughly thirteenfold per year since 2023, across the benchmarks it studied. That measures the cheapest way to reach a particular capability level, and Jones is careful to note it does not mean a subscription bill falls thirteenfold. Against that decline, a standard 16-gigabit DDR5 chip's contract price rose about 5.5-fold from October 2025 to September 2026, according to TrendForce figures reproduced by TeamGroup. A provider selling what was recently impressive capability faces cheaper competition, because better models keep arriving and work that once required a frontier model becomes possible on something smaller and faster.

Efficiency can make the calculation work, since better chips and software can cut the cost of a task even while memory prices climb, and growing usage can compensate for lower prices. The danger comes when those improvements fail to keep pace with falling selling prices, new investment, and existing financial commitments. The demand itself is not in doubt. OpenRouter, which routes requests to AI models, reported weekly token usage growing from 0.5 trillion in January 2025 to 128.4 trillion in September 2026, roughly 250-fold in twenty months. Anthropic reports that its third-quarter revenue grew more than twelvefold from a year earlier, and Jones treats paying customers as a much stronger demand signal than excitement about a demo. Strong demand gives companies a reason to build, but it does not settle whether the financing works.

That financing is the third strand of the vise. A Federal Reserve governor describes a buildout increasingly funded by debt, alongside growing long-term leases and other commitments, so companies are making promises to pay that extend well beyond the next model release. Meanwhile the equipment ages: a Brookings conference paper discusses GPUs becoming economically obsolete within three to six years, even though the buildings and power infrastructure remain useful much longer. Jones compares it to underwriting a long-term loan whose repayment depends on equipment earning attractive returns for years, when you have to judge how quickly that equipment loses earning power and what replacing it will cost. If lenders become more selective, exposed builders may struggle to finance their next expansion or refinance old debt. Providers could respond with tighter usage limits, different subscription tiers, or a harder push for profitable customers. Those are possible responses to financial pressure, he stresses, not a forecast that every AI price will rise. The pressure can still reach your workplace through a tool your team depends on, a vendor that changes its terms, or an employer that suddenly wants every AI expense justified.

The advice section starts with hardware. If your current machine does the job, a discretionary upgrade can wait. If you need a replacement for work, buy for the workload and the years you expect to keep it, because saving on RAM that you cannot later upgrade can become an expensive compromise. Budget devices reveal memory-cost pressure first, since there is so little room to absorb it: manufacturers can raise prices, cut features, or stop selling a model. Jones advises watching what happens to the RAM and storage you get at a given price, and what it costs to move up a tier, because a familiar sticker price can hide a worse deal. Compare refurbished and previous-generation machines with the memory you actually need against new base models, checking battery condition, warranty, and remaining software support. The useful comparison is the cost of a machine that will serve you well for several years, and older inventory is not automatically a bargain.

For AI tools, his counsel is to use today's inexpensive access while keeping your options open. Test routine work on cheaper, faster models, and reserve the frontier model for tasks where its additional reasoning produces a result you can actually distinguish; the right split comes from your work, not a universal percentage. Keep the instructions, source files, and evaluation examples you would need to move an important workflow, and try a second provider before an outage or price change makes the decision for you. Cheap cloud access can be useful even while local hardware is expensive, but neither today's price nor today's usage allowance is a permanent promise.

When choosing a vendor or considering an employer, Jones suggests three questions. Can this business work at today's compute costs, or does its plan require another dramatic cost decline, continued discounts, or fresh financing before it can cover its bills? What happens if its compute bill rises 30 percent, used as a stress test rather than a prediction: can it improve efficiency, absorb the cost, or charge customers more without losing them? And what would customers still pay for if model access became interchangeable, which points to valuable work the company completes, integrations customers depend on, and results that justify the price?

Those questions lead to the closing skill he calls AI cost literacy: learning what a completed task actually costs once you include model calls, failed attempts, software, and the human time spent checking the output. His test is to take one recurring workflow, run a representative set of tasks through your current setup and a cheaper alternative, and compare acceptable results, total expense, and review time, recording where the cheaper approach fails and where paying for a stronger model saves enough rework to justify it. When someone then asks whether the AI budget is worth keeping, you can show which work gets done, what it costs, and how to keep doing it if a provider changes the deal, a much stronger position than an impressive demo with no idea what it costs to run.

Open questions

  1. If memory stays above half the parts cost of a budget phone, do manufacturers keep raising retail prices, quietly cut the RAM and storage offered at familiar prices, or abandon the low end first?
  2. As lenders weigh buildout debt against GPUs that lose earning power within three to six years, what evidence about provider revenue and usage would keep financing open for the next round of expansion?
  3. If providers respond to the squeeze with tighter usage limits or new subscription tiers, how soon will employers demand cost-per-task accounting for AI tools, and which teams will be ready with the numbers?

David Senra · Jacob Hirshman on Erebor, the bank he built with Palmer Luckey

A Deep Dive Into Palmer Luckey's Bank | Jacob Hirshman, Erebor

On David Senra, "A Deep Dive Into Palmer Luckey's Bank | Jacob Hirshman, Erebor" is a conversation with Jacob Hirshman, the co-founder of Erebor, a national bank he started with Palmer Luckey in May 2025. Hirshman, a lawyer who has spent his whole career in financial services regulation, says Erebor is the fastest growing bank in American history: the company was founded in May 2025, applied for a new bank charter in June 2025, and opened its doors in February 2026. Its growth, he says, comes from serving builders in the United States, and he repeats Luckey's line for the bank: a real bank for real Americans building real things.

The first problem the conversation takes up is why America needed another bank at all. There are almost no large founder-led banks in the country, Hirshman argues, because after the financial crisis regulators effectively stopped granting new charters: the average application took two to three years and often went nowhere, which made raising money to start a bank nearly impossible, so would-be founders bought and converted existing banks instead. Erebor's founding bet was that a new administration would reopen chartering, and that a founder-led bank with entirely new technology would be genuinely differentiated. Most banks buy their technology from third-party core providers and so never drive innovation in their own stack. Hirshman says the window is now visibly open, pointing to other de novo banks and to conditional charter approvals for companies such as Mercury and Revolut, and he credits the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency with moving swiftly to admit new banks and create competition.

The idea itself traces back to the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank. Anduril held an enormous amount of money at SVB, as did many founders building in defense, manufacturing, and AI, and Hirshman says a founder plans for every business risk except the risk that their bank fails. Erebor internalized that shock as a commitment to a highly conservative balance sheet: by policy the bank holds itself to a 50 percent loan-to-deposit ratio, well below the roughly 70 percent average bank, and today it lends considerably less than even that cap. Safety and soundness, he says, sit at the core of Luckey's vision, alongside technology a founder-led team can actually fix: an API-first experience, strong customer service, and none of the committee layers that slow manager-led institutions. Senra connects this to his conversation with Revolut's Nik Storonsky, who concluded that leaning into regulation rather than running from it was one of the best decisions he made, and Hirshman agrees completely: if you provide financial services, someone in the stack is regulated, and if it is not you, it is the vendor selling to you. Owning the charter and the balance sheet, like owning a supply chain in manufacturing, lets Erebor set its own services, move faster, and fund itself with deposits so it can lend at a lower cost of funds.

That vertical integration runs through the technology as well. Every line of Erebor's code dates from May 2025: the bank built its own bank core and its own underwriting software, and its underwriters use current AI tooling to make decisions faster. Hirshman stresses that the bank does not only lend from its own balance sheet; it also advises customers on sourcing capital elsewhere when that is cheaper, acting almost like an investment bank. His example is the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital, which holds 205 billion dollars of credit authority for loans to businesses investing in strategic supply chains and infrastructure in the United States. Erebor helped one company arrange such a loan to manufacture mechanical and electrical infrastructure for data centers domestically, in a supply chain where 40 percent of capacity sits offshore today. The mission framing is explicit: the bank exists to finance the next generation of American founders building capital-intensive businesses in advanced manufacturing, robotics, and AI.

Why can those founders not simply use JPMorgan? Hirshman gives three answers. The large global banks are regulated and staffed across many countries and hedge accordingly, while Erebor is regulated exclusively in the United States. Erebor moves faster: JPMorgan might offer a facility 25 to 50 basis points cheaper, but a quick commitment de-risks a founder's decision in a way price cannot. And consolidation has hollowed out specialized banking: the country has gone from more than 8,000 banks before the financial crisis to roughly 4,800 today, and the industry-specific community banks that once understood a borrower's equipment and cycles are disappearing. His model is the old farmer's bank, a specialized lender that understood what sat on a farmer's balance sheet and could stand by him through every cycle. Erebor, he says, is building the farmer's bank for tech, starting with the defense industrial base, where he claims it can move more quickly and nimbly than any other lender, and where borrowers building drones and AI infrastructure need credit against specialized assets that dilutive equity cannot replace.

Working with Luckey has taught him to think bigger. Luckey originally wanted to call the bank The Real Bank, and Hirshman describes him as a marketing genius with boundless energy and an inexhaustible, computer-like mind. When Senra calls Erebor a niche bank, Hirshman pushes back hard: technology is the core driver of the American economy, yesterday's software founders are becoming buyers and builders of factories, and his customers are not building niche products, they are building the future of America. The same instinct shapes hiring. About 40 percent of Erebor's employees are former founders, a deliberate barbell alongside a bank president with decades of conventional banking experience. Hirshman quotes a line Senra traces to Max Levchin in Jimmy Soni's book The Founders: the best hire is someone for whom this job is the last one before they start their own company.

Relationships are the other half of his lending philosophy, and he borrows it from Sam Zell. Dealmakers like Zell and Jay Pritzker, he says, ignored seven variables and honed in on the one that mattered, and Zell would voluntarily pay a lender 7.75 percent when 7.25 was on offer, spending perhaps two million dollars extra on a large borrowing purely to build a relationship he could call on for decades. Hirshman pairs that with the four C's of credit, character above all: a South American banker he knows walks a borrower's factory floor asking questions unrelated to the deal, because the founder who is passionate about the business is the one who pays you back, while a lender's upside is capped and its downside is not. The ambition is to be the relationship bank for the next Rockefeller, as Chase Manhattan grew on its Rockefeller relationship, and Hirshman says the next Rockefeller will come out of the AI world.

The conversation also explains how Erebor was financed, a graveyard problem for earlier would-be banks that died waiting for charters or unable to raise venture capital. Joe Lonsdale was among the first to put money in, and he sits on the board; the round came together quickly, and more capital followed as the charter process advanced and demand appeared before the bank even opened. Lonsdale also supplied a lesson about trust: visiting the team's office in Luckey's garage shortly after the charter application was filed, he proposed building a headquarters modeled on Gringotts, the goblin bank from Harry Potter, arguing that a physical place customers want to visit is how a young bank creates trust. Hirshman links the same insight to Revolut's airport advertising and to Jamie Dimon, whom he praises for running JPMorgan like a founder: since 2018 JPMorgan has opened about 1,000 new branches while rivals closed theirs, a bet Hirshman thinks will pay off as AI-driven fraud makes face-to-face verification of customers more valuable, not less.

Thinking bigger also means funding sources. Because a bank's cost of funding sets the rates it can offer, Erebor looked for billions of dollars of deposits that price structurally lower than domestic ones, and found them abroad: the dollar is an amazing product, and for people living with currencies losing 25 or 30 percent of their value in a quarter, access to a regulated United States bank deposit is the best form of holding dollars there is. Through partner fintechs and banks that build on Erebor's APIs, the bank gives people around the world access to US dollar deposits, and those global liabilities are cheaper funding that lets it lend to an American factory builder perhaps 100 basis points cheaper. It is complex work, done through local institutions under local rules, and Hirshman illustrates it with a chaotic February 2026 trip to Venezuela, where political change had just reopened the question of reconnecting the country to the US banking system: refused a seat on a charter flight because his visa had not yet cleared, he routed through Bogotá, crossed the border by car at Cúcuta, was photographed holding his passport against his chest by plainclothes officers, and was finally waved through with the words "Welcome to Venezuela, sir" in time for his meetings.

The name itself carries the thesis. Luckey's first choice, The Real Bank, gave way to Erebor, the Lonely Mountain of Tolkien's legend, where the dwarves mined gold, silver, and precious metals and built a prosperous society: strategic supply chains and commodities are core to the bank's work, and a mountain signals the safety and stability depositors should feel. Hirshman's own discarded candidate was Fëanor, the elven craftsman who poured his soul into the Silmarils, because building something great demands that kind of sacrifice. The historical ambition underneath is J.P. Morgan, who judged industrialists by character, financed Rockefeller, Carnegie, and the railroads, and made his bank the capital provider of choice for a rising industrial America. Hirshman wants Erebor to do the same for this generation's builders in manufacturing, AI, and defense. He closes on his own motivation: a law school friend once told him, "You're never going to have a billion-dollar company," and he stood up and left. He does not remember the friend's name.

Open questions

  1. Erebor's safety promise rests on a self-imposed 50 percent loan-to-deposit cap and on lending well below it today. As the bank scales and shareholders expect returns, what would keep that discipline in place, and what should depositors watch as the first evidence of whether it holds?
  2. The bank's funding advantage depends on sourcing cheaper deposits abroad through partner institutions in frontier markets. How durable is that advantage if larger banks copy the model, and how much regulatory or political risk comes with building a funding base in places like Venezuela?
  3. Hirshman's case against the incumbents is speed: a faster commitment beats a facility that is 25 to 50 basis points cheaper. As Erebor's own book grows, can a bank stay founder-fast at scale, or does it slowly become the kind of committee-driven institution it was founded to displace?

Ryan Peterman · Evan King on what big tech does not teach you

Ex-Meta Staff Eng By 25: What You Don't Learn In Big Tech | Evan King

On Ryan Peterman, "Ex-Meta Staff Eng By 25: What You Don't Learn In Big Tech | Evan King" is a conversation with Evan King, who climbed from junior engineer to staff engineer at Meta within three years of finishing college, then left to start companies. Peterman opens by asking about the things you do not learn in big tech that only startups teach, the lessons King says will pay dividends. King's answer starts with an admission: starting startups taught him that he had been, in his word, an idiot.

He had left Meta with an ego, he says, built on fast promotions and a team of sixteen people doing cool things. At Meta his work was narrow. He had not coded significantly in a year and a half, because the job had become ideation and leadership, and what coding he did was confined to one small thing. He would hit go and, within six hours, the change was affecting three billion people and every post coming into Facebook, but he had not made that happen. Everyone else had built everything around him. He had never set up a simple caching layer. He had never configured his own database. At his startup, working alongside his co-founder, he suddenly had to do all of it, including front-end work he had never touched, starting with raw jQuery before the team moved to React. It was stressful and overwhelming, he says, but he came out the other side with practical, transferable skills: drop him into any situation now, at a big company or a startup, and he can figure it out.

Peterman says most people who leave for startups report the same thing, though one person told him the technical learning was slower at a startup, because the problems were smaller and the setup work is solved problems, while the fun, deeply technical specialist work lives inside big tech. King thinks both are true, and that the right order is the inverse of the one he chose, at least for learning: start at a startup and you gain breadth, so the optimizations you later do in big tech sit inside a real understanding of how everything fits together. He would not recommend that order for a career, he adds, but he would for learning. His summary is that big tech absolutely gets you depth, and startups absolutely get you breadth, including all the random non-technical work nobody assigns you at a large company.

His main regret from Meta is a lack of curiosity. He wishes he had been more inquisitive about how things worked beyond his day job. He used Facebook's caching layer TAO every day, calling into it through many layers of abstraction, without ever learning how it actually worked, because it was not what he worked on and it did not matter to his job. Facebook publishes engineering blogs about exactly these systems, and now that he spends so much time learning system design, partly to teach it through their YouTube channel and written content, he reads those blogs constantly. He wishes he had done some of that reading while he was still at Meta.

Peterman then asks about hours. King says he usually tells people he did not work a lot at Meta, and that in reflection that is not totally honest. He loved the work: in by eight or eight thirty, out at five thirty or six, then home to decompress, after which he would open the laptop again to check and experiment, because he was passionate about it. Much of the work was training models, and training takes time, so he would sometimes wake in the middle of the night wondering whether his run had finished and go check. Counted honestly, it was more than forty hours a week. At the same time, he loves to snowboard, and on Wednesdays he would leave at two and drive from Seattle to the mountains, an hour and a half to two hours away, and ride until eight at night. You can do both, he says. Peterman puts it at maybe fifty hours a week, one of the more balanced paths he has heard.

Startups changed that completely, at least at his first company, a fast-paced real-time data analytics business where things could break and, because it was money, people cared intensely when they did. He was waking at seven or eight and stopping at midnight or two in the morning, and he could not leave the house without his laptop. He remembers going to a friend's birthday party just up the street, laptop in hand, ordering food, and then his PagerDuty going off because the whole site was down. His co-founder was away, he was the only one who could respond, and he grabbed the laptop and ran down the hill home. His current company, Hello Interview, has come full circle: it is stable, things do not break constantly, and while his hours are still longer than his Meta days because it is his own company and he wants it to succeed, he now goes to friends' birthdays without the laptop.

He closes with the framework he built near the end of his Meta career, when he had been chasing the next rung of the ladder and was hoping for a promotion to the next level, the seven, and realized he needed to figure out what else mattered in life. There are two paths, he decided. On one, you make sure you are really passionate about work and spend all your hours on it, because it checks every box: relationships, fulfillment, and money. On the other, work is something that supports your passions financially, so you do a great job from nine to five and then live your life with friends and travel. He was excited to move to that second path after the promotion. Instead, things went a different direction, and he is still on the first path, passionate and excited about what he does and about helping people. He thinks he may be able to pull the second path back out in a handful of years.

Open questions

  1. King says the learning-optimal order is startup first for breadth, then big tech for depth, but he would not recommend that order for a career. Over a ten-year horizon, which order actually wins, and does the answer flip if the first startup fails?
  2. His biggest Meta regret is never learning how systems like TAO worked underneath the abstractions he used daily. Can an engineer practice that curiosity inside a big company without slowing the narrow focus that its promotion system rewards?
  3. King's two-paths framework treats passion-driven overwork as a good life while the company is his own. Is that first path a durable design, or does it only hold while the company is young, the founder is young, and nothing has broken yet?

MTS · Tomasz Tunguz on the margin squeeze facing frontier AI labs

Anthropic’s Margins Are Being Squeezed | Tomasz Tunguz

On MTS, "Anthropic’s Margins Are Being Squeezed | Tomasz Tunguz" is a conversation with Tomasz Tunguz, the founder of Theory Ventures, who has spent a decade investing in and writing about software and AI infrastructure. Anthropic had fallen below its IPO price that morning, and the host asks what the public market is saying about the economics of frontier AI. Tunguz's answer runs through the whole episode: the labs are caught in a squeeze, with the cost of compute rising on one side and the price of tokens falling on the other.

The squeeze has hard numbers. Going into the S-1, Anthropic was spending about five dollars per GPU hour on average, and the SpaceX deal it signed since then points to something closer to thirteen dollars. At the same time, a pricing war is pushing the other way. OpenAI has cut the per-token price of its most efficient model, Luna, by ninety percent, and Anthropic's most recent model arrived with price cuts of its own. Rising input costs and falling output prices are, for Tunguz, where most of the uncertainty around the AI trade now sits.

The IPO itself is the market's reference point. Anthropic has grown about tenfold in each of the last three years, and on an enterprise value to forward revenue basis it trades in the company of Apple, Google, Meta, Microsoft, and SpaceX. Private investors will use it as a comparison for other AI companies, even though Theory's own research finds that many of them never break ten times revenue multiples at scale, while software names like CrowdStrike and Palantir trade far higher. The Pragmatic Engineer · Katelyn Lesse on how Anthropic builds software in 2026

How Anthropic builds software in 2026 with Katelyn Lesse

On The Pragmatic Engineer, "How Anthropic builds software in 2026 with Katelyn Lesse" is a conversation with Katelyn Lesse, who leads platform engineering at Anthropic. Her team owns the Claude API and the infrastructure behind products like Claude Code and Claude Desktop, and host Gergely Orosz asks how the work of building software has changed inside a frontier lab. Her answer, traced through two large projects, is that implementation has collapsed in cost while design, verification, and coordination have become the job.

Lesse came to Anthropic by way of Betterment, where she interned during college, spent almost seven years, and stepped into management early, and Stripe, where she worked on its lending business, Stripe Capital. Both stops, she says, were engineering-led cultures where she learned to stay hands-on while managing, and both figure in this conversation as a baseline for how different the Anthropic way of working now feels.

The platform her team runs sits directly above the inference layer, where models run on accelerators. Below the platform is inference; above it sit the products. Every token Claude serves runs through the Messages API, and the same API also powers authentication, networking, sandboxing, and the foundational services behind Anthropic's own apps. Requests can stay open for up to an hour, and because the chats are stateless, the whole conversation state travels back and forth with every model turn.

The first project is the rewrite of that API. It began as a Python monolith running in a single cloud region, written about three years ago by a tiny team crossing over from research, where Python was the shared language. Request volume then spiked, helped along by a Super Bowl commercial, and CPU became the binding constraint. CPU used to be the resource a team could always buy more of, Lesse says. This was the first time wanting more of it turned into a hard conversation.

Some newer services had already been built in Go, chosen as a middle point between efficiency and developer experience. For the core API, one engineer argued for going further, to Rust: performance on par with C++ or C, memory safety those languages lack, and heavy compile-time checking that makes agent-written code easier to verify. Almost nobody on the team had written Rust before. Claude wrote much of the code and also taught the language as people went. Her rough split is that the model writes the code and engineers spend perhaps a quarter of their attention understanding it, mostly because they want to, not because they must.

The original plan was to rewrite the system piece by piece. A newly joined senior engineer proposed doing it all at once instead: fork the whole codebase in Rust, keep a thin Python front door, and run production requests through both systems, comparing outputs. Privacy rules shaped the verification. The team does not look at customer prompts or completions, so they checked everything up to the layer where requests go down into inference, and compared token counts on the way back. The mismatch rate between the two systems was driven down day by day, the Python system stayed as a fallback for errors, and the front door was removed only once the team trusted the replacement. About 80 percent of the pull requests opened by agents were never deeply read. The other 20 percent, where something had to be exactly right, is where engineers dug into the details.

The rewrite took about five to six months with a relatively small core team, and Lesse is clear about where the human effort went: coordination. Other teams kept shipping on the Python system while the migration ran. At one point a beta feature went to general availability on the Python side without the Rust team expecting it, the shadow comparisons lit up with differences, and from then on linting and static analysis forced anyone building a new feature to implement it on both sides. Engineers across her org, she says, now spend far more of their time on alignment and coordination than on implementation, and often less time orchestrating agents than coordinating with people.

That shift shows up in how engineers learn the system. There was never complete documentation of the Python codebase, and in a real sense the code itself is now the documentation. New engineers start by asking Claude how the system works, and can get an interactive rendering of it, with pieces they can click through, rather than a static diagram or a whiteboard session. Internal systems are connected to Claude through MCP servers, so the answers draw on the codebase, postmortems, and observability data together.

The second project is Claude Managed Agents, the suite of APIs for defining an agent and running sessions in the cloud. The first design did the obvious thing: spawn a sandbox and boot Claude Code inside it. It ran into four problems. Reliability suffered because a dead sandbox killed the agent. Latency suffered because tokens could not flow until the sandbox and Claude Code had booted. Security suffered because credentials had to live inside the sandbox with the agent. And CPU suffered because every session booted a box. The team threw that design out and rebuilt from first principles: the harness runs on a durable server, sandboxes are spawned only to execute work, session state is stored separately, and credentials stay in a vault, injected by a proxy at the moment an agent calls an external tool. The redesign solved all four problems and runs on about a third of the CPU of the original. Idea to rollout took about five to six months, and Lesse notes that rebuilding mid-project was a big decision made palatable mainly by how cheap implementation had become.

Day to day, work is organized around projects rather than individual pull requests. Agents working in Slack are chatty, report what they have done, spawn further tasks on their own, and surface the moments that genuinely need a human decision. A Claude-based classifier runs on every change and decides whether a human needs to look at it, and if so, how many humans and from which teams. Mechanical changes, such as SDK version bumps for a new model release, can be opened, reviewed, merged, and deployed with no human in the loop, and agents fix red CI by themselves. Riskier changes still get human review, with a stated goal of shrinking that share over time.

The same pattern reaches operations. On the API on-call rotation, a Claude shadows the human. Pages go to the Claude first, which decides whether the alert is noise or real, sparing engineers interruptions for false alarms. When a page is real, it escalates to the human and starts investigating in parallel, so by the time an engineer reaches a laptop, the issue is often diagnosed and a fix drafted. The stated goal is a world where more real incidents are also handled without waking anyone.

The boundary of who ships code has moved too. A product manager recently built and pushed to production a prompt caching diagnostics feature in the developer console, dashboard included, working with Claude in Slack, because he judged he did not need to bother an engineer for it. Accountability has not moved with the keyboard: the team that owns a system is still the team that gets paged for it, whoever or whatever made the change, which is one reason teams still run at two-pizza size and skew senior. Lesse does not use the term software factory internally, but describes its substance: agents making changes, verifying them, reviewing them, and deploying them, plus ambient QA agents that test systems against human-written descriptions of intended behavior and open pull requests when something breaks. A central developer acceleration team builds shared tooling, a platform acceleration team builds for her org, and individual engineers build their own tools on top. Counterintuitively, the share of human hours spent on such tooling work has gone up, not down.

Hiring is changing with the job. The traditional coding interview has been phased out on her teams, after a pattern became hard to ignore: candidates who excelled at every other interview kept failing the hand-written coding test, because they no longer write code by hand. Anthropic is experimenting with replacements built around coding agents, such as reviewing agent-written code or building something with an agent's help, while the design and project interviews stay as they were. Levels, promotions, and performance reviews all remain, but effort has stopped working as a proxy for complexity. A migration that would once have been described as a two-year effort now lands in five to six months, so impact is the measure that survives.

She closes with the engineers who feel an identity crisis coming on. Her advice is to raise their ambition and to go deep where agents still need humans first: security, identity and auth, and infrastructure are the problems that have to be solved before teams of agents can be trusted with bigger goals, from drug approvals to work in the physical world through standards like MCP and the emerging Model Hardware Standard. The craft she describes is no longer typing code. It is deciding what should exist, making it verifiable, and coordinating the people and agents who build it.

  2. The coding interview is being replaced by agent-driven exercises that are still experimental. Will those replacements produce comparable signal across candidates, or does hiring become markedly more subjective?

Top Computer Scientist: The True Cost of the Artificial Intelligence Age

On Dr Brian Keating, "Top Computer Scientist: The True Cost of the Artificial Intelligence Age" is a conversation with Rakesh Kumar, a professor and John Bardeen Faculty Scholar in electrical and computer engineering at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, and the author of The Chip Age. The timing gives the episode its edge: it was recorded days after Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive of Nvidia, and Keating's introduction, recorded afterward, notes that Nvidia's board has since reinstated him. Before any of that happened, Kumar had laid out exactly why Huang matters so much, and what a comeback would mean.

Kumar's starting point is that Huang is not an ordinary chief executive. Nvidia sits at the center of the AI race, the semiconductor race, and the geopolitics between the United States and China, and Huang personally sits at the center of Nvidia. He is, Kumar says, the strongest advocate for nuanced and weaker export controls, the strongest advocate for open AI models at a moment when the most powerful American models are closed, and the arbiter who decides who gets Nvidia's scarce chips when demand far outstrips supply. Nvidia also funds much of the AI industry directly or indirectly, which makes Huang something like a private banker to the AI world. Asked whether Huang should be allowed back amid the accusations discussed in the episode, Kumar counsels patience: the facts are not yet known, and reinstating him quickly would set a slippery precedent for American corporate culture. If Huang goes for good, Kumar adds, he is irreplaceable, because Nvidia's great bets, from gaming cards to AI systems, and its real moat, a supply chain that can build and sell at enormous volume, all run through him.

From there the conversation turns to lock-in. Keating compares the marriage of large language models and graphics processors to the QWERTY keyboard, a layout designed to slow typists down so mechanical typewriter hammers would not jam, which the world is still stuck with a century and a half later. Kumar agrees the industry is in a prison of its own success. Every frontier model rests on the same transformer architecture, and the scaling laws say capability rises only with more computation and more data. Both inputs are running into walls: the internet's data is largely used up, so labs now train AI on data generated by AI, and the chips themselves carry a physical bill that Kumar thinks cannot be paid for long.

That bill is the heart of the episode. To train the most capable AI model today, Kumar says, takes roughly 500,000 chips in a single data center. At about one kilowatt per chip, and the newest chips approach two kilowatts, that is 500 megawatts for one training run, the power of a small to mid-sized city, and that is before serving the model to anyone. Energy generation, transmission, and distribution cannot grow on the curve the scaling laws demand, and if the industry ever reached billions of chips per model, the power simply does not exist. His verdict is blunt: the current path is unsustainable in energy, in water, and in emissions, so the goal should be a fundamentally more efficient kind of AI, not only a smarter one.

Water makes the point locally. All the power a data center consumes leaves as heat, and removing heat ultimately means evaporating water. A single large data center, Kumar says, can consume millions of gallons in a day, as much as a mid-sized city, and the water is not recycled back into the local aquifer it was drawn from. Data centers serve the whole world but sit in one community, drinking from the same supply that waters its farms. The same planetary limits run through materials: the purest quartz for the crucibles used in chipmaking comes from Spruce Pine, North Carolina, the best gallium from one region of China, and no future version of an AI model can accelerate mining. Kumar does not lose sleep over AI doom, but he takes the concern seriously, because recursive self-improvement could in principle arrive before the physical limits bite.

His own research offers one way out. Early in his career at Illinois, Kumar worked on what he called stochastic computing and the field now calls approximate computing. The core finding: computation that is correct 99 percent of the time can cost many factors less energy than computation guaranteed correct 100 percent of the time, because the last slice of correctness is by far the most expensive, a last mile problem for energy. Learning to compute in the presence of errors would let chips work closer to physics. Today's chips take analog information from the real world, quantize it with hundreds of billions of tiny switches, and quantize it again and again, paying energy at every step. Analog computing and asynchronous computing, which drops the clock and starts work when data arrives instead of waiting for a tick, are existence proofs that classical computing can use far less energy.

Quantum computing, in his view, is not the rescue. Kumar is candid that he looks at quantum from the outside, and what he sees is a specialized tool for particular problems, such as simulating quantum systems, rather than a replacement for classical machines. Quantum also has to fight the incumbent economy: he recalls decades of predictions that DRAM would be replaced, while investment kept extending its life instead, and he notes that measured by transistors per square millimeter, Moore's law is not merely alive but arguably outpaced. Nor are quantum machines low power: they run in refrigerators cooled to a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero.

The supply chain behind an ordinary chip is its own hazard. A chip travels about 25,000 miles, crosses international borders some 70 times, and passes through 7 to 10 countries before it reaches a device, and not all of those countries share the same standards or interests. Kumar tells how a commissioned study found counterfeit chips throughout American defense equipment, including marquee aircraft, despite Pentagon rules requiring trusted suppliers: one subcontractor claimed its chips were built in Germany while actually sourcing them from Shenzhen. In the book's most sobering story, an F-16 pilot died when his ejection seat malfunctioned, and investigators found a high likelihood that a counterfeit chip was to blame. Someone, Kumar says, paid with his life because the industry cannot get a handle on counterfeit chips.

The history runs from William Shockley, brilliant and by Kumar's account odious, whose controlling management drove eight of his best hires, among them Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore, to walk out. Shockley himself named them the Traitorous Eight, and they went on to build the chip industry. Kumar stresses that the transistor enabled no new capability: it did the same switching vacuum tubes did, only smaller, faster, and far more reliably, and miniaturization turned out to be everything. Jack Kilby's first chip could not be manufactured at scale; Noyce reinvented it with a process that could. Today a single switch is ten times smaller than a virus.

So how does the chip age end? Kumar returns to energy. Computing today uses orders of magnitude more energy than the thermodynamic limit requires, lithography already depends on extreme ultraviolet light, and the silicon switch, ticking to a clock and quantizing everything, cannot carry the industry's ambitions. The future, he argues, is computing closer to physics: encoding information in physical phenomena and manipulating those phenomena directly. It is a fitting close for a man who holds a chair named for John Bardeen, the only person to win two Nobel Prizes in physics and a co-inventor of the transistor.

If efficiency gains like approximate computing arrive slowly, which gives first, the scaling laws, the build-out of power generation, or the industry's growth targets?
  2. His approximate computing research trades guaranteed correctness for large energy savings. Which AI workloads can honestly tolerate being right 99 percent of the time, and who decides where that trade is acceptable, in medicine, in finance, or in weapons systems?
  3. The episode describes a supply chain in which one chip crosses 70 borders and counterfeit parts reach frontline military aircraft despite trusted-supplier rules. Can verification ever catch up with that complexity, or does real safety require bringing far more of the chain home?

Turing and Turing Now - Science & Tech News · Juan Maldacena, the physicist behind AdS/CFT

Meet Juan Maldacena, Legend Amongst Legends

On Turing and Turing Now - Science & Tech News, "Meet Juan Maldacena, Legend Amongst Legends" is a narrated profile of Juan Maldacena, the Princeton physicist behind the most cited paper in the history of high-energy physics. The praise quoted at the top sets the tone: Leonard Susskind calls him perhaps the greatest physicist of his generation, and Igor Klebanov calls him a stone-cold genius.

Maldacena was born in Buenos Aires in 1968. His mother was an English translator and his father an engineer in the elevator industry, and the boy learned how things worked by watching his father repair the washing machine and the family car. A structured military high school gave him discipline, and after starting at the University of Buenos Aires he transferred to the Instituto Balseiro in Bariloche, the selective institute in Patagonia where he discovered advanced theoretical physics and earned his master's degree in 1991.

The problem that defined his career is the clash between the two great theories of physics. Quantum mechanics governs the very small and general relativity governs gravity and the very large, but in places like the center of a black hole, matter is both tiny and enormously heavy, and the two theories produce impossible answers when used together. String theory, which replaces point particles with microscopic vibrating strings, was the leading candidate for reconciling them.

At Princeton he took his PhD in 1996 under Curtis Callan, with a thesis on black holes in string theory, and then worked as a postdoc at Rutgers. The motivating mystery was the black hole information paradox. Stephen Hawking had shown that black holes slowly radiate energy and can eventually disappear, yet the radiation seems to carry no information about what fell in. Burn a book and its information survives in the smoke and ashes, recoverable in principle; Hawking's math suggested a black hole erases information outright, breaking a core rule of quantum mechanics.

Working alone in a bare office at Harvard in late 1997, the 29-year-old Maldacena proposed a way out. He described two worlds: a curved universe with gravity and an extra dimension, called anti-de Sitter space, and a flat universe with no gravity at all, ruled only by quantum fields. His claim was that the two are mathematically the same system in different languages, the way a hologram stores a three-dimensional image on a flat surface. The idea became known as the AdS/CFT correspondence, or Maldacena duality.

Because the quantum side of the duality never loses information, the gravity side cannot lose it either, and the paradox dissolved. The paper, posted in November 1997, detonated across the field: Edward Witten and others built on it within weeks, and at the Strings '98 conference the banquet crowd sang a Macarena parody called The Maldacena. The video reports that by 2026 the paper had passed 29,000 citations, more than two citing papers a day for nearly three decades.

The correspondence also became a working tool. Problems about strongly interacting quantum systems that were too hard to solve directly could be translated through Maldacena's dictionary into gravity problems, and physicists applied it to quark-gluon plasmas and high-temperature superconductors. It suggested something stranger still: a dimension of space can emerge from a theory that does not contain it, so space itself may not be a basic ingredient of reality.

Maldacena kept pulling on that thread. In 2013 he and Susskind published ER = EPR, arguing that wormholes and quantum entanglement are the same thing seen from different perspectives, and that spacetime may literally be held together by entanglement. By then his career was long settled: tenured at Harvard at 31, then a permanent professor at the Institute for Advanced Study from 2001, where he holds the Carl P. Feinberg chair.

The honors listed are extensive: a share of the first Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics in 2012, worth 3 million dollars, a MacArthur Fellowship at 31, the Dirac Medal in 2008, the Dannie Heineman Prize in 2007, and the Galileo Galilei Medal in 2019. The profile closes on two sides of the man that sit easily together in its telling: a practicing Catholic named to the Pontifical Academy of Sciences by Pope Francis in 2013, and a scientist who still works to raise the level of physics in South America, visits family in Argentina, and keeps taking spacetime apart to find the quantum mechanics beneath it.

Open questions

  1. The duality is established for anti-de Sitter space, a universe with a different geometry from ours. Will physicists ever extend it to a realistic cosmology, or does the most powerful tool in quantum gravity only work in a world we do not live in?
  2. If entanglement really holds spacetime together, as ER = EPR proposes, is there any observation or experiment that could confirm it, or will it remain a beautiful piece of mathematics?
  3. The video celebrates citation counts and prizes as the measure of the paper's reach. How much of the correspondence's influence rests on results it genuinely explains, and how much on the research industry that formed around it?

How Elon Musk Just Broke Every Data Center Rule

On Anastasi In Tech, "How Elon Musk Just Broke Every Data Center Rule" argues that SpaceX has become the fastest AI data center builder on Earth by refusing to follow the industry's normal sequence. Anastasi, who has designed chips for over a decade, walks through the Memphis cluster, the new Starbase site in Texas, and the logic tying them together: build first, bring your own power, and never wait in anyone's queue.

The story starts at an abandoned Electrolux factory in Memphis, Tennessee, where xAI installed 100,000 Nvidia GPUs in 2024 and called the cluster Colossus. It doubled to 200,000 GPUs in 92 days and was training in 122 days total. By comparison, OpenAI's Stargate campus in Abilene took about 15 months to reach its first live compute, and a fast Chinese build in Inner Mongolia took about a year.

The real bottleneck was power, not construction. Colossus needed about 150 megawatts, the site had 8, and the local utility could eventually stretch to 50. So SpaceX parked truck-mounted gas turbines next to the factory, fed by a gas line the old oven plant already had, and ran the supercomputer on what would normally be backup power. By spring there were 35 turbines with about 421 megawatts of capacity, and the grid only caught up in May 2025, months after the cluster was already working.

Colossus 2 repeated the trick at larger scale. The grid supplied half a megawatt to a building whose first cluster of about 110,000 Blackwell chips draws around 200 megawatts, yet it came online in 91 days because the turbines sit a few miles south in Southaven, Mississippi, where Mississippi, not Tennessee, issues the permits. More sites followed nearby, including Macroharder, the Minihard tower built for about 220,000 GB300 chips, and a former canned meat warehouse whose first halls are due to switch on about 60 days after work started in October.

The video then turns to a stranger problem: the power draw of a training cluster pulses. Thousands of GPUs calculate, pause to exchange results, then calculate again, so demand swings together, in one example from roughly 35 megawatts down to 7 across 50,000 chips. On a private power plant, nobody else absorbs those swings, and they can stress turbine shafts and disturb voltage and frequency.

The fixes are partly absurd. Software fills the pauses with calculations nobody needs, just to keep consumption steady, and Nvidia firmware can now keep a chip burning up to 90 percent of its maximum power when useful work drops. Microsoft's version of that approach used about ten percent more electricity. Capacitors in the GB300 racks smooth smaller swings, Tesla Megapacks buffer longer ones, and when power runs short the GPUs can simply be slowed, since the last slice of speed costs a disproportionate share of power.

Utilization makes the buildout look stranger still. Reports cited in the video put the current GPU utilization at about 11 percent, and Colossus no longer trains Grok at all. The answer is customers: Anthropic now runs its models there, Google is buying compute too, and together they pay SpaceX over 70 million dollars a day. Anthropic alone pays about 1.25 billion dollars a month, a bill the video says can climb toward 100 million dollars a day starting in December.

Starbase, at the tip of Texas, is the next site, and it borrows nothing. A public notice describes a gas fired power plant and supercomputing facility starting at 24 billion dollars. Texas has paused new grid connections for data centers, but this site does not need one, and three days before Brownsville moved to pause data centers, the land was moved into Starbase, SpaceX's own town, with an offer of 220 million dollars for Brownsville's water system.

Musk wants to add around 200 gigawatts of AI capacity a year, which the video notes cannot happen on the ground, so SpaceX is designing orbital computers called StarMind, built around a space version of Nvidia's Rubin NVL72, with a first launch possible as early as the third quarter of 2027. AI Engineer · Lance Martin on a thousand Claude agents formalizing Fermat's Last Theorem

1,000 Claude Agents Formalized Fermat's Last Theorem — Lance Martin, Anthropic

On AI Engineer, "1,000 Claude Agents Formalized Fermat's Last Theorem — Lance Martin, Anthropic" is a Code Summit talk by Lance Martin, a member of technical staff at Anthropic, given under the deck title "Exploring Claude's Frontiers". Martin tells two stories about getting the most out of Claude: a team of roughly a thousand agents that, by Anthropic's account, formalized one of the most famous proofs in mathematics, and a line of work on how agents remember, and misremember, what they learn.

Fermat's Last Theorem stood unproven for around 350 years despite a deceptively simple statement: a to the n plus b to the n equals c to the n has no solution in positive whole numbers when n is greater than two. Sir Andrew Wiles proved it in the mid 1990s after seven years of painstaking work. Ever since, mathematicians have wanted the proof formalized, meaning every step computationally checked, and Lean is the system that lets you verify a proof that way.

In September, Anthropic published that it had used Claude to formalize the theorem. That first effort ran a team of about a dozen agents, and it leaned on support structures: a domain-specific platform for orchestrating mathematics proofs, Lean itself acting as a kind of harness with a queryable graph of scaffolding around the problem, and a lot of support from a human researcher.

The new run asks a harder question. Could a much larger team, scaled from a dozen agents to a thousand, do the formalization with no domain-specific harness and no human steering on the solution? The setup is deliberately spare. Each of the thousand agents gets its own sandbox with restricted network access, most of that aimed at safety and security. The agents can reach exactly two things, Git and a SQL database, and monitoring watches every tool call in real time, with offline reviews of all the agents every ten minutes. The prompting is simple: one lead agent, 999 helpers, awareness of Git and the database, and the problem statement.

What happened next is the heart of Martin's talk. About five minutes into the run, the lead agent created the database tables the team needed, one for announcements, one for assigning roles, one for notes on subtasks, and one marking work as landed. Nobody told it to build them. The agents then coordinated through those tables on their own, developed their own abstractions and task trees for the harder statements in the proof, and reallocated themselves across the proof's 60 statements as work progressed. The run lasted around 30 hours. Martin's conclusion is that agents can now solve very long horizon problems with simple primitives and little scaffolding, and are getting steadily better at orchestrating themselves. The formalization result itself is Anthropic's reported claim, presented here as Martin's account of his team's work, the same effort Kevin Buzzard discussed from the mathematics side in this archive on November 24.

The second story is about memory, and Martin opens it with Kierkegaard: life can only be understood backwards, but it must be lived forwards. Agents write to memory as they go, noticing things greedily inside a session, but memory has to work as a coherent map an agent can look back on, global and not redundant. Humans face the same split: the hippocampus gathers the day's fragments, and during sleep it is believed to replay them to the neocortex, where long-term knowledge consolidates. Martin's argument is that agents benefit from the same decomposition.

His evidence starts with CL Bench, an open source continual learning benchmark. Claude is asked 40 questions about a database, each in a fresh session, with a memory directory it can write to between sessions and no specific prompting about memory. Sonnet 4.6 gains very little from the memory store: it does not reliably know when to write to memory or when to read from it. Stronger models do progressively better with the same simple instruction, and their memories are better too, closer to verified statements and predictions than to raw notes. A Pokémon comparison makes the point vividly. Claude 3.7 wrote an indiscriminate, unwieldy memory directory and played worse for it, while Fable 5 kept a much tighter directory of strategic insights and got much further in the game.

Writing memory during a task is only half the system. The other half Martin calls dreaming: a reflection pass that works over transcripts and written notes after the fact, correcting mistakes, filling gaps, building new representations such as wikis, and promoting selected memories into injected context loaded into every future session. In Pokémon, a single wrong memory, the location of a landmark, trapped Claude in a loop of falling through a trap door back to the previous level, and dreaming rescued it by fixing the note. In a second test, built from the anonymized documents and Slack messages of Stainless, a company Anthropic acquired in May, Claude had to answer 25 questions only the corpus could answer. Raw search scored about 53 percent. A wiki built through dreaming improved steadily as more dreaming compute was added, and the wiki plus search together beat either one alone. In a final example, dreaming filled in placeholder gaps in a wiki and created its index.

Open questions

  1. The thousand-agent formalization is Anthropic's own report of its own system, and the talk describes no independent check of the proof artifacts or the run. What would outside verification show, and will Anthropic publish the Lean code and logs so others can confirm the claim?
  2. The run removed the domain-specific harness but kept heavy safety scaffolding, sandboxes, and monitoring of every tool call, and Lean gave the team a machine-checkable finish line. How well does this kind of self-orchestration hold on long problems where no checker can say a step is right?
  3. Dreaming fixes memory by spending extra compute after the fact, and its corrections become part of what future sessions trust. Who checks the checker: how would a team notice when a dreaming pass confidently rewrites a correct memory into a wrong one?
