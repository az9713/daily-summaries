Nate's Substack · Nate B. Jones on the memory shortage behind rising phone and laptop prices

Why the AI Boom Is Raising Laptop and Phone Prices

In Nate's Substack, Nate B. Jones's "Why the AI Boom Is Raising Laptop and Phone Prices" argues that the AI buildout now reaches people who never use AI at all, through the price of the phones and laptops they buy. A year ago, memory accounted for about 20 percent of the parts cost of a low-end smartphone. Now it accounts for 54 percent, according to Counterpoint Research, more than half the component budget going to RAM and storage before the manufacturer pays for the screen, cameras, processor, or battery. Worldwide average smartphone retail prices have risen 20 percent this year, by the same report.

Jones frames the result as a financial vise. Phone makers and laptop makers compete with data centers for constrained supplies of memory, so physical inputs keep getting more expensive. At the same time, the companies building those data centers face a second pressure: the market price of the intelligence they sell keeps falling, as competition pushes down what customers will pay for a given level of AI capability. Understanding who can survive that squeeze matters, he writes, when you are buying a computer, choosing an AI tool, or deciding where to work.

The first section explains how agents pulled ordinary memory into the boom. When an agent fixes a bug, the model reasons about the problem, and then the agent opens files, runs code, reads the result, launches a test, and tries again, perhaps opening a browser to inspect the page it changed. Each step needs somewhere to execute, somewhere to hold the working state, and memory for the programs doing the work. Multiplied by agents running across thousands of companies, sometimes for hours, the hardware story becomes much bigger than the GPU answering a prompt.

AI has always needed ordinary servers alongside specialized chips, but agents make those servers more central to the workload. Counterpoint's latest memory analysis identifies agentic AI as a major source of demand for conventional memory, including DDR and LPDDR, the broad families also used in personal computers and phones. Servers and laptops do not need identical modules to compete, because their manufacturers draw on overlapping production capacity. When cloud companies secure more of that capacity, consumer-device makers have less room to negotiate. And supply cannot respond quickly: expanding memory production takes years, and power has its own delays. Berkeley Lab found that, in regions with available data, new generation projects reaching operation in 2025 had spent a median of more than five years getting from a grid-connection request to operation. As Jones puts it, you cannot software-patch a memory fab into existence.

The second section turns to the squeeze on the AI providers themselves. Epoch AI estimates that the cost of achieving a given level of AI performance has fallen roughly thirteenfold per year since 2023, across the benchmarks it studied. That measures the cheapest way to reach a particular capability level, and Jones is careful to note it does not mean a subscription bill falls thirteenfold. Against that decline, a standard 16-gigabit DDR5 chip's contract price rose about 5.5-fold from October 2025 to September 2026, according to TrendForce figures reproduced by TeamGroup. A provider selling what was recently impressive capability faces cheaper competition, because better models keep arriving and work that once required a frontier model becomes possible on something smaller and faster.

Efficiency can make the calculation work, since better chips and software can cut the cost of a task even while memory prices climb, and growing usage can compensate for lower prices. The danger comes when those improvements fail to keep pace with falling selling prices, new investment, and existing financial commitments. The demand itself is not in doubt. OpenRouter, which routes requests to AI models, reported weekly token usage growing from 0.5 trillion in January 2025 to 128.4 trillion in September 2026, roughly 250-fold in twenty months. Anthropic reports that its third-quarter revenue grew more than twelvefold from a year earlier, and Jones treats paying customers as a much stronger demand signal than excitement about a demo. Strong demand gives companies a reason to build, but it does not settle whether the financing works.

That financing is the third strand of the vise. A Federal Reserve governor describes a buildout increasingly funded by debt, alongside growing long-term leases and other commitments, so companies are making promises to pay that extend well beyond the next model release. Meanwhile the equipment ages: a Brookings conference paper discusses GPUs becoming economically obsolete within three to six years, even though the buildings and power infrastructure remain useful much longer. Jones compares it to underwriting a long-term loan whose repayment depends on equipment earning attractive returns for years, when you have to judge how quickly that equipment loses earning power and what replacing it will cost. If lenders become more selective, exposed builders may struggle to finance their next expansion or refinance old debt. Providers could respond with tighter usage limits, different subscription tiers, or a harder push for profitable customers. Those are possible responses to financial pressure, he stresses, not a forecast that every AI price will rise. The pressure can still reach your workplace through a tool your team depends on, a vendor that changes its terms, or an employer that suddenly wants every AI expense justified.

The advice section starts with hardware. If your current machine does the job, a discretionary upgrade can wait. If you need a replacement for work, buy for the workload and the years you expect to keep it, because saving on RAM that you cannot later upgrade can become an expensive compromise. Budget devices reveal memory-cost pressure first, since there is so little room to absorb it: manufacturers can raise prices, cut features, or stop selling a model. Jones advises watching what happens to the RAM and storage you get at a given price, and what it costs to move up a tier, because a familiar sticker price can hide a worse deal. Compare refurbished and previous-generation machines with the memory you actually need against new base models, checking battery condition, warranty, and remaining software support. The useful comparison is the cost of a machine that will serve you well for several years, and older inventory is not automatically a bargain.

For AI tools, his counsel is to use today's inexpensive access while keeping your options open. Test routine work on cheaper, faster models, and reserve the frontier model for tasks where its additional reasoning produces a result you can actually distinguish; the right split comes from your work, not a universal percentage. Keep the instructions, source files, and evaluation examples you would need to move an important workflow, and try a second provider before an outage or price change makes the decision for you. Cheap cloud access can be useful even while local hardware is expensive, but neither today's price nor today's usage allowance is a permanent promise.

When choosing a vendor or considering an employer, Jones suggests three questions. Can this business work at today's compute costs, or does its plan require another dramatic cost decline, continued discounts, or fresh financing before it can cover its bills? What happens if its compute bill rises 30 percent, used as a stress test rather than a prediction: can it improve efficiency, absorb the cost, or charge customers more without losing them? And what would customers still pay for if model access became interchangeable, which points to valuable work the company completes, integrations customers depend on, and results that justify the price?

Those questions lead to the closing skill he calls AI cost literacy: learning what a completed task actually costs once you include model calls, failed attempts, software, and the human time spent checking the output. His test is to take one recurring workflow, run a representative set of tasks through your current setup and a cheaper alternative, and compare acceptable results, total expense, and review time, recording where the cheaper approach fails and where paying for a stronger model saves enough rework to justify it. When someone then asks whether the AI budget is worth keeping, you can show which work gets done, what it costs, and how to keep doing it if a provider changes the deal, a much stronger position than an impressive demo with no idea what it costs to run.

Open questions

  1. If memory stays above half the parts cost of a budget phone, do manufacturers keep raising retail prices, quietly cut the RAM and storage offered at familiar prices, or abandon the low end first?
  2. As lenders weigh buildout debt against GPUs that lose earning power within three to six years, what evidence about provider revenue and usage would keep financing open for the next round of expansion?
  3. If providers respond to the squeeze with tighter usage limits or new subscription tiers, how soon will employers demand cost-per-task accounting for AI tools, and which teams will be ready with the numbers?

David Senra · Jacob Hirshman on Erebor, the bank he built with Palmer Luckey

A Deep Dive Into Palmer Luckey's Bank | Jacob Hirshman, Erebor

On David Senra, "A Deep Dive Into Palmer Luckey's Bank | Jacob Hirshman, Erebor" is a conversation with Jacob Hirshman, the co-founder of Erebor, a national bank he started with Palmer Luckey in May 2025. Hirshman, a lawyer who has spent his whole career in financial services regulation, says Erebor is the fastest growing bank in American history: the company was founded in May 2025, applied for a new bank charter in June 2025, and opened its doors in February 2026. Its growth, he says, comes from serving builders in the United States, and he repeats Luckey's line for the bank: a real bank for real Americans building real things.

The first problem the conversation takes up is why America needed another bank at all. There are almost no large founder-led banks in the country, Hirshman argues, because after the financial crisis regulators effectively stopped granting new charters: the average application took two to three years and often went nowhere, which made raising money to start a bank nearly impossible, so would-be founders bought and converted existing banks instead. Erebor's founding bet was that a new administration would reopen chartering, and that a founder-led bank with entirely new technology would be genuinely differentiated. Most banks buy their technology from third-party core providers and so never drive innovation in their own stack. Hirshman says the window is now visibly open, pointing to other de novo banks and to conditional charter approvals for companies such as Mercury and Revolut, and he credits the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency with moving swiftly to admit new banks and create competition.

The idea itself traces back to the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank. Anduril held an enormous amount of money at SVB, as did many founders building in defense, manufacturing, and AI, and Hirshman says a founder plans for every business risk except the risk that their bank fails. Erebor internalized that shock as a commitment to a highly conservative balance sheet: by policy the bank holds itself to a 50 percent loan-to-deposit ratio, well below the roughly 70 percent average bank, and today it lends considerably less than even that cap. Safety and soundness, he says, sit at the core of Luckey's vision, alongside technology a founder-led team can actually fix: an API-first experience, strong customer service, and none of the committee layers that slow manager-led institutions. Senra connects this to his conversation with Revolut's Nik Storonsky, who concluded that leaning into regulation rather than running from it was one of the best decisions he made, and Hirshman agrees completely: if you provide financial services, someone in the stack is regulated, and if it is not you, it is the vendor selling to you. Owning the charter and the balance sheet, like owning a supply chain in manufacturing, lets Erebor set its own services, move faster, and fund itself with deposits so it can lend at a lower cost of funds.

That vertical integration runs through the technology as well. Every line of Erebor's code dates from May 2025: the bank built its own bank core and its own underwriting software, and its underwriters use current AI tooling to make decisions faster. Hirshman stresses that the bank does not only lend from its own balance sheet; it also advises customers on sourcing capital elsewhere when that is cheaper, acting almost like an investment bank. His example is the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital, which holds 205 billion dollars of credit authority for loans to businesses investing in strategic supply chains and infrastructure in the United States. Erebor helped one company arrange such a loan to manufacture mechanical and electrical infrastructure for data centers domestically, in a supply chain where 40 percent of capacity sits offshore today. The mission framing is explicit: the bank exists to finance the next generation of American founders building capital-intensive businesses in advanced manufacturing, robotics, and AI.

Why can those founders not simply use JPMorgan? Hirshman gives three answers. The large global banks are regulated and staffed across many countries and hedge accordingly, while Erebor is regulated exclusively in the United States. Erebor moves faster: JPMorgan might offer a facility 25 to 50 basis points cheaper, but a quick commitment de-risks a founder's decision in a way price cannot. And consolidation has hollowed out specialized banking: the country has gone from more than 8,000 banks before the financial crisis to roughly 4,800 today, and the industry-specific community banks that once understood a borrower's equipment and cycles are disappearing. His model is the old farmer's bank, a specialized lender that understood what sat on a farmer's balance sheet and could stand by him through every cycle. Erebor, he says, is building the farmer's bank for tech, starting with the defense industrial base, where he claims it can move more quickly and nimbly than any other lender, and where borrowers building drones and AI infrastructure need credit against specialized assets that dilutive equity cannot replace.

Working with Luckey has taught him to think bigger. Luckey originally wanted to call the bank The Real Bank, and Hirshman describes him as a marketing genius with boundless energy and an inexhaustible, computer-like mind. When Senra calls Erebor a niche bank, Hirshman pushes back hard: technology is the core driver of the American economy, yesterday's software founders are becoming buyers and builders of factories, and his customers are not building niche products, they are building the future of America. The same instinct shapes hiring. About 40 percent of Erebor's employees are former founders, a deliberate barbell alongside a bank president with decades of conventional banking experience. Hirshman quotes a line Senra traces to Max Levchin in Jimmy Soni's book The Founders: the best hire is someone for whom this job is the last one before they start their own company.

Relationships are the other half of his lending philosophy, and he borrows it from Sam Zell. Dealmakers like Zell and Jay Pritzker, he says, ignored seven variables and honed in on the one that mattered, and Zell would voluntarily pay a lender 7.75 percent when 7.25 was on offer, spending perhaps two million dollars extra on a large borrowing purely to build a relationship he could call on for decades. Hirshman pairs that with the four C's of credit, character above all: a South American banker he knows walks a borrower's factory floor asking questions unrelated to the deal, because the founder who is passionate about the business is the one who pays you back, while a lender's upside is capped and its downside is not. The ambition is to be the relationship bank for the next Rockefeller, as Chase Manhattan grew on its Rockefeller relationship, and Hirshman says the next Rockefeller will come out of the AI world.

The conversation also explains how Erebor was financed, a graveyard problem for earlier would-be banks that died waiting for charters or unable to raise venture capital. Joe Lonsdale was among the first to put money in, and he sits on the board; the round came together quickly, and more capital followed as the charter process advanced and demand appeared before the bank even opened. Lonsdale also supplied a lesson about trust: visiting the team's office in Luckey's garage shortly after the charter application was filed, he proposed building a headquarters modeled on Gringotts, the goblin bank from Harry Potter, arguing that a physical place customers want to visit is how a young bank creates trust. Hirshman links the same insight to Revolut's airport advertising and to Jamie Dimon, whom he praises for running JPMorgan like a founder: since 2018 JPMorgan has opened about 1,000 new branches while rivals closed theirs, a bet Hirshman thinks will pay off as AI-driven fraud makes face-to-face verification of customers more valuable, not less.

Thinking bigger also means funding sources. Because a bank's cost of funding sets the rates it can offer, Erebor looked for billions of dollars of deposits that price structurally lower than domestic ones, and found them abroad: the dollar is an amazing product, and for people living with currencies losing 25 or 30 percent of their value in a quarter, access to a regulated United States bank deposit is the best form of holding dollars there is. Through partner fintechs and banks that build on Erebor's APIs, the bank gives people around the world access to US dollar deposits, and those global liabilities are cheaper funding that lets it lend to an American factory builder perhaps 100 basis points cheaper. It is complex work, done through local institutions under local rules, and Hirshman illustrates it with a chaotic February 2026 trip to Venezuela, where political change had just reopened the question of reconnecting the country to the US banking system: refused a seat on a charter flight because his visa had not yet cleared, he routed through Bogotá, crossed the border by car at Cúcuta, was photographed holding his passport against his chest by plainclothes officers, and was finally waved through with the words "Welcome to Venezuela, sir" in time for his meetings.

The name itself carries the thesis. Luckey's first choice, The Real Bank, gave way to Erebor, the Lonely Mountain of Tolkien's legend, where the dwarves mined gold, silver, and precious metals and built a prosperous society: strategic supply chains and commodities are core to the bank's work, and a mountain signals the safety and stability depositors should feel. Hirshman's own discarded candidate was Fëanor, the elven craftsman who poured his soul into the Silmarils, because building something great demands that kind of sacrifice. The historical ambition underneath is J.P. Morgan, who judged industrialists by character, financed Rockefeller, Carnegie, and the railroads, and made his bank the capital provider of choice for a rising industrial America. Hirshman wants Erebor to do the same for this generation's builders in manufacturing, AI, and defense. He closes on his own motivation: a law school friend once told him, "You're never going to have a billion-dollar company," and he stood up and left. He does not remember the friend's name.

Open questions

  1. Erebor's safety promise rests on a self-imposed 50 percent loan-to-deposit cap and on lending well below it today. As the bank scales and shareholders expect returns, what would keep that discipline in place, and what should depositors watch as the first evidence of whether it holds?
  2. The bank's funding advantage depends on sourcing cheaper deposits abroad through partner institutions in frontier markets. How durable is that advantage if larger banks copy the model, and how much regulatory or political risk comes with building a funding base in places like Venezuela?
  3. Hirshman's case against the incumbents is speed: a faster commitment beats a facility that is 25 to 50 basis points cheaper. As Erebor's own book grows, can a bank stay founder-fast at scale, or does it slowly become the kind of committee-driven institution it was founded to displace?
