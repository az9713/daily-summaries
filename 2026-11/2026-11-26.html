MTS · Eddie Chang on generative brain models and speech interfaces

Generative Brain Models Will Arrive in 12 Months | UCSF

On MTS, "Generative Brain Models Will Arrive in 12 Months | UCSF" is a conversation with Eddie Chang, the chair of neurosurgery at UCSF and one of the pioneers of speech brain computer interfaces. His team has just published a paper in Science showing that a small patch of the brain's auditory cortex, the region long understood to handle listening, also plans the words we are about to say, roughly a quarter of a second before we say them. The finding rewrites a piece of textbook neuroscience, and Chang argues it points to where the whole brain computer interface field goes next: away from hardware, and toward generative models of the brain itself.

The puzzle is one hundred fifty years old. The auditory cortex, in the left temporal lobe just above the left ear, takes sound from the ears and turns it into words we can understand. Yet people with injuries in that area also struggle to speak. They lose the ability to finish sentences, mix up words inside a sentence, drop syllables, and sometimes invent words without realizing it. If the region only processed hearing, none of that should happen. Chang has treated those patients throughout his career, and the new study resolves the contradiction: inside the auditory cortex sits a small patch that issues commands to the parts of the brain that move the mouth and the vocal tract. It is, in effect, the planning area where words are generated.

The evidence is in the timing. Heard speech reaches the auditory cortex with a lag of about one hundred fifty milliseconds, and that listening activity shows up across the left temporal lobe. The patch behaves differently: it switches on about two hundred fifty milliseconds before a person speaks, and what it encodes is not mouth movement or sound. It carries the words themselves, things like the number of syllables a word has and how common the word is. The planning happens at the level of language, right at the interface where a thought becomes words, which is why damage there leaves patients unable to turn their ideas into speech that other people can understand.

The discovery rests on a method with no real substitute. Chang's practice treats epilepsy and brain tumors in the language areas of the brain, and during awake surgeries his team records directly from the brain with electrodes, eavesdropping on neural activity at the scale of millimeters and milliseconds. The work took fifteen years to come together, and it went public in an unusual way: the post Chang wrote about the paper on X was, he says, the first tweet he ever sent, and it went completely viral.

For brain computer interfaces, the planning area is a new place to listen. In 2021, Chang's group was the first to decode words from the brain of a person paralyzed by stroke, and groups around the world have since replicated the result. Today's speech neuroprostheses mostly read out the commands headed for the lips, jaw, and tongue, the execution side of speaking. That approach depends on the execution machinery still generating its commands, and it cannot help everyone. A signal that carries what someone is about to say, a quarter of a second early and at the level of whole words, sits upstream of the movements, and Chang hopes future systems will read from both areas at once and expand who these devices can help.

The bigger shift, he argues, is industrial. For the last ten years, the brain computer interface field has been about devices, the implants built by companies like Neuralink and Paradromics. In the new era of AI, he expects a generation of companies that look completely different, focused less on the interface itself and more on the models that can be built on top of it. What he means is a world model of the brain: a generative model, a mathematical account of how the neural code, the brain's own language for thoughts, emotions, and words, turns into human behavior. The brain is the substrate, the mind is what we experience, and the model is the expression that connects the two.

Asked for a timeline, Chang says it comes down to how quickly the data comes online, and that the first of these generative brain models will probably start appearing within the next twelve months. The medical stakes are the obvious ones: a model that could predict how a brain responds to its environment, to stimulation used as a therapy, or to medication would transform the understanding of neurological and psychiatric conditions. His lab has worked on depression and chronic pain, where the map of the involved regions is now known and the open problem is the code itself: how to deliver stimulation at exactly the right moment, and in exactly the right amount.

By 2030, he expects the first interfaces that truly model part of the human brain, including embodied forms. His group recently published work in which a participant controlled a digital avatar of his whole body, and he sees a path from there toward embodying the mind through avatars and, someday, robotics. Underneath it all sits his framing of the field: the brain is the most complex structure in the universe, the final frontier is inside our own heads, and even now, he says, neuroscience is barely past first base in understanding it.

Open questions

  1. The recordings behind findings like this one come from a small number of awake surgical patients. If generative brain models are trained on that kind of data, whose brains will they model well, and whose will they miss?
  2. A planning signal sits at the line where a thought becomes words. As interfaces learn to read it, what rules decide where decoding a patient's intended speech ends and reading unspoken thought begins?
  3. Chang expects the first generative brain models within twelve months and embodied interfaces by 2030. What clinical evidence would it take for planning based decoding to move from celebrated demonstrations to devices patients can rely on every day?
