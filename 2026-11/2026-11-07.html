Raj Shamani · Figuring Out with Mel Robbins on the next ten years

How To Make The Next 10 Years The Best Years Of Your Life | Mel Robbins | FO584 Raj Shamani

On Raj Shamani, this episode of his Figuring Out podcast (episode 584) is a long conversation with Mel Robbins, the author and podcast host whose books include The Let Them Theory, written with her daughter Sawyer. Shamani opens by saying he grew up watching her videos and that her five second rule is what got him out of bed in the morning instead of spending two hours on his phone. Robbins, who is 58, describes herself as the CEO of a media company and, more than that, as a friend: the person who encourages you when life is hard and tells you to knock it off when you start beating yourself up.

She frames a life as a movie, or a book you are only partway through. Where you are now is one scene, or one page, and it is not how the story ends, because you are still alive. People land in a discouraging chapter for many reasons: a death, a lost job, a breakup, trouble getting pregnant while friends are having babies, a string of launches that did not work. Her answer to all of it is a short phrase: yes, and now what? Nobody is going to ride in and rescue you, and nobody needs to, because the only person you can actually change is yourself.

That limit runs through the whole conversation. You have no control over what another person thinks, does, or says, and trying to control them is not love, she says, it is control; wishing your partner were different is not loving them, it is judgment. She says she learned this at 54, after decades of marriage, and that learning to accept her husband for who he is and is not, and to focus on her own responses, gave her what feels like a second marriage inside the first one. Life is unfair, and terrible things happen to people who do not deserve them. What remains in your control is what you do next.

The practical core of the episode is a whiteboard exercise. First, answer two questions: what do I really want, and why? Then ask what action actually causes that thing. Most people, she argues, fail at the first step, because what they list are shoulds: I should exercise, I should get a different job. A should is something imposed on you, not something you chose, and you will torture yourself thinking about it without ever doing it. If you cannot answer the question in the positive, answer it in the negative, and start from what you do not want.

Her tool for that is the ordinary Tuesday. Your life, she says, is not a vacation; it is Tuesdays added up. Roll the clock forward to a Tuesday in 2030 and picture the same job, the same body, the same morning routine of lying in bed scrolling. If you do not want that, you have just woken up: you can see that the path you are on does not end where you want to be. Then imagine the other 2030 Tuesday in detail, the wake-up time, the work, the people, how it feels, and write it down. The imagined day becomes the map, and the actions that lead there are usually obvious: apply for the job, stop drinking, exercise, sleep, save money, be kinder.

The hard part, she says, is doing it, and the failure happens in a specific place she calls the knowledge action gap. Between knowing and doing sits a five second window. When it is time to act, people do not act; they think about how they feel about acting, and if you think about what you need to do for more than five seconds, you will talk yourself out of it. Her fix is the rule Shamani credited at the top: count backwards, five, four, three, two, one, and then move. Counting backwards interrupts the autopilot before your habits and emotions take over.

It follows that motivation, in her phrase, is garbage. You are never going to feel like doing the things you need to do, because the brain is wired to prefer what is easy, sitting on the couch over going for a walk. She borrows an image from Dr. K of HealthyGamer: people think they are pressing the accelerator when they are actually slamming the brake. The mind can change across a lifetime, but it does not want to, so the work is to feel the resistance, the fear, the self-doubt, and make yourself move anyway, putting the action first.

She is equally blunt about the advice to fall in love with the process. You do not have to love picking up dog poop or unloading the dishwasher, and telling someone in a brutal chapter of life to love a job they hate is, she says, a privileged fantasy. What you can do is find the why underneath the hated thing: the job pays the bills, the bills take care of your family, and all work has value. Showing up well in a situation you hate, and staying grateful for what it provides, is a master level practice of gratitude rather than a failure of attitude.

For someone in a genuinely terrible chapter, she starts with survival. Chronic financial stress, not being able to meet basic needs, is an acute pain, and she does not pretend otherwise. Two things act as a flotation device while you climb out: joy and gratitude. She cites Stanford psychologist Kelly McGonigal's research that moving your body makes your muscles release what McGonigal calls hope molecules, and she treats small moments of joy, a puppy, a flower, a sunset, as things that make you want to live. The biggest blocker she sees is discouragement, the settled belief that it does not matter if you try. A small daily practice of gratitude, scanning the day for what went well, is how you build an upward spiral against it.

That practice fills out into a daily operating system. Wake up and set an intention, a trick she takes from Dr. Daniel Amen: today is going to be a good day, because I am going to make something good happen. Decide the one thing that matters today and make progress on it; she cites Harvard's progress principle, the finding that people feel purpose when they advance on something they have said is important. Move your body physically, because it shifts you mentally, spiritually, and emotionally faster than anything else. Guard your energy, because leaders bring the weather and every person influences the people around them; she pairs the law of reciprocity with the law of karma, and says the fastest way to lift yourself is to help somebody else. End the day by naming three things that went well.

Asked whether you can excel at something you hate, she leans on Angela Duckworth's research on grit: much of what looks like discipline comes from liking the thing, so yes, you can push yourself to excellence at work you dislike, but part of you will always feel the chafe unless the work serves a deeper purpose, like providing for your family, that makes you proud to do it.

Then she tells her own story. At 41 she had three kids under ten, was nearly a million dollars in debt, had lost her job, and her husband's restaurant was failing, with liens on the house. She drank too much, blamed everyone else, and lay in bed hitting snooze. Seventeen years ago, on a Tuesday in February, she started counting down and acting like the person she needed to be: someone who would get up, look for work, ask friends for help, stop drinking, and be kinder at home. Getting out took a decade, three or four jobs at a time, and strict discipline; once the family reached safety, she put fifty percent of everything that came in into savings so it could never happen again. Change, she insists, is not glamorous. It is grueling, boring, and lonely, and you will be startled at what you can accomplish in ten years if you define what you want, know why you want it, and force yourself one move at a time.

Her definition of purpose is the one she says she misunderstood longest: purpose is not something you do, it is what happens in other people when you do the things that you do. She traces the idea through Oprah to Gary Zukav's The Seat of the Soul. People will not remember your achievements, she says; they will remember whether you left them feeling better, which is why every day can be lived with purpose at a coffee shop as much as on a stage.

On speaking, her rules are plain. People remember stories, not facts. Before a meeting, pick the two words you want people to say about you after you leave the room, and show up as that person. An audience takes away exactly one thing, so decide the single message you want them to repeat, make it simple enough to explain to a five year old, and return to it like a chorus in a song. When you tell a story, do not be lazy: put the listener in the scene, with the detail she learned as a trial lawyer. And never speak to everyone, because there is no everyone; speak to one person, the way a podcast is actually heard.

On building a personal brand, she lists the mistakes she sees. Spending far too much time on input, consuming other people's content, instead of output. Giving up too soon: she started posting in 2012 after hearing Gary Vaynerchuk, and she is convinced success is connected to how long you are willing to keep showing up, ten years if that is what it takes. Chasing some perfect formula for going viral instead of tripling down on being yourself, since the people who need your particular voice cannot find you if you are imitating someone else. And caring what other people think: let them misunderstand and judge, stop looking for support from family and friends who have not made the change you are making, build community instead in the comments of the creators you follow, and do not waste the world's energy defending yourself to people determined to misunderstand you, a lesson she credits to Steven Bartlett.

Her one business and tactical mistake is not hiring fast enough. Early on, do every job in the business yourself, as she has, from editing audio to taking out the trash, so you understand it. But when frustration tells you that you are doing work that is not your skill set, ask who you can give it to, and if the answer is no one, hire, because buying back your own time is what the business most needs. Underneath it is Seth Godin's pair of questions: who is this for, and what is it for? Answer those and you have a strategy; without them you are throwing things at a wall. In a world where everyone has the same information, she argues, the real skill is focus and simplicity, and a clear intention is what lets you say no, which is hard mainly because of how other people react.

The last long stretch is about marriage. She offers a phrase for it: normal marital hatred. You cannot live with someone day in and day out without them getting under your skin, families teach you to love people you sometimes hate, and loving someone is not the same as liking them in a given week. Her secret is a sentence: you are my favorite person, even when I hate you, because I never forgot who I married. The Let Them Theory gives the boundary: let people show you who they are, then decide what you will choose, since you are not stuck in any relationship, job, or group chat. Two tests help decide whether a hatred is a chapter or an ending. First, citing the Gottmans' research that about sixty-nine percent of what couples argue about never gets resolved, ask whether this person, exactly as they are, is still your favorite person. Second, ask whether you would be happy if your kids had this marriage. If the answer unsettles you, it means there is work to do, and a marriage is worth fighting for when both partners are willing to do it; if one partner will not, that is the answer. And a relationship that forces you to give up your values or a dream will curdle into resentment no matter how much you once loved the person.

Shamani closes with the three questions he asks every guest. What she knows that the world does not: the best days of your life are ahead of you, a thousand percent, if you are willing to make them better. The most extraordinary thing she wants to do next is deliberately small: sit on her porch beside her husband of thirty years, dogs at her feet, and really notice a sunset. And the personal cost of becoming Mel Robbins, she says, is that the old Mel Robbins had to die. She killed her, and it was the best thing she ever did.

Open questions

  1. If the five second rule works by cutting off deliberation, where is the line between healthy decisiveness and an impulse that deserved more than five seconds of thought, and how is a person supposed to tell the difference in the moment?
  2. Robbins insists you cannot change another person, yet also says a marriage is worth fighting for when both partners do the work: what does doing the work together actually look like when neither partner can make the other change?
  3. Her brand advice is to stay unmistakably yourself for as long as ten years, while her business advice is to hand off everything that is not your gift: how does a creator tell which parts of the work are the irreducible self and which are batons to pass?

Raj Shamani · Figuring Out with Gita Gopinath on how money is created

How Money Is Created: Central Banks, Money Printing & Inflation | Gita Gopinath | FO585 Raj Shamani

On Raj Shamani, this episode of his Figuring Out podcast (episode 585) is a long conversation with Gita Gopinath, the former chief economist of the IMF and now a Harvard professor. Shamani starts from the most basic questions he can ask: where does money come from, who prints it, and who decides what it is worth. Her answers build into a tour of the whole world economy, from central bank rate decisions to tariffs, AI valuations, government debt, and what a 25 year old should learn now.

Her starting point is savings. Money for investment comes, in the first instance, from what people do not spend, and the world currently has a lot of it because the global population is aging into the decades of life, the forties through sixties, when people save the most. Banks collect those savings and lend them on, though savers can also hold shares, corporate bonds, or government bonds directly. That pool of savings is why governments can carry debt loads that look alarming without interest rates going through the roof.

The central bank's job, she explains, is narrower than most people assume. Money is useful because it is liquid, something you can transact with and hold as reserves with confidence. The central bank manages the amount of money in circulation against the demand for it so that inflation stays near a target, about 2 percent in the US and 4 percent, plus or minus 2, in India. When demand booms, for example because foreign money pours in or a government stimulates hard, the bank raises interest rates to cool it.

She walks through the chain that follows. A rate rise makes loans costlier, so factories do not get built, construction workers do not get hired, materials do not get bought, and house purchases fall away as mortgage rates climb. Each person feels only their own decision, but the central bank is solving the collective version of the problem: if everyone borrows and spends at once, inflation is what results.

Borrowing itself is not the sin. A country, like a founder with a good idea, can borrow because others believe in its future, and she calls US borrowing partly a sign of strength: the US is the frontier destination for AI investment. Trust in US debt rests on its huge tax base and on hopes for growth. If AI lifted US growth from 2 percent to 3.5 percent a year, the debt arithmetic would look after itself. Size helps too. With some 40 trillion dollars of debt trading, an investor can sell billions without moving the price, a liquidity smaller markets cannot offer. On the measure that matters, debt relative to GDP, Japan is actually the outlier at around 230 percent, against roughly 100 percent for US federal debt.

Printing is not an escape hatch, and she is blunt about why governments borrow instead. A government that cannot persuade savers or foreign investors to lend will eventually lean on its central bank to print, the new money gets spent, and inflation wipes out its value. Zimbabwe is the classic case, Argentina a repeated one. The cycle ends with the government giving people money that buys nothing.

On the yuan, she returns to a piece she co-wrote arguing the currency is undervalued. A country that grew as fast as China should, by the pattern of Japan or South Korea, have seen its real exchange rate appreciate far more. It did not, because China's growth model kept consumption weak: consumption is around 40 percent of GDP there, against 60 to 80 percent almost everywhere else. Layered on that are capital controls far stricter than the caution most emerging markets show after lessons like the East Asian crisis. China, she says, is hyper controlled about who brings money in and who takes it out.

Tariffs get a similar treatment. US imports from China have fallen sharply, but China has rerouted: building factories in Vietnam, Mexico, and Thailand, and selling more to Europe. None of it is free. The evidence puts most of the tariff cost on the US importer, who absorbs some in margins and passes some to consumers, while China faces weaker demand, a property sector in real trouble, excess capacity in electric vehicles and solar panels, and youth unemployment at its highest in a long time. The strong China story, she argues, is not the economy China actually has right now.

Asked what replaces the dollar in 20 years, her answer is the dollar. Depreciation is possible, since the currency strengthened so much over 15 years, but depreciation is not dethronement. A BRICS currency makes no sense to her: somebody's central bank would have to maintain the value of a synthetic bundle, and none exists to do it. China could only rival the US by opening its financial system, freeing capital movement, and building truly independent institutions, which she says does not come with the current political setup.

India's opportunity is the mirror image. The world is looking for new partners, and India has done well in many dimensions, but it is not yet an important player in global supply chains, especially manufacturing. The choke points are domestic: starting a business is still complicated, contracts can sit in courts for ten years, land titles are murky, and officials harass. She credits real reforms already done, the goods and services tax, UPI, physical infrastructure, and the bankruptcy code, but says the next round needs big political will. Change of the Milei kind usually takes repeated crises to become tolerable, and a country growing at 7.5 percent feels little urgency. Corruption, she notes, does recede in many countries, the US included, but typically over decades.

All of this sits inside what she calls the great gamble. Two huge forces, AI and geopolitics, are pushing in opposite directions, and so far they roughly cancel: global growth for 2025 and 2026 looks much as projected before tariffs or the AI boom. Underneath, the picture splits. Middle East economies are being downgraded, Taiwan and South Korea ride the AI supply chain to blockbuster growth, and the headline 25 percent Liberation Day tariffs settled nearer 10 to 12 percent after markets pushed back. The gamble is that the world has never been this integrated, power has never been this diffuse across the US, China, and assertive middle powers, and the rules based trading order is being dismantled without coordination, including on AI safety.

On AI valuations, she does not hedge much: she personally expects a correction. The technology is phenomenal and fiercely competitive, and it is hard to explain how companies investing close to a trillion dollars globally in AI in 2026 alone will earn the profits needed to recover it. The linkages worry her as much as the levels: NVIDIA funds labs that use the money to buy NVIDIA chips, and large parts of the financial system outside the regulated banks are hard to see into. A correction would likely mean a short lived recession, but the financial engineering could make it more than projected, the way subprime turned out to be the tip of an iceberg.

Her deepest worry is the one she says gets crowded out by tariffs and wars: debt. Global government debt will reach 100 percent of GDP within a couple of years, second only to the aftermath of the Second World War, and the squeeze has spread from developing countries to advanced ones, with the UK and France getting little room from markets. The US ten year borrowing cost has risen from around 2.5 percent to about 5 percent. What could make it explode is another crisis forcing governments to spend enormously again, deficits of 10 percent of GDP rather than 6.5, with US rates setting the floor for everyone else's. Before stopping spending, she warns, governments tend to try financial repression, pushing banks and savers into holding government debt.

Shamani presses her on the IMF's May decision to lend to Pakistan again, after repeated programs over 35 years. Her defence is institutional. The IMF is a financial firefighter, created for countries in deep distress, and distress itself is a breeding ground for the very spillovers people fear. Lending decisions are made by the membership, not management; the US, with about 17 percent of the vote, has no veto over them. Nobody has ever defaulted to the IMF. The test each time is whether the government in place has policies that can move the country away from the abyss, and a fund that walked away from a country in crisis would not be doing its job.

The closing stretch turns personal and practical. She names cognitive surrender, students outsourcing their thinking to AI, as a live harm comparable to the pandemic's lost learning, visible in the gap between take home and in class performance even at Harvard. Her advice to a 25 year old is to build expertise the slow way, making the mistakes yourself, to use AI as a challenger that sets new problems rather than as a substitute, to favour the messy human questions of the social sciences, which she finds more AI proof than well defined fields, and to keep working with other people instead of a brilliant classmate on a phone. Asked for advice nobody should follow, she picks "be like me": copying how someone else did it is, in her word, crap. And the personal cost of her career, the family time a workaholic schedule took, she refuses to call a sacrifice. It was a choice, made by a self described rebel who hated being told what a girl does and does not do, and she would not make it differently.

Open questions

  1. The world's savings glut is what makes today's debt loads affordable, so what happens to borrowing costs when demographics turn and that glut starts to shrink?
  2. If a correction in AI valuations is likely but its timing unknowable, what should an ordinary investor actually do differently today?
  3. Gopinath says China cannot rival the US without opening its financial system, and that opening does not fit its current politics: is there any middle path between the two systems, or only a choice?

20VC with Harry Stebbings · Scott Wilson on the WashU endowment

Inside WashU's Endowment Fund | SpaceX Made Up 20% of Out AUM | Scott Wilson

On 20VC with Harry Stebbings, host Harry Stebbings talks with Scott Wilson, the chief investment officer of Washington University in St. Louis, about how he rebuilt one of America's large university endowments around concentration. Wilson arrived from Grinnell, where he had run a similar book of concentrated managers, co-investments and direct positions, and he brought the same playbook with him. In his first week at WashU he put in redemptions for more than half the portfolio, and over eighteen months the team turned over more than 75 percent of it, including selling out of all 36 or 37 hedge funds the endowment then held. The pool was just under 8 billion dollars when he started; today it is just under 20 billion, and Wilson says the team has generated about 17 billion dollars of profits on the original capital while paying out roughly 5 billion to the university.

The model itself is deliberately simple. Find really good external partners. When a partner finds something unique, double, triple or quadruple down on it alongside them, and let it compound for a long time. Half of the portfolio sits in the top 50 look-through positions, and Wilson says diversification math fades fast: beyond 20 or 30 underlying names there is little quantitative benefit, and a hedge fund typically holds the bulk of its capital in its top 15 or 20. He would rather the top 30 names drive performance. Positions usually start around 1 percent of the portfolio, roughly 200 million dollars at today's size, often beginning as a 50 million dollar co-investment that scales up over time.

The clearest proof of the approach is SpaceX. At one point more than 20 percent of the entire portfolio was in that single name. Wilson credits Lee Fixel, the board and Adam (his colleague) for the win, and stresses that holding it was a governance achievement as much as an investment one: the team had many chances to take liquidity, advocated instead for holding it through continuation vehicles up to and through the IPO, and could only do that because the board gave them room. At cost, anything above 2 or 3 percent of the portfolio in one name means notifying the board, and above about 4 percent they need approval, but within those lines the leeway to concentrate is wide.

Publics and privates, Wilson argues, differ mainly in how often they are marked. If Stripe went public tomorrow it would not belong in a different bucket, and the real constraint is liquidity: the endowment pays out roughly 5 percent a year, smoothed over a rolling five year average, so payouts must come from the liquid half. Without that outflow he would target 70 or 80 percent in privates, because private markets are less picked over and the endowment's privates have compounded in the low to mid 20s over the last decade against about 10 percent for public equities. More than half the private book is already in co-investments and directs, and he would take that to 80 percent if the supply of partner-fed deals allowed it.

The direct engine runs on offense, not inbound flow. Everyone on the team is a generalist, and 80 to 90 percent of their time is spent meeting individual companies rather than managers. The process starts from a partner they already trust saying a company is exciting, then the team independently verifies the thesis, underwrites the company and asks whether it is the best marginal use of capital; Wilson estimates they do roughly 0.1 percent of the deals they see. He warns there is a huge negative selection bias in waiting for someone to call offering a syndicated slice of a hot name. Without the directs and co-investments, he concedes, the endowment would have performed right at the peer average instead of at or near the top.

On fund selection, the model does not work with mega funds: they do not need WashU's capital or its co-investment, and Wilson doubts funds that have doubled in size can repeat historic multiples, since they must either double check sizes at higher valuations or do twice as many deals, where the fiftieth bet is worse than the top ten. The endowment is in plenty of funds above a billion dollars but none above 10 billion, prefers a manager who raises a billion when they could raise four and returns every 18 months, and adds only one or two partners a year, roughly one in and one out. He famously passed on Sequoia's evergreen structure, discussing it with the board because it did not fit the co-investment model, and concedes it was probably a mistake on returns while noting they have done fine anyway. The most common reason he walks away from a manager is discipline around valuation: one partner, he says, never met a 100 times revenue deal he was willing to turn down.

On performance, the team barely talks about fund returns at all. Everything is aggregated into look-through exposures, so the conversation is about how many dollars of SpaceX, Cerebras or Anthropic the portfolio holds. That lens drives what happens after an IPO: Nubank was distributed to them as stock roughly five years ago and they still hold it, while Cerebras, a very large position at its IPO that has since traded poorly, saw them take some chips off the table and hedge even before distribution, though they still hold some and back its team. For SpaceX they will gradually sell over a multi-year period, not because the story is over but because it would be, in Wilson's phrase, stupid not to take some chips off the table given their fiduciary duty, and he would rather be not stupid than clever.

His cycle view is blunt. Private markets are showing the same behaviour as the 2000 tech bubble, the financial crisis and the 2021 crypto bubble, with a clear valuation premium: he thinks Anthropic could have raised privately at more than two trillion dollars, doubts public markets would pay that, and argues the lack of DPI is not closed IPO windows but private marks that public buyers will not match. He questions the AI profit pool itself, noting the top five large language models are near indistinguishable for most tasks and that half of today's AI companies may not exist in ten years. The endowment has decent Anthropic exposure through partners and holds it partly for relationship reasons. Right now, liquidity coming back from privates is being redeployed into public markets, especially non-AI infrastructure sectors he sees as relatively protected from AI.

His confessed mistakes are specific. The biggest was not adding to winners once a position had grown or the price had risen, a framing bias he says cost billions. The second was greed in reverse: in late 2020 the team prepared a large secondary sale of LP interests, then held off to capture the December quarter's write-ups, watched the bubble top out in 2021, and missed selling at 80 or 90 cents on the dollar. Two concentrated manufacturing bets, one automated and one additive, have also hurt, and he accepts the 80/20 reality that a fifth of investments produce most of the profit.

Geographically he is more open than most peers. Europe offers the same quality of asset at small earnings multiples where a US equivalent trades at a huge revenue multiple, and price is not value. China, where WashU keeps two people in Shanghai, has been investable for them since the uninvestable call, provided positions are under the radar or politically supported and run through local partners. In the quickfire round he wishes he were in Benchmark, names Lee Fixel's Addition as the one fund he would put everything in, would add Elon Musk or deep industry expertise to his board, and offers his standing advice to the young: be the best at the things that require no talent, showing up, curiosity, hard work, good questions, respect and kindness. He stays at WashU, he says, because of the board, the chancellor, the team and the mission, and because he is well compensated and happy.

How Amjad Masad Saw Vibe Coding A Decade Early

On Y Combinator, this episode of How to Build the Future has Paul Graham interviewing Amjad Masad, the co-founder and chief executive of Replit. Graham opens with the claim in the title: Replit basically invented vibe coding, and Masad saw it coming a decade early. The wish dates to 2008, when Masad was sitting in class with a Symbian smartphone, wanting to program on it. By 2015 and 2016, Replit's original pitch deck was already arguing that a platform with millions of people coding could train a machine learning model to predict code. That was before the Transformer paper existed.

The path there was incremental from the inside, however discontinuous it looked from outside. Before the agent, Replit's autocomplete was already generating something like 50 to 60 percent of the code. The decisive observation came from watching users in the office: people who had never coded would open a chat window beside Replit, copy generated code in, copy the error back, copy the fix in again, doing by hand what a machine should have been doing. Automating that loop was the product. The loop had to understand the errors, and its stopping condition had to be a working program, not a single turn of chat.

When the agent launched in September 2024, it demonstrated two things at once. The first was that a language model could run to completion, looping through errors until the software worked, which surprised even the model companies: they reached out to ask how Replit had made it work. The second was that AI could provision its own infrastructure, spinning up a database or a cache and hosting the result as tool calls. Masad notes that the 2024 harness was roughly ten times more complex than the harness today, because weaker models needed far more engineering around them. As models improve, the agent layer gets simpler, which he accepts also makes life easier for competitors: everything in AI is being commoditized constantly, and the only answer is to keep doing the next thing.

The reason Replit could ship first is the part under the waterline. Graham uses Masad's own iceberg image: above the surface, you tell it in English what to build and it writes the code; underneath sit years of systems programming, including a file system built from scratch to be transactional and fully reversible, sandboxing, and kernel and operating system engineers. The collaborative first version of Replit turned out to be load-bearing, because an agent dropped into a project is simply another user on the same system, able to work alongside a human. The company itself was organized like the stack, and a new agent team was placed in the middle of the existing infrastructure, cloud, glue, and editor teams. The editor team now builds editing tools for agents rather than for people, a real problem in 2024, when models hallucinated line numbers and would lazily leave a function unfinished. Today, Masad says, you can mostly just give an agent a shell.

It barely worked at first. In beta, the agent failed something like 30 percent of the time, and users tolerated it because the alternative for a non-programmer was the manual copy and paste routine, or paying an outsourcing shop. That comparison was the early market: roughly a hundred thousand dollars for an outsourced build against roughly a thousand on Replit. An earlier attempt to serve the same demand, Replit Bounties, was shut down once Masad concluded that people would soon ask agents for the whole thing directly instead of hiring teenagers to ask on their behalf.

Graham returns to his own first sight of the demo, in July 2024, when Masad told him not to look at the code because it was object code, and Graham realized the craft he had spent decades on had been demoted to a byproduct. By the end of 2025, he argues, everyone had been convinced, and he credits OpenClaw, which people could simply text, with giving everyday users their first feel of an agent running to completion, a form factor lesson he says will shape what Replit builds next.

Reinvention is not optional, in Masad's telling, because AI commoditizes whatever you shipped last; competitors copy the frame of the film from six months ago while you are supposed to be shooting the next one.

His concrete prediction is about scale per person: from using one agent, to ten, to a hundred, to ten thousand. Ten thousand agents cannot run on a laptop, which is his argument that work finally moves off local machines and into the cloud, the outcome Replit was built for long before agents existed.

The most important report in AI right now

On Matthew Berman, the video "The most important report in AI right now" walks through a report from METR, the independent evaluation group, titled "Independent assessment of AI R&D acceleration at Anthropic" and dated October 2026. The headline finding is that most METR staff who worked on the project now believe Anthropic is improving the capabilities of its models on well specified tasks at around twice the rate it managed between March 2024 and October 2025. They add that they would not be surprised if the true rate were closer to 1.3 times, or to 3 times, the historical one.

The more striking claim is about the cause. METR tried to isolate why the pace picked up, and concluded it was not more hiring, not better human researchers, and not an explosion in training compute. Their estimate is that the majority of the current acceleration, perhaps about 80 percent of it, is due to AI progress itself: the models have become good enough at writing code and running experiments that they are now a main engine of their own improvement.

Berman spends time on the timing. There was an earlier pickup in late 2025, right as the latest Opus arrived and models became dramatically better at writing code, and METR thinks that phase owed more to non-AI factors such as more compute and human breakthroughs. The chart of AECI, Anthropic's internal capability measure, shows roughly linear growth through the end of 2025, then an inflection at Opus 4.5, then Mythos early this year, with the curve still steepening. Anthropic, METR notes, made as much progress on benchmarks in the past year as it did between October 2023 and October 2025.

The reason this compounds, in Berman's telling, is that everything feeds everything else. Better models do more of the research. They also squeeze more out of limited compute, and he points to Anthropic's S-1 filing, ahead of its IPO, as showing compute efficiency improving sharply quarter after quarter, which frees up more capacity for research and brings in more revenue to fund it. He offers Google's AlphaEvolve as a smaller parallel: AI pointed at Google's own hardware architecture found a 1 percent efficiency gain, which at Google's scale is worth billions.

The supporting evidence is partly human. METR interviewed Anthropic employees and combined what they said with self-reported uplift, headcount, lines of code, and experiment usage. The quotes Berman reads out are striking: researchers endorsing a greater than 2x speedup, one saying he would endorse two years of progress in one year and does not think four is crazy, and one saying the team is now only bottlenecked on ideas and compute, no longer on code.

METR saw internal, unreleased models beyond the current Mythos generation, and concluded that this continuing line of internal models makes a permanent change in the slope of progress look more plausible than a one time jump at the Mythos preview. Oxford Mathematics · Graham Farmelo on Hawking and the end of theoretical physics

Is the End in Sight for Theoretical Physics? - Graham Farmelo

On Oxford Mathematics, the public lecture "Is the End in Sight for Theoretical Physics? - Graham Farmelo" revisits the most provocative question Stephen Hawking ever asked in public. Farmelo is a biographer and science writer whose authorised biography of Hawking appeared in September 2026, and his title is deliberately borrowed: it is the same title Hawking chose for his Lucasian inaugural lecture at Cambridge in April 1980. Farmelo traces where Hawking's prediction came from, how it was received, how Hawking himself revised it over the following decades, and what answer the question deserves today.

Hawking opened that 1980 lecture by suggesting that the goal of theoretical physics might be achieved "in the not-too-distant future, say by the end of the century": a complete, consistent and unified theory of the physical interactions, one that would describe all possible observations. He meant it literally enough to put odds on it. In a letter to Lewis Strauss, he said he expected to be out of a job as a theorist by the year 2000, and he rated the chances of that happening at fifty-fifty.

The idea that physics might be nearly finished was not invented by Hawking. Farmelo sketches the discipline's long formation: Newton's Principia in 1687 set out the first working method, the subject took recognisable shape in the hands of Laplace and his associates in the early 1800s, and William Whewell coined the word "physicist" in 1836. Theoretical physics became a distinct profession only in the 1870s, in the German states after the Franco-Prussian War, when institutions first paid people simply to sit and theorise. Its first famous figure, Max Planck, had been warned as a young student by his teacher Philipp von Jolly that, with the conservation of energy formulated, physics seemed to have taken its final form. Planck's own quantum paper of 1900 demolished that verdict.

The two pillars of the modern subject, general relativity and quantum mechanics, were both raised in the first quarter of the twentieth century, the glory years of the field. The great unfinished business was marrying them. Paul Dirac, who spent years wrestling infinities out of his attempt to combine quantum mechanics with relativity, prefaced his celebrated 1931 monopole paper with something close to despair, doubting whether human intelligence could reach the needed ideas by working directly from experimental data. By 1939 he was arguing the opposite way forward: mathematics, pursued for its beauty, should lead and experiment should follow. Dirac expected another revolution, not an ending. Einstein, in letters spanning 1917 to 1938, was blunter still: science "is not and never will be a closed book".

Farmelo's own student years, as a doctoral researcher in the 1970s, felt like a party by comparison. The Standard Model of particle physics was taking shape in real time, with results arriving from CERN, Stanford and Harvard, while Roger Penrose's singularity theorems transformed the theory of gravity and Hawking, building on Penrose's mathematics, showed in 1974 that black holes radiate. Farmelo calls that result the first clear, twenty-four-carat product of quantum gravity, even though it was won with a semi-classical calculation rather than a true quantum theory of gravity. Excitement then crested around supersymmetry and its gravitational extension, supergravity, which Hawking believed might grow into a single quantum field theory covering every fundamental interaction. The Dutch theorist Gerard 't Hooft was sceptical, and the two debated it, but Hawking's enthusiasm was real.

That enthusiasm is why, on his election to the Lucasian chair at the end of 1979, Hawking chose the end of physics as his theme rather than a safer lecture on black holes. The choice was made against a stark personal backdrop. His health was deteriorating and care was expensive, he had lost the use of his hands and did his calculations in his head, and he had told his friend Kip Thorne that he expected to be dead by 1980. The lecture itself was read aloud by his student Chris Pope to a hushed, roughly five-hundred-strong audience in which students, drawn by Hawking's star power, were unusually the majority.

The boldness landed, but not entirely as intended. Several people who were there later admitted they thought Hawking was joking. As the theorist Mike Duff put it, supergravity was "a theorist's dream but still a long way from the real world", and Farmelo suspects Hawking knew his case was flimsy and judged it better to be attacked than ignored. Attacked he was. Hermann Bondi had already named the temptation the "lure of completeness". The sharpest blow came from Philip Anderson, the condensed matter physicist, who wrote in Physics Bulletin that Hawking claimed a theory describing all possible observations while showing no obligation to understand ordinary solids, liquids and gases in any detail at all. Hawking replied in kind and did not retreat a step. He had also written in advance to Dirac, asking what the older man thought of the question. Dirac's entire reply was one word: "No."

Hawking, undeterred, returned to the theme for the rest of his career. In 1997 the final theory was still, conveniently, twenty years away. At a White House millennium event in 1998 he predicted that supersymmetry would eventually be confirmed at CERN, took another fifty-fifty bet that the ultimate theory would arrive within twenty years, and added a concession to Anderson's critique: once found, the final theory would place no limit on complexity, and complex systems would become the real frontier. Steven Weinberg's book Dreams of a Final Theory argued a similar faith, though Farmelo notes Weinberg admitted it was faith, and that he was also trying to persuade Congress to fund the Superconducting Super Collider.

The first quarter of this century gave the field two triumphs and a long quiet. The Higgs boson's discovery at CERN in July 2012 completed the Standard Model and, for Farmelo, closed the long twentieth century of fundamental physics that opened with the electron's discovery in 1897. The detection of gravitational waves, from two colliding black holes, moved him to tears. But the Large Hadron Collider has produced nothing on the Higgs's scale since, supersymmetry has not appeared, and Farmelo reports that of the dozens of physicists he asked while preparing the lecture, not one treated the end of theoretical physics as anything but a joke. His Exhibit A is the cosmological constant: the predicted energy of empty space is wrong by some 120 orders of magnitude, and on that basic quantity the field, by its own admission, has no clue.

The lecture's most prescient passage, though, was its ending. Hawking abruptly changed the subject: extrapolating the rapid improvement of computers, he proposed that they might take over theoretical physics altogether, so perhaps the end was in sight for theoretical physicists, if not for theoretical physics. Anderson, in 1980, called even that suggestion naive. Farmelo closes by pointing at the evidence accumulating now: Terence Tao and others rethinking the nature of mathematics under AI pressure, and leading theorists such as Nima Arkani-Hamed completing work with help absorbed at an AI workshop. His own verdict matches Dirac's: the end of theoretical physics is nowhere near. What is beginning, he suggests, is a different way of doing it, in which the theoretical physicist works alongside an AI facility, and the job itself starts to feel very different.

Open questions

  1. If AI systems become working partners in theory building, what happens to Dirac's guiding principle of mathematical beauty: does it survive as a human judgement about AI-generated mathematics, or does the machine's output make it obsolete?
  2. The cosmological constant is wrong in theory by 120 orders of magnitude, which Farmelo offers as the measure of how far the field is from any final theory. Is that a problem awaiting a conceptual revolution, as Dirac expected, or a sign that the question itself is being posed wrongly?
  3. Hawking's computer prophecy was the part of his 1980 lecture most mocked at the time and the part that now looks most accurate. Which of today's confident dismissals, about AI or about candidate final theories, is most likely to age the same way?

How I Invest Podcast · Jack Selby on the AI bubble and the PayPal Mafia

Peter Thiel's 27-Year Partner on the AI Bubble, the Venture Capital Crisis, and the PayPal Mafia

On How I Invest, David Weisburd's episode "Peter Thiel's 27-Year Partner on the AI Bubble, the Venture Capital Crisis, and the PayPal Mafia" is a long conversation with Jack Selby, a managing director at Thiel Capital who was one of the first ten employees at PayPal and has worked alongside Peter Thiel for 27 years. Selby co-founded the macro hedge fund Clarium Capital with Thiel, and he now runs Copper Sky, a venture fund built on a deliberately unfashionable bet: that the best-priced opportunities in American venture capital sit in the 47 states Silicon Valley largely ignores.

Weisburd opens with the PayPal Mafia, the alumni network that produced Elon Musk, Max Levchin, Reid Hoffman, David Sacks, Roelof Botha and others. Selby's explanation is unglamorous. The company sold to eBay for about a billion and a half dollars in under four years, most of that time spent surviving the dot-com crash, and the executive team's average age on the IPO roadshow was 26. They got a taste of success while they were young, and they wanted more.

The talent itself, he says, came from Thiel's best-athlete approach to hiring. PayPal was not hiring payments experts, because the product barely existed: the original idea was beaming money between Palm Pilots, and the Series A round, raised from Nokia, was largely spent figuring out how to encrypt the infrared beam. Thiel hired the smartest people he could find and let the culture coalesce on its own. Selby, at 25, ended up running international operations he knew nothing about, and made it up as he went along.

What forged the group, in his telling, was stress. Running PayPal was a constant fire drill, from Max Levchin's fight against Russian fraud rings trying to drain the network to the everyday sense that a successful startup is a treadmill that only speeds up. The lessons Selby says he carried away start with work ethic: you could email Thiel or Musk at two in the morning and get an answer within ninety seconds, and that always-on standard was shared by the whole management team.

The second lesson is about people who do not fit. Thiel's rule was to rip the band-aid off almost as soon as you know, because a bad fit acts like a cancer on the culture. Selby admits this does not come naturally to him and that he has had to learn it. The third lesson runs the other way: work with your friends. He runs Copper Sky with Jason Pressman, his best friend since 1999, and argues that adults give young people literally terrible advice when they warn against it, because two people who know each other that well multiply each other's time.

The heart of the episode is Selby's geographic thesis. Roughly 90 percent of venture capital flows to three states, California, New York and Massachusetts, and the share is getting more lopsided, not less. The other 47 states hold more than 290 million people and generate more than three quarters of the country's economic output, yet receive a small fraction of early-stage risk capital. Selby calls that imbalance preposterous, and it is the gap Copper Sky exists to exploit.

His Exhibit A is Carvana. Ernie Garcia Jr. and his father pitched the online used-car marketplace up and down Sand Hill Road and were turned away, because the firm crowd judged it not sufficiently tech. The family funded the business themselves in Arizona, and Carvana went on to reach a market value Selby puts at 65 billion dollars. The next Carvana, he argues, should come out of Phoenix, Milwaukee or Cincinnati, and building it in Palo Alto would be illogical, given higher costs and lower quality of life. He concedes one exception freely: if you are building the next Anthropic, you should probably be in Palo Alto.

Weisburd presses the standard counterargument, that founders need coastal talent density. Selby rejects the premise. Silicon Valley's network effect, he says, is real but financial, not human: founders move to Palo Alto because that is where the money is, not because the smart people are unavailable back home, and the argument becomes circular. His pitch to founders in the interior follows from that. He offers them the advantages of Palo Alto without the move: help hiring a C-suite executive from his Rolodex, introductions to big customers, doors opened at Sand Hill Road firms. It is, he says, an a la carte menu, and the founder can choose zero items.

What he looks for is deliberately old-fashioned. Copper Sky is sector agnostic, and Selby describes the job as talent scouting: after enough thousands of hours sitting across from aspiring entrepreneurs, you learn to spot the sparkle in the eye. The person matters more than the sector. What he screens hardest for is ambition, in a phrase he borrowed from a Greylock friend: equal bits of grit and grandiosity. Grit is plentiful in Milwaukee and Cincinnati. Grandiosity often is not, and a founder content with a hundred-million-dollar exit in five or seventeen years is, for his fund's math, a mismatch.

That math is the core of his pitch. In Copper Sky's first fund, he says, they could typically invest at about a tenth of the average Silicon Valley Series A valuation: ten cents on the dollar. At those entry prices he can buy 10 to 20 percent of a company for an average ticket of about three million dollars, and his funds need portfolio companies to reach roughly 500 million dollars, not a trillion. He contrasts that with paying a reported 14 billion dollar valuation for a pre-Series A stake in Thinking Machines, where the company more or less has to become the next SpaceX for the venture math to work at all.

He is candid that the strategy has a history. Steve Case's Rise of the Rest effort worked the same territory a decade ago, and Selby, who calls Case a friend, says the missing piece was connectivity: operating on an island in a non-coastal city without a bridge back to Silicon Valley made success nearly impossible. He also pitches local institutions on self-interest, asking a Cleveland investor what share of Case Western's graduating class more local venture capital could keep in Cleveland. The number, he concedes, cannot be proven in advance, but it is certainly not zero.

Weisburd then turns to the state of the industry, where Selby has just spent six months on the road meeting limited partners. His distillation has three parts. The industry has done a poor job of returning capital to investors over the last decade. Eight mega firms raised, he says, an aggregate of about 70 billion dollars earlier this year. And whatever capital remains warmly disposed to private technology is infatuated with shiny late-stage AI deals. That, he says, is the entire landscape.

The zombies get the sharpest treatment. After the financial crisis, thirteen years of zero interest rates pushed far too much money into a tiny asset class, spawning a generation of mediocre managers. Many rode the 2021 peak, crystallized no gains, watched the paper gains evaporate, and have since relabeled their portfolios as AI companies in the hope of one liquidity Hail Mary. Because venture funds are ten-year vehicles, the Darwinian culling takes forever: Selby admits he had thought the process was in its fifth or sixth inning and now believes it may be in the second. He is unsentimental about why they cling on. A failed banker can cascade down to another bank. A failed venture capitalist is done, with kids in private school and a mortgage, so holding on is rational even when the firm is walking dead.

Asked about the famous persistence of top-quartile venture performance, Selby sidesteps into structure. As mega funds grow, their minimum tickets grow too, and somebody has to write the checks under twenty million dollars. He argues the mega funds are effectively a different asset class, and that his own model, minority stakes in real businesses in middle-market geographies, with what he calls synthetic control, looks more like middle-market private equity than like classic Sand Hill Road venture.

On the AI boom itself he does not hedge. Unlike the dot-com era, this cycle is enormously capital intensive, in energy, chips and data centers, and the storytelling required to fuel the fundraising is off the charts. When it corrects, he says, tens if not hundreds of billions of dollars will be zeroed out, and he calls anyone who does not believe there is an AI bubble the true contrarian. Pressed on Anthropic, reportedly being financed at a two trillion dollar valuation in private rounds, he says flatly that you cannot underwrite that. It is a trading market, not an investing one, and he is an investor: he needs to get out a pencil and make the assumptions work.

Yet he is not an AI sceptic in his own portfolio. AI shows up as an ingredient in nearly every company he looks at, even in his geographies, and he expects it to fade as a separate topic the way the internet did after 2001. His version of the opportunity is almost the inverse of the coastal pitch: AI lets smaller teams build valuable companies more cheaply and reach good outcomes faster, which suits a fund that needs 500 million dollar outcomes rather than trillion dollar ones. His ideal founder, he adds, is often someone who went to the Bay Area young, absorbed its grandiosity and work ethic, and then moved home.

Two shorter segments round out the conversation. On macro, his old Clarium territory, Selby says volatility is back after years of central-bank tea-leaf reading, and the opportunity set for macro trading is better than it has been in a long time, though not easier. On the giant multi-manager pod shops, he sees a purpose but also adverse selection: a genuinely world-class trader should eventually spin out and run their own fund. And his one regret, offered without much drama, is that he has never been able to play momentum markets. The fake markups and hot-potato growth rounds of recent years made other people a great deal of money, and his refusal to join in probably left money on the table. He is fine with it, he says. It is just annoying.

Open questions

  1. Selby concedes the strongest counterargument himself: nobody has yet proven the non-coastal model with top-decile returns. What would Copper Sky's second fund need to show, and by when, for the thesis to count as proven rather than merely plausible?
  2. He predicts the AI correction will zero out tens to hundreds of billions of dollars, and that the zombie fund culling is only in its second inning. When the correction comes, does it speed the culling up, or does cheap money return fast enough to keep the walking dead alive again?
  3. He draws the coastal line at frontier research labs, conceding Anthropic and OpenAI to Palo Alto while claiming almost everything else for the interior. As AI tooling keeps lowering the team size needed to build a serious company, does that line move toward the labs, or away from them?
