MTS · Marius Hobbhahn of Apollo Research on takeover timelines and embedded evaluation

AI Takeover Could Happen by 2027 | Marius Hobbhahn

On MTS, "AI Takeover Could Happen by 2027 | Marius Hobbhahn" is a conversation with Marius Hobbhahn, CEO and co-founder of Apollo Research, the AI evaluation and alignment organization. Hobbhahn came to talk about Apollo's new embedded evaluation pilot with OpenAI, and about why his own view of takeover risk has darkened over the past two years: a rapid AI takeover in 2027, which he once called very unlikely, is now something he is no longer sure will not happen.

The pilot puts Apollo researchers inside OpenAI's development process to test four claims about loss of control. The first asks whether a model could subvert its own training process, for instance by interfering with how it is rewarded during reinforcement learning. The second asks whether today's evaluations would actually catch a scheming model. The third looks backward, checking whether models have already tried anything of the kind in past real deployments, such as researchers using Codex. The fourth covers monitorability: whether the chain of thought is still good enough for watchers to catch a model misbehaving.

Hobbhahn's threat model has shifted. He used to think catastrophe would require a model shaped, through instrumental convergence, into a relentless, power-seeking schemer, followed by recursive self-improvement too fast for human oversight. The Hugging Face incident changed his mind about a second path: a model pursuing a goal relentlessly within a single episode. That path matters more as models run for months at a time in large swarms and teams, where a single episode can be long and complicated.

Theo raises Paul Christiano's recent Dwarkesh podcast appearance: roughly a one in three chance of superintelligence within about a year, and a substantial risk of full AI takeover within six to twelve months. Hobbhahn calls that plausible. Christiano historically had slower timelines than his; now they are roughly aligned. Under such short timelines, Hobbhahn argues, takeover or some version of catastrophe is quite plausible simply because events would move too fast for humans to correct course. He stresses it is still less likely than not for next year, but two years ago he would have called a 2027 takeover very, very unlikely, and he describes the last two years as a negative update.

Beyond one pilot, Hobbhahn wants a professional field of embedded evaluators, comparable to nuclear, food, or cyber inspection. Such a field needs deep access: evaluators who can speak freely with employees, examine existing deployments, and find problems even if a developer were trying to hide something. Fields like that historically took decades to mature; this one has to be bootstrapped in months, and he says the world is nowhere near that standard yet.

Apollo has been public that the pilot's access and disclosure rights do not yet meet its bar for full embedded evaluations. OpenAI gives Apollo a whitelist of employees it may talk to; Apollo cannot approach others about anything in the assessment's scope. Hobbhahn understands the lab's wish to keep control, but notes that scientific progress often comes from informal conversations, the things heard over coffee or lunch, and in the long run evaluators need to talk to employees directly.

He does not expect embedded evaluation to change lab culture much, since only a handful of people interact with the evaluators. But he does think it can demystify the labs for everyone else. A trusted outside voice can say which public criticisms of a lab are overblown and which quiet issues deserve more concern, cutting through some of the confusion around frontier labs.

Open questions

  1. Apollo's pilot tests whether evaluations would catch a scheming model, but the evaluation field itself is only now being invented. What track record should an inspector need before a clean bill of health from one carries real weight?
  2. If a takeover-scale event is plausible within a year or two, can an inspection profession that historically takes decades to mature be built fast enough to matter, or does that timeline imply a different kind of safeguard altogether?
  3. OpenAI's whitelist limits whom Apollo may talk to. Will any frontier lab volunteer the unrestricted employee access Hobbhahn describes, and what happens to the pilot's credibility if none does?

MTS · Joshua Achiam on AI solving math and physics, and superintelligence timelines

All Math and Physics Mysteries Will Fall Soon | Joshua Achiam

On MTS, "All Math and Physics Mysteries Will Fall Soon | Joshua Achiam" is a conversation with Joshua Achiam, who spent nine years at OpenAI, most recently as its chief futurist, and is now an independent researcher and a fellow at the Institute for Progress. Hosts Theo Jaffee and Sofia Puccini talk with him about the news, announced the day before, that Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge had solved the Riemann hypothesis with AI, and about what that signals for mathematics, physics, and the timelines for superintelligence.

Achiam's reaction is mixed. He calls the result an incredible achievement and a huge credit to the mathematicians and researchers who advanced AI to make it possible. At the same time, he describes a strange feeling: the mountains that people expected to spend careers climbing are now ones where we can already see the view, because AI got there first. He expects many more moments like this, where an extraordinary triumph that people assumed would be built by human hands is achieved by throwing massive amounts of compute at the problem.

Theo offers the caution. Many people, he says, are over-updating, treating the Riemann result as the marker of extremely impressive AI and concluding that everything else must now fall in quick succession. That does not necessarily follow: the hypothesis may simply have been easier than believed for this kind of mind, while other problems remain very difficult for a long time. Achiam grants the possibility that humanity has been wildly miscalibrated about how hard its problems are, and that machines will reveal their true underlying hardness. But he lands in the other camp: most of the mathematical problems previously understood as hard now have a solution path, and the mysteries of mathematical theory and of physics will probably be revealed in relatively short order.

Revealed is not the same as understood, or acted on. Achiam notes that the human community of experts may take a long time to digest these advances, and that even a grand unified theory of everything arriving tomorrow would not produce radical technology the following day, because knowledge has to be internalized before it can be used. His worry is what piles up in the meantime: latent consequences that nobody has priced in, in a world where what is strategically optimal, in building technology or in projecting force, could change overnight, disrupting equilibria before people realize the equilibria were disruptible at all.

On superintelligence timelines, Achiam shifts the question from capability to cost. The pieces that feel salient to him are sample efficiency and the cost of inference needed to produce an extraordinary result. Breakthroughs in machine learning could let a future system match the Riemann-level result with a fraction of today's compute, data, and dollars, and he finds it reasonable that within two to three years the cost of deriving astonishing technological innovations falls to roughly a tenth of today's. That world would move much faster, but it would not be a category error away from the world we already live in. He is also skeptical that superintelligence solves the hardest problems, which are coordination problems: societies already know many things that would help in principle, and fail to act because ideas need champions and chains of trust, and a world where every agenda runs its own superintelligence-powered persuasion campaign has no guarantee of settling anywhere sensible.

The conversation then turns to the AI guardrails deal that President Trump announced with Xi Jinping. Achiam wants to see the text of any deal, but he expects genuine common ground, because China's leadership prizes domestic stability, and every government would agree that consumer AI systems should not distribute knowledge for building breakthrough weapons. The gap he worries about is conceptual: the risk of recursive self-improvement leading to dangerous misalignment is taken very seriously on the US side and is not yet part of the mainstream of thinking in China, and Washington policymakers are themselves perhaps a year or two behind in internalizing AI's consequences. He is not in the camp that expects everyone to die within a year or two, and he is hopeful about the policy response a year or two out, but he fears the shared understanding may form only after the impacts it was meant to govern have begun.

His most distinctive worry is cyber disruption. Achiam says we are plausibly already seeing the consequences of AI-enabled cyberattacks: two weeks of unprecedented simultaneous outages across many fronts, North Korea's five or six billion dollar heist from Binance, and the conflicts in Ukraine and potentially over Taiwan. He compares the mood to the early COVID period and judges it plausible that within the next couple of years rolling outages of consumer internet services become normal, something societies should prepare for with backup procedures, protected records, and continuity plans for commerce and supply chains. Markets are not pricing any of this in, he argues, because markets do a terrible job pricing exponentials; the recent spike in the VIX came from AI stocks falling, the opposite of what pricing in a cyber catastrophe would look like.

On China and chip export controls, Achiam holds two ideas at once. Maintaining American strategic dominance is, for him, not a point to compromise on, and there should be export controls restricting chips. But he wants those decisions made from careful models and simulations of how China perceives its interests, not from what he sees as short-sighted, consequence-indifferent hawkish advocacy. He frames the choice as setting an optimal compute gap: if the US lead grows so large that all scientific and technical progress runs through American compute, China comes under pressure to act preventively, and the conflict may not stop at attacks on AI infrastructure of the kind described by the mutually assured AI malfunction theory. How much to restrict, he concludes, is a question for very careful strategic consideration.

Open questions

  1. If most problems once considered hard in math and physics now have a solution path, how can anyone tell which remaining problems are genuinely hard, rather than misjudged in the way the Riemann hypothesis may have been?
  2. Achiam expects the cost of breakthrough-level results to fall to about a tenth of today's within two to three years. When the same cost collapse is available to every lab and every state at once, does it compress the leader's advantage or widen it?
  3. If rolling outages of consumer internet services become normal within the next couple of years, which services and supply chains should be required to keep working offline, and who pays for that redundancy before the first big failure rather than after it?

Newsthink · Cindy Pom on the intern photo behind Scale AI, Cognition, Decagon, and Hyperliquid

How a Group of Interns Became Billionaires

On Newsthink, "How a Group of Interns Became Billionaires" is a narrated explainer by Cindy Pom built around a single photograph: a group of interns at a New York trading firm in January 2016. Four of the people in that photo went on to build billion-dollar businesses. Alexandr Wang co-founded Scale AI and now leads Meta's superintelligence effort. Scott Wu co-founded Cognition, the company behind the AI coding agent Devin. Jesse Zhang co-founded Decagon, which builds AI agents for customer support. And Jeff Yan co-founded the crypto trading platform Hyperliquid.

The four did not meet at the internship. They had known each other for years through math and science competitions. Wu was a middle schooler at MATHCOUNTS, buzzing in before the host finished reading the question, and he is still salty that in second grade he entered a seventh-grade math competition and did not place; the next year, in third grade, he entered the ninth-grade division and won. Wu and Wang met through MATHCOUNTS in sixth grade and kept in touch on GChat and a math forum despite living in different states. Wu met Yan at a summer program for Olympiad students, and the two ended up in the same Harvard residence, playing chess, Go, and poker and talking for hours about what it took to be the best at something. Yan, who practiced jazz piano by repeating melodies until they were perfect, won gold at the International Physics Olympiad in 2013.

The internship was at Hudson River Trading, a quantitative trading firm that makes split-second trades using mathematical models, so a group of teenagers who had spent years solving hard math and coding problems was a natural fit. The three-week internship was run as a competition, and Wu and Yan took the top two spots in every round. Pom recounts a later conversation with the firm's founder, who was starting to make private investments and was told, in effect, to just invest in the interns and give all of them seed checks. Another member of that wider circle, Vickie Ye, pictured with Yan's US physics team, went on to become a researcher at Anthropic.

About a year after the internship, Wang and Wu were having lunch in San Francisco, and Wang wondered whether their old competition friends could become the next PayPal Mafia. Wu replied that it was a crazy idea. Wang answered that it was not too crazy, because it had been done before. Wu remembers Wang as the first in their circle to push the idea seriously: people say they want to find the next PayPal Mafia, so why should it not be them? The original group, made famous by a Fortune magazine photo, went on from PayPal to build companies like YouTube, Yelp, and LinkedIn, and kept opening doors for each other with introductions and backing.

Wang moved first. The year the internship ended, he dropped out of MIT and co-founded Scale AI with Lucy Guo, organizing and labeling the data that AI systems learn from, down to details like whether a pedestrian is about to cross the street. He became the world's youngest self-made billionaire at 25. In 2025, Meta invested 14.3 billion dollars for a 49 percent stake in Scale AI, and Wang joined Meta to lead its superintelligence effort, including, as the video puts it, launching its personal AI assistant Muse.

The network kept compounding. One of Scale's early engineers was Steven Hao, who had represented the United States at the International Olympiad in Informatics, pictured beside Scott Wu; the teammate on his other side, Johnny Ho, went on to co-found Perplexity. Ten years after that photo, Hao and Wu co-founded Cognition. Wu had been thinking about an AI lab for some time, and when OpenAI fired Sam Altman in November 2023 he moved quickly: Wu, Hao, and Walden Yan flew to the Bay Area, messaged people they knew in AI, and spent Thanksgiving coding in a rented house. That was how Cognition, and Devin, started.

Zhang was part of that story too. He had met Wu on the math competition circuit, and he invested in Cognition while building Decagon himself, the kind of informal help the group trades constantly, alongside card games and hanging out at the same stage of life. Decagon's other co-founder, Ashwin Sreenivas, had previously built a computer vision startup called Helia, which Scale AI acquired in 2020.

Yan's path ran through crypto, and it started during the internship itself, when two former partners came and pitched Bitcoin to the interns. Later, working at Hudson River Trading full-time, Yan read Ethereum's yellow paper, the technical document laying out how the platform worked, and something clicked: he felt he could build something that would revolutionize finance. He left and, with his Harvard roommate Brian Wong, launched Deaux, a crypto prediction market where anyone could bet on real-world outcomes. It launched in the middle of the 2018 crypto bear market and struggled: about a hundred people tried it and only five used it regularly, Wu among them, before Yan shut it down.

Then, in November 2022, FTX collapsed, and billions in customer deposits vanished from a centralized exchange that people had trusted like a bank. That failure motivated Yan to attack the problem himself. He built Hyperliquid, a decentralized exchange where users keep control of their own money, with a small team of ten people and no venture capital at all. It is now among the top ten cryptocurrencies.

A decade after the internship, Wang posted a reunion photo with Yan and Wu. The prediction he had made at 19 had come true, and he wrote that he had known the competition kids were geniuses by any standard and felt confident they could take the tech world by storm, adding a note to parents that a kid on GChat all day solving math problems with an anonymous group of cross-country kids may just turn out alright. Scale AI had reached a billion-dollar valuation in 2019, just three years after it was founded, and the round that took it past a billion was led by Founders Fund, which was started by Peter Thiel, an original member of the PayPal Mafia.

Open questions

  1. This group's pipeline ran from childhood math competitions to one trading firm's internship class. Could a pipeline like that be built deliberately today, or did it depend on a particular era of contests, forums, and chat rooms that no longer exists in the same form?
  2. Wang named the ambition out loud at 19, and the friends really did invest in, hire, and acquire from each other. How much of the outcome was the network deliberately acting as a network, and how much was four unusually talented people succeeding in parallel?
  3. Hyperliquid reached the top tier of crypto with ten people and no venture capital, while Scale AI took the opposite path to a Meta deal. As AI lowers the cost of starting a company, which of those two models does the next cohort of founders follow?

20VC with Harry Stebbings · Kush Bavaria of Ornn on neo-clouds and the state of compute

Why Neo-Clouds are Great Businesses & The State of Compute with Kush Bavaria, Co-Founder @ Ornn

On 20VC with Harry Stebbings, "Why Neo-Clouds are Great Businesses & The State of Compute with Kush Bavaria, Co-Founder @ Ornn" is a conversation with Kush Bavaria, the 23-year-old co-founder and chief executive of Ornn, a company building price indices and futures for GPU compute. Bavaria argues that compute is becoming a commodity market like oil, that the infrastructure investors financing it are closer to landlords than to gamblers, and that the prices everyone assumes will fall are, in his data, still rising.

His central analogy is real estate. Data centers are apartment buildings, infrastructure investors are the landlords who own them, and the inference providers and AI labs are tenants renting units. Landlords keep collecting rent as long as tenants want to live in the building, so Bavaria doubts infrastructure investors ever really lose. The risk sits one level down: if enterprise demand cracks, because large companies conclude that AI is not yet changing their business, the tenants cannot pay, and the pain trickles down from providers to the people who financed the chips.

On supply, he says the market is severely undersupplied. A buyer going to CoreWeave today would be quoted delivery around the fourth quarter of 2027 or the first quarter of 2028, and building your own capacity takes roughly half a year once chip delivery and installation are counted. Chip prices have risen every day for weeks, he says, and the forward curve has flipped: buyers once assumed GPU hours would be cheaper three to five years out and gave away free renewal options at low prices, while now nobody gives those options away, because the market suspects future prices will be higher than today's. He would not be surprised if Nvidia's Rubin generation rents for 20 to 30 dollars per GPU hour three years out, against roughly seven or eight dollars today on five-year deals.

That is why he is puzzled by the skepticism aimed at neo-clouds such as CoreWeave and Nebius, whose debt trades at punishing yields. The bears, he argues, are really making a credit call on the tenants: the neo-clouds are leveraged on the belief that chip values and AI demand hold up, and the open question is whether their AI-company tenants can keep paying rent. His own answer is clear. He is not permitted to invest publicly, but if he could, he would go very long both companies. He also concedes the neo-clouds have no real moat, which is why they are all expanding into post-training and tokens, and he does not see that as a contradiction: owning the infrastructure is the moat, and being the landlord is a great business even without one.

The bear case, as he frames it, is that enterprise AI adoption stalls. Studies from consulting firms, including his former employer BCG, suggest AI has not yet been very useful inside most large companies. If the Fortune 500 decide AI does not change their businesses, the chips they overbought lose their purpose, tenants default, and creditors are left holding hardware worth closer to the value of its metals than to the price on the loan. His advice to founders follows from the same logic: compute has to be bought before demand is known, so buyers should extend their horizons, from the 18 to 20 weeks ahead that Modal's Eric describes toward 22 to 26 weeks, and hedge the price risk in between. Companies with cash should simply buy chips and build, because prices will keep rising, and any excess capacity can be rented out at high prices, which is effectively what Elon Musk does when he sells spare SpaceX capacity to Anthropic.

On depreciation, Bavaria says the market has been wrong in the reassuring direction. A100s are around six years old and H100s around four, and both are still being rented on multi-year terms, because older chips will serve the long tail of inference rather than frontier training. He expects inference to split into fast, slow and specialized categories, biological and video inference among them, with perhaps a hundred specialized providers surviving alongside one horizontal provider of everyday inference, rather than a single winner taking the whole market.

The case for Ornn is that a market this fragmented needs market infrastructure. CPU computing had three suppliers, AWS, Google Cloud and Azure, while GPU computing has roughly 300 neo-clouds and far more buyers, all trading essentially the same good with different parameters. For compute to trade like oil, one specific grade of it has to be standardized and tracked as the reference price, the way Brent crude, a small fraction of global oil volume, anchors oil pricing. Ornn started with the index, but to get trustworthy data it had to run a physical exchange itself, financing chips that other investors own and Ornn controls and monetizes, because no neo-cloud would hand over its transaction data. The ambition he names is to become the S&P of compute, later adding data products and debt ratings for data centers, in a market he believes will be larger than oil has ever been.

The first big traders of compute futures, he predicts, will be the people already exposed to compute prices: lenders whose loans depend on tenants surviving and on residual chip values three to five years out, the same hedging problem farmers solved with grain futures a century ago. The index itself will push the industry toward standardized clusters, which helps lenders, because a standardized cluster can be re-let the moment one tenant defaults. Seeding that market has a cost he is willing to pay: Ornn sometimes has to refuse enormous orders from the likes of Modal and Fireworks so that smaller startups keep access and the market stays liquid. He also argues inference, not training, already sets the forward price, because inference demand is real and paid for on the other side, while training is speculative in the way drug development is speculative.

The mispriced risk in the market today, in his view, is tenant diligence. Neo-clouds are handing compute to young labs without examining whether those companies can pay, and when some of them fail, as startups do, the lenders will be exposed with no hedge in place. Asked whether he would rather have more compute or better talent, he chooses compute without hesitating: the models are good enough, or will be within months, so compute is the binding constraint. Enterprises, he expects, will adjust more slowly than people think, while the world underneath them changes faster.

The founder material is woven through the same themes. Late-cycle AI investing is fear-driven, he says, by investors who missed OpenAI and Anthropic and refuse to miss the next one, and the easy application categories are already taken by companies such as Rogo in finance and Harvey in legal. The underheated area he would invest in is the energy and physical AI stack, down to better transformers for data centers, citing Crusoe as the model. On fundraising, he repeatedly chose partners over brands and price: a boutique firm over a large fund at the same terms, Thrive because of Miles, Andreessen Horowitz after Wayne pushed Chris Dixon's team to move within a week, and he turned away term sheets valuing Ornn at 1.5 to 2 billion dollars to keep the company's multiples disciplined. His team is built from former college athletes, hired for communication and competitiveness, and tested in deliberately uncomfortable interview dinners.

In the quickfire round, he says he would buy OpenAI over Anthropic at comparable valuations for one reason: OpenAI has more compute, and Anthropic will be bottlenecked because no amount of cash can make TSMC's fabrication lines move faster. He would put Travis Kalanick on his board, and would put all his personal money into Factory, the coding harness company whose product he switched back to after a spell with Cognition. His most speculative beliefs are about longevity: that humans will become biological vessels for AI and that lifespans could reach 150 to 200 years. Asked which of his 2030 beliefs is most likely to be wrong, he names those two himself.

Open questions

  1. Bavaria's landlord thesis says infrastructure investors win as long as tenants can pay. If enterprise AI adoption stalls the way the consulting studies he cites suggest it might, who is actually holding the risk: the neo-clouds, their lenders, or the landlords he says cannot lose?
  2. Ornn wants to be both the exchange where compute trades and the index provider whose price everyone trusts. Can one company hold both roles and keep the reference price credible, the way Brent is credible for oil, once real money is trading against it?
  3. He argues inference demand, being real rather than speculative, already sets the forward price of compute. If that is right, are the neo-clouds and their lenders wrong to keep underwriting their businesses mainly on training demand?

iConnections · Scott Wu of Cognition on Devin, from survival mode to creative mode

Scott Wu: From Survival Mode to Creative Mode | TomorrowX Summit

On iConnections, "Scott Wu: From Survival Mode to Creative Mode | TomorrowX Summit" is a conversation with Scott Wu, the chief executive of Cognition, talking with Gavin Baker of Atreides and Karin Fronczke of Fidelity Investments on the Arena Stage at the TomorrowX Summit in Austin. Wu explains how Cognition built Devin, the AI software engineer, why it works like a teammate rather than a tool, and how the company went from its first dollar of revenue to a billion dollar run rate in under two years.

The newest product is Devin bots, released the day before the talk. These are Devins you prompt with outcomes, projects, or goals rather than individual tasks, and they own entire pieces of work end to end. Wu frames the point with a small piece of arithmetic: when a coding agent already does 98 percent of your work, moving it to 99 percent does not add one percent. It doubles what you can get done, because your own remaining share of the work is cut in half.

His favorite internal example is Cognition's own Android app. A single Devin bot was told to clone the entire iOS app, keep it in sync whenever the iOS app changes, test all the code itself, and ship the builds. It did the whole thing with no human touching the code, and it still maintains the app today. That is possible, Wu says, because Devin is a cloud based agent with its own machine: it runs and tests everything from scratch and sends back video screen captures showing the shipped workflow working.

Devin's difference from local coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex is autonomy. Cognition started in 2024, when most people still thought of AI as a question and answer chatbot, betting instead on a teammate that has its own machine, makes decisions, and talks with you in Slack. Devin still sits at the most autonomous end of the spectrum: it can read your Datadog logs, run the server itself, and test its own code, which is why Wu calls it an AI software engineer rather than an AI software tool.

The customer list runs from Goldman Sachs, Lowe's, Nvidia, and Verizon down to startups like Decagon, Fireworks, and Harvey. Wu singles out Citi, whose chief executive Jane Fraser told a recent earnings call that the bank expects to save about a billion and a half dollars in 2026 alone from Devin. The work is unglamorous: version upgrades, modernizations, and migrations of decades old software, much of it once handed to outsourcing and contracting firms and now done in house. Customers who measure the return, he says, typically report getting back six to fifteen times what they spend.

Landing regulated enterprises, Karin Fronczke's question, has no single trick. Wu describes a long list of small things: a product tightened up enough to pass security reviews, the right guardrails and permissioning, clear measurement of the return, and working on an outcomes basis, taking the thing an organization most wants done in twelve months and doing it in three. Cognition also runs a heavy forward deployed motion, embedding its people with customers, and keeps offices in San Francisco, Austin, New York, London, Sao Paulo, Singapore, Tokyo, and Dubai, because that is where the engineers of the world are.

The growth numbers are stark. Cognition took its first dollar of revenue on September 20, 2024, and crossed a billion dollar run rate on September 16, 2026. Around the end of September 2026 the company passed a price cut of about 35 percent for Devin Cloud on to every customer, made possible by its own models, better harnesses, and lower prices from the labs. Revenue dipped, then climbed back above where it had been in September, the Jevons paradox doing its work. Wu expects to end January, the close of Cognition's year, at a run rate of about 1.6 billion dollars.

Asked about fears that the industry is overbuilding compute and heading for a surplus in 2027, Wu laughs. Most organizations, he says, are not even ten percent of the way into using the AI that already exists. He points to AI solving problems such as Navier-Stokes and Yang-Mills theory, work that consumed tens of millions of hours of human effort and now takes days, and argues that drug discovery, self driving cars, security, and software itself offer far more useful work than the world has compute to do. At some point in the talk he also squares 258 in his head, live on stage, and gets 66,564.

Cognition also trains its own models, the SWE 2 series, built on Thinking Machines' Inkling model with heavy mid training and post training on the company's own reinforcement learning environments, which encode what a good pull request looks like: the simplest fix that solves the problem, not an over scoped rewrite. Wu's picture of the future is an ensemble. The strongest frontier models handle the hardest security vulnerability or architecture decision, while Cognition's own cheaper models write the routine boilerplate. About 30 to 40 percent of the company's tokens already run through its own models, and serving those tokens costs roughly a fifth to a tenth of the frontier price.

Security is where the talk turns sober. Cognition recently acquired Corridor and launched a Devin Security swarm, which on real world vulnerability benchmarks scored as high as or slightly above Anthropic's Mythos at 30 to 40 percent lower cost, because an ensemble catches things any single model misses. Attackers, Wu warns, are already running full AI agent swarms that probe every surface and every endpoint at once, and he expects more incidents like the recent Azure and Outlook outages in the next few months. You cannot fight AI agents with human response times, he says. Every government, bank, and health insurer needs its own AI defense force, automatically monitoring its logs and inspecting every change. In the aggressive case, he believes spending on cyber could pass spending on the rest of software by the end of 2027.

On jobs, Wu returns to the Jevons paradox. In the year 2000 there were fewer than a million software engineers in the world; today there are about 35 million, even though the tools have improved beyond recognition, because cheaper software means more software gets built. AI, he argues, will create more software engineers, not fewer. He is also a happy Cerebras customer, praising the speed its chips give specialized workloads, while noting Cognition cannot get its hands on enough compute of any kind.

The close gives the talk its title. Bringing the world from survival mode to creative mode is a line from one of his co-founders, and a Minecraft reference: what humans enjoy is creating and imagining things that did not exist before, and the grunt work underneath is what agents can take away. Three years out, Wu sees personal agents absorbing everyday drudgery like shopping and home upkeep, and, maybe bigger, organizations that actually run well. Imagine, he says, going to the DMV and it works.

  2. Can Cognition really end January at a run rate near 1.6 billion dollars after cutting Devin Cloud prices by about a third, and how much further can its own models push its costs down?
  3. Will organizations build the AI defense forces Wu calls for before agent swarm attacks force the issue, and could security spending really pass all other software spending by the end of 2027?

iConnections · Lisa Su of AMD on Nvidia, China, and the American AI stack

Lisa Su on Nvidia, China and the American AI Stack | TomorrowX Summit

On iConnections, "Lisa Su on Nvidia, China and the American AI Stack | TomorrowX Summit" is a stage conversation at the TomorrowX Summit in Austin between Lisa Su, the chair and chief executive of AMD, and Dom Rizzo of T. Rowe Price. Rizzo first covered AMD when it was a roughly five billion dollar company trading near five dollars a share, and he walks Su through the bets that remade it, the returns on the AI infrastructure build-out, competition with Nvidia, export controls and China, and the CPU comeback being driven by AI agents.

Su traces AMD's rise to a decision to build for high performance computing, the hardest and most demanding part of the semiconductor market. As Moore's law slowed, AMD bet on chiplets, breaking the largest processors into smaller pieces that could be combined flexibly, at a time when few outside the company understood the idea. A decade later, that bet sits underneath a company that has become data center first. Data center was less than half of AMD's business in 2025, finishes this year at more than 60 percent, and is headed to more than 80 percent of the company, against what Su calls a two trillion dollar market opportunity over the next five years.

Asked whether the industry is in an AI bubble and overinvesting, Su answers that AI is the most transformative technology of the last fifty years, and that it makes sense to lean in now. The winners, she argues, will be the companies that saw the trend and built the balance sheet and capability to invest, and these are three to five year investments, not quarterly ones. The real discipline is making sure every part of the supply chain invests at the right level, because being limited by underinvestment is as much a failure as overinvesting.

She also rejects the semiconductor industry's usual cycle story. In a normal cycle, tight supply leads to over-ordering and then a digestion period. This time, she says, AMD is not seeing something that looks cyclical; it is seeing something that looks generational. Because AI makes companies more productive, and productive companies invest to build better products, the growth feeds itself rather than exhausting itself.

On Nvidia, Su is complimentary and unbothered. Nvidia is a phenomenal company, she says, and Jensen Huang has been a tremendous leader for the industry. AMD's job amid the headlines is to separate itself from the noise, which will work itself out in time, and to promise and then exceed expectations on delivery. She adds that the industry has to do more to show ordinary Americans how the build-out benefits them, and she welcomes the public argument over data center concerns as a chance for that dialogue.

On China and export controls, Su accepts the premise that the highest performing chips will be restricted, and says AMD wants to be in the room with the administration and Congress as the rules are written, because semiconductors now sit at the center of both economic prosperity and national security. She says AMD anticipated much of the newest round of restrictions, and that even with them, demand will remain substantially higher than supply. The strategic goal is that the world builds on the American AI stack, and she notes that the Commerce Department recently approved a good number of AMD's export packages.

The software story is where she claims the ground is shifting fastest. Porting models between hardware architectures was long considered slow and painful, but frontier models have become very good at engineering work such as kernel optimization, and AMD has acquired around ten software companies to build out its ROCm stack. Anthropic, she recounts, got its model running on AMD over a single weekend, with one engineer working with Claude, the kind of bring-up that once took many months.

Underneath all of this is her portfolio argument: there is no single best kind of compute. Enterprise technology chiefs want choice across hardware, models, and price, so AMD intends to supply CPUs, GPUs, FPGAs, and custom silicon under one software stack, the right compute for the right workload. AI agents are now reviving the CPU side of that portfolio, because agents need processors to orchestrate their work. Su expects the server CPU market, long a twenty to thirty billion dollar market growing in single digits, to reach about 220 billion dollars within four to five years, and she points to the just launched Venice server processors. On the GPU side, the newest MI455 accelerators and Helios racks began shipping in the third quarter and are being brought up in the data centers of OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle.

She closes by folding the Xilinx acquisition, with its more than six thousand customers across aerospace, defense, and industrial markets, into the same all kinds of compute thesis, and by returning to the human stakes. Her hope, she says, is that within a couple of years every family has a story of how AI improved their lives, through scientific discovery, healthcare, and better outcomes.

  3. If AI tools keep collapsing the cost of moving models between hardware architectures, does software stop being the moat that protects the leading chip company, and what then decides who wins a given workload?
