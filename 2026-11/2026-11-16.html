Golden Gate Institute for AI · Jacob Tsimerman on what AI does to mathematics

Jacob Tsimerman on AI can supercharge mathematics - what is the end state? | The Curve 2026

On Golden Gate Institute for AI, “Jacob Tsimerman on AI can supercharge mathematics - what is the end state? | The Curve 2026” is a talk by Jacob Tsimerman, a research mathematician who has lately pivoted to AI safety, about what it feels like inside mathematics now that AI has, in his words, come for the field at full throttle. He opens with a joke about the previous speaker's poll on AI risk, and with a plug for a group he recently co-founded with fellow mathematicians and other theoreticians working on AI safety, which he calls more important than the talk itself. Then he turns to the state of his old profession.

His central claim is blunt: AI is here for mathematics, and it is now better than mathematicians at doing math. He has done math his whole life, and for basically any problem he wants to solve, the fastest route is to work with AI. It is not yet a replacement for him, he says, but it is better than him, and he is definitely better with it than without it.

The community has found this hard to absorb, because for many mathematicians the subject is closer to a spiritual or artistic pursuit than a job. Doing math is what you are, not just what you do, and people find meaning in it in their spare time. The field was also slow to see the change coming. He recounts a long period of goalpost shifting after ChatGPT: the models could speak but could not do math, since there was supposedly no training data, then they could do math but not hard math, and so on.

What finally made people take notice, he says, is that AI has solved Navier-Stokes, one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, the small set of problems mathematicians enshrined at the start of the millennium as the most important in the field. Only one of them had ever been solved before. Now, he says, major conjectures that would normally take years or decades to resolve are being resolved every day, and journals, conferences, and the archive are overwhelmed by the number of submissions. The field is not keeping up with the changes.

The reception is split. One camp holds that what is happening is simply not good: humans understand less even though more knowledge is out there, PhD training systems are breaking, and some people have gone so far as to sign letters saying mathematicians should not use AI for mathematics and companies should stop doing mathematics using AI. Tsimerman places himself in the other camp. This is ultimately a good thing for mathematics, he argues, but it requires a change of perspective. It is no longer true that a person can grow up to become a mathematician who proves a major conjecture and enshrines their name in the history books. That time has passed, which is a bit sad, but far more interesting math results are arriving, and mathematicians will learn how to dissect them, engage with them, and adapt. Between the camps sits a confused middle of PhD students and young faculty who trained their whole lives for work that is being upturned beneath them.

He is often asked whether a PhD still makes sense, and his answer is that it does not, at least not the way it used to be done. The old paradigm took very talented young students and made each of them a deep expert in one narrow area, inside an economy of specialists who knew other things. That no longer works, he says, because by the time a student finishes learning their narrow thing, AI will have moved the field generations ahead, and that particular skill set will be too narrow. At the very least, people should stop doing what they were doing in the way they used to do it.

He then describes his own path with the tools. Four years ago he was using AI as a curiosity. Two years ago it became good enough that he was learning subjects outside his expertise exclusively through AI systems. Six months ago he was doing research with the models, including an episode where he asked one to check a proof he had written down informally. The model insisted there was a mistake. He said there was not. The model was right. It could not fix the proof, but he could, and he calls that the kind of interaction people now go through every day.

A month ago, working on open source game theory research relevant to AI safety, he ran things very differently. He handed the models an idea and a paper he thought was relevant, had them spawn ten sub-agents to interact in a set way, and got results back by the morning: work he estimates would have taken him a month, done in a few days, a speedup he puts at five to ten times. The catch is oversight. He has to check carefully that the output is correct, that it is relevant, that it is comprehensible, and that it is actually what he asked for and not something else. He is not currently doing pure mathematics professionally, having pivoted to AI safety, but if he were, he says, his entire workflow would be upended: he would be programming AI harnesses all day, every day.

Looking ahead, he predicts that the current landscape of open problems and researchers developing theories around them will all be resolved within five years, with roughly five normal generations of mathematics occurring in that span at a far faster rate than before. AI will get good at explaining, digesting, and curating results, until there is effectively a math bot that does mathematics all the time and writes it up.

He also thinks science should revisit how ambitious it is. There is a joke that all physics is math, all chemistry is physics, and all biology is chemistry. That is not really a joke, he says, it is a fact of life. Each field works at its own level because the level below is too hard to deal with directly: physics is done as physics because the mathematical model is too hard to use. That might not be true anymore once we have systems sufficiently more intelligent than humans, and these are things we should be revisiting.

His last proposal, which he calls his wonkiest and most out there view, is that philosophy should be revisited too. We should be developing theories of love, theories of attention, and theories of value. Such proposals normally draw cynicism, he notes, because the people offering them are usually naive and have not grappled with the difficulties involved. But he thinks we all have to revisit our assumptions, and he closes on exactly that line.

Open questions

  1. Tsimerman says the time has passed when a human could grow up to prove a major conjecture and enter the history books. If AI systems produce the results, what does mathematical understanding consist of now, and who or what gets the credit?
  2. He argues the traditional PhD, deep training in one narrow specialty, no longer makes sense because AI moves the field too fast. What should replace it, and what happens to the students and young faculty caught in the middle of the change?
  3. His five year prediction assumes the flood of AI results can be checked, digested, and curated as fast as it arrives, yet he also says journals, conferences, and the archive are already overwhelmed. What institution, human or machine, verifies the new mathematics, and what happens to results nobody has verified?

Joe Maddalone · Joe Maddalone on senior engineers, AI, and the software factory

Senior Engineers Are Using AI Wrong and Software Factories Are Dumb

On Joe Maddalone, “Senior Engineers Are Using AI Wrong and Software Factories Are Dumb” is a video essay about the mental models engineers carry into the AI era. Maddalone opens with the computing history books on his shelf and a pattern he finds in them: there is always somebody who misses what is coming because they cannot see beyond what they already know.

His first example is Marvin Minsky, a pioneer of artificial intelligence featured in the book Genius Makers. Minsky was deeply skeptical of neural networks, mostly because he could not imagine that a machine could produce a new idea it was not explicitly taught first. Maddalone says Minsky pretty much single-handedly caused the AI winter, and calls it a great example of how even brilliant people struggle to imagine a future that does not fit their current mental model.

He contrasts two kinds of software books. Martin Fowler's Refactoring is a beloved text, but mostly a recipe book: pull it off the shelf, look up the technique, apply it, put it back. The Phoenix Project and The Pragmatic Programmer work differently, teaching the principles of software development through parables and metaphors about how to think, how to approach problems, and how to make better decisions.

Metaphors are the essay's hinge. We understand new technology by comparing it to the world we already know, which is why engine power is still measured in horsepower and why computers still pretend to be little desks, with documents, file cabinets, and a trash can. The universal save icon is a floppy disk that many adults today have never seen in person, yet they know what it means. The metaphor has outlived the thing it represents.

That inheritance has a cost. A metaphor shapes a mental model, the mental model shapes habits, and when the mental model is wrong, the habits are wrong. Maddalone compares old design decisions to training wheels: rely on them long enough and you may forget they are removable, and find yourself defending choices made in the 1970s and 1980s as though they were laws of physics.

His economic argument follows. Many respected software engineering practices were derived from the cost of developing, maintaining, and coordinating software written by human beings. AI is fundamentally changing those economics, which should mean some best practices stop making sense. Yet whenever he looks at a vibe-coded app, he sees the exact same best practices implemented, which he takes as a signal that engineers are not ready to let go of the old mental models, himself included.

The tradition he names is the developer as craftsman, the philosophy of The Pragmatic Programmer: hand-polishing every function, obsessing over clean code, adhering to rules like DRY, don't repeat yourself. That made sense when every character was typed by a human hand and every bug meant a human had to read that exact code. But if AI can refactor or discard 500 lines of boilerplate in seconds, the cost of duplication and the cost of abstraction both change, and spending three hours hand-polishing a class hierarchy is not craft anymore. It is nostalgia.

His replacement image is the urban planner. Engineers are not woodworkers now; a planner does not care about the grain of wood on a park bench, but about traffic flow and utility capacity. Planners set boundaries, enforce constraints, and direct growth. When a tool writes the code, an engineer's value is no longer clean syntax. It is the ability to express clear intent, evaluate tradeoffs, and guide growth, a role now being called design engineering. Anyone still hand carving wood, he says, is focused on a bench while the city around them burns.

He applies the same thinking to how code is organized. For decades we have pretended software lives in physical file cabinets, building directories and nested paths to support a spatial mental model in which understanding a system means knowing where things live. Large language models and vector databases do not care that a helper function is buried three levels deep in a folder called utils. They evaluate code by semantic context, what it means and how it relates to the problem, regardless of where it sits on disk. Engineers say they do not read the code anymore, yet spend energy getting AI to organize it around a crutch invented in 1985 to solve a human short-term memory problem. The field has not moved from the spatial model to the semantic one.

Then he turns to the narrative he expects to lose people over. The current discourse holds that AI tools are only useful if you are already a senior developer, because you need the background to catch the AI's mistakes and the classic patterns to keep the system on the rails. The loudest voices, he says, are senior engineers demonstrating how much faster they can execute the things they already know. He calls that a trap. A seasoned developer tends to use AI as a hyper-efficient autocomplete for their own mental models, writing the same design patterns and architectures they have written since 2008, only faster. They do not reimagine the work; they supercharge the baggage they bring to it.

The people who move the field into its next era, he argues, will be the ones without that baggage: people who do not know what a floppy disk is, who feel no attachment to DRY or traditional refactoring, who do not have to unlearn decades of dogma, and who are free to invent new ways of working with code from scratch.

The final trap is the software factory. For about twenty years the industry has tried to turn development into an assembly line, and it has half succeeded: CI/CD works, and testing, building, and deployment run like a factory floor. The dangerous leap is extending that model to the entire life cycle, an assembly line populated by AI agents trained on old mental models, where a product manager drops in a specification and a finished product rolls out the other side. That picture is attractive if you believe a developer's edge is speed of implementation. Maddalone says it is completely wrong: the modern developer's edge is taste, verification, and edge case evaluation.

He supports the point with an article by Rob West, published about a month earlier, arguing that the agented software factory is a wrong metaphor. As Maddalone paraphrases it: a factory assumes design is solved, but figuring out what to build and how it should behave is the work, and a ticket is not a blueprint. Building software changes our understanding of the specification as we go, so design engineering evolves the spec rather than consuming it. An assembly line exists to eliminate variation, while design engineering benefits from variation, because comparing alternatives is how better approaches are found. And a factory has to be fed, which pressures teams to formalize requirements before they understand the problem; the ambiguity does not disappear, and the agents fill the gaps with confident best guesses and produce the wrong thing.

His verdict is that the problem is not the technology. It is applying a 19th-century industrial mental model to a 21st-century cognitive shift. Just as Minsky could not see past his assumptions about how machines would learn, engineers risk using groundbreaking technology to do the wrong things faster. The classics got the field here and supplied the metaphors it needed for its first era, but hanging onto them now will leave engineers standing on an assembly line, producing artifacts for a world that has already moved on.

Open questions

  1. Maddalone argues that many best practices were really answers to the cost of human-written code, but he does not sort them. Which practices were only ever cost workarounds, and which ones protect something the cost argument never touched, such as correctness or security?
  2. He says the modern developer's edge is taste, verification, and edge case evaluation, and also that senior engineers mostly spend their AI leverage on old patterns. If neither the veterans nor the newcomers are practicing that edge, where does it get learned?
  3. He rejects the factory as the organizing metaphor for AI-built software but offers the urban planner mainly as an image for individual work. What replaces the factory at the level of a whole team or company, and who owns quality inside that model?

Joe Maddalone · Joe Maddalone on local AI hype and tribalism

Local AI is Overhyped and Tribalism is Ruining It

On Joe Maddalone, “Local AI is Overhyped and Tribalism is Ruining It” is an argument against picking teams in AI. Maddalone opens with Steve Jobs, who by the account in Walter Isaacson's biography was cruel, manipulative, and difficult, but was also the person who got things done. You do not have to like his personality, Maddalone says, to appreciate the products and technologies he brought into existence.

He applies the same test to Sam Altman, Dario Amodei, and Elon Musk. All three have made spectacularly questionable public statements, but their companies are producing the best AI products available today, and pretending otherwise would be silly. Those companies are not shy about their ambitions either: they want their products embedded in your workflows, your business, and eventually almost everything you do. That is not an indictment of the technology, he says, just something to be aware of before deciding how much of your profession to build on top of it. But if you cannot appreciate what these companies have built without becoming a cheerleader for their business model, that is tribalism.

The mirror image bothers him just as much. Maddalone has made a ton of videos about open source and local AI projects, Ollama, llama.cpp, open weights, and other tools, and he plans to keep making them, because working with them has taught him more about machine learning principles and how language models are actually trained than a glorified chatbot would. The people on those projects are thoughtful and intelligent. But the product experience from the open source tools is simply not keeping up with the big AI companies, and pretending otherwise would be just as silly. If you cannot appreciate the open source mission without becoming an apologist for an ecosystem that has yet to deliver on its promise, that is tribalism too. We owe those projects no more loyalty than we owe the big companies.

Tribalism would be harmless if it stayed online, but it creeps into real decisions. People spend more on hardware than is financially sensible because they have decided cloud inference is inherently bad. Others refuse to look at a local solution because they have decided cloud inference is inherently better, or refuse to use a model because it was made by a company or a country they do not like.

He does not think this happens by accident. Newcomers arrive to find new models every week, benchmarks they cannot possibly understand, and acronyms stacked on acronyms, so they ask what they should use, what they should buy, and whether it is worth it. An entire industry has popped up around that question and answers it with far more certainty than it has: this changes everything, cloud AI is a scam, local is the future, here is the hardware you need, each title paired with a thumbnail of a man with his mouth open. The most honest answer is that it depends, on your budget, your data, and your tolerance for tinkering. But it depends is the worst video title. Nuance does not get clicks. Certainty gets clicks, outrage definitely gets clicks, and tribalism gets clicks.

For some creators, tribalism is a business model. Convince viewers the other camp is the enemy and you have a loyal audience that returns every week to hear what it already thinks, plus affiliate links, sponsorships, paid courses, and communities you must subscribe to or be left behind. Not everyone with an affiliate link is lying, he says, but when someone asks you to spend thousands of dollars on hardware, a service, or a course, it is fair to ask who benefits. And guides have little incentive to make you competent, because confused people watch the next video and competent people may not.

Maddalone counts himself in. He is a person with a camera making videos about this exact topic, he is not exempt, and he admits his own bias: he spends most of his time on the open source side. So he would rather give viewers a way to check him than ask them to trust him. His filter for any creator, himself included: do they show you where the thing fails, or only a highlight reel? Do they ever tell you not to buy something? Do they disclose sponsorships and commissions? Have they ever changed their mind in public? Are they testing on work that looks like yours, or on a demo designed to look impressive? If the answer to all of those is no, you are not watching an evaluation. You are watching a pitch.

His alternative is to evaluate the thing in front of you rather than the tribe it belongs to. Technology does not care which side you are on; it either solves your problem or it does not. Is it good, is it useful, is it affordable, is it appropriate for the data you are dealing with? Those questions are more productive than asking whether something is local or cloud, American or Chinese, open or proprietary. Yet once people pick a side they are expected to defend everything attached to it: like OpenAI and you must defend Altman, like Anthropic and you must defend Dario, like open weights and you must pretend that running a 30 billion parameter model on your Mac beats the best cloud offering, use the cloud offering and you have sold your soul to a technology monopoly.

He closes where he started. You can appreciate something without endorsing everything associated with it, and criticize something without rejecting everything associated with it. You do not need to like Steve Jobs to appreciate what he built, you do not need to trust Sam Altman to appreciate what OpenAI has produced, and you do not need to pretend local AI is better than it is to appreciate the people behind it. You do not need to pick a team.

Open questions

  1. Maddalone says the honest answer to what should I use is it depends, on budget, data, and tolerance for tinkering. Can advice that conditional compete for attention against the certainty and outrage that get clicks, and if so, in what form?
  2. His checklist for spotting a pitch assumes viewers will apply it even to creators they like, including him. Does handing the audience a way to check the creator change the creator's incentives, or does the audience that enjoys tribal content simply drift to someone who never asks to be checked?
  3. He argues you can use a company's products without endorsing its ambitions, even when the company openly wants to become indispensable in your workflow. At what point does building your profession on a tool stop being neutral evaluation and start being dependence, and who is watching for that line?

MTS · Reed Albergotti on NVIDIA's shrinking chip moat

NVIDIA’s Chip Moat Is Shrinking Fast | Reed Albergotti

On MTS, "NVIDIA’s Chip Moat Is Shrinking Fast | Reed Albergotti" brings back Reed Albergotti, the technology editor at Semafor, in the middle of the biggest week in NVIDIA's history. NVIDIA's board has removed founder Jensen Huang as chief executive, and Albergotti, whose newsroom has been reporting the fall all week, works through how the company got here, who wins and who loses, and why he thinks the company's long run looks weaker than its short run.

He starts with a Semafor story published about an hour before the taping, reported with Garrison Lovely, Ashley Gold, and Rohan Goswami. It describes NVIDIA's general counsel leaning on Huang's influence with President Trump to push Commerce Department officials around, pressing to speed up the licensing regime that lets H200 chips be sold into China. Albergotti's verdict is that NVIDIA, like a lot of the tech industry in this Trump term, went too far. It acted as if the president's favor would last forever and misunderstood Washington, where the political winds always change. The midterms changed the balance of power, and the closeness is now coming back to bite the company.

Some of the wounds were self-inflicted. Nobody forced Huang into the affairs, he notes, including one involving a Chinese national whom the show names as Alice Wong. Asked what evidence exists that she was a Chinese government asset, Albergotti draws a careful line. The public evidence is that she is a Chinese national, and that Beijing holds potential leverage over any Chinese national with family in China, a category that runs from Silicon Valley engineers to Stanford PhD students. Against the stronger claim, that she was actually a spy working for the MSS, he says there is a lot of evidence, and nobody he knows is making that claim at this point. The danger was that she could potentially have been influenced.

On winners and losers, he splits the answer by horizon. In the short term, NVIDIA will be fine, and could even be more profitable, because founder chief executives like Huang routinely sacrifice near-term profit for moves aimed ten years out, and that pressure is now gone. In the long run, he says, he just cannot imagine the company being as innovative, at exactly the moment the chip industry is in flux. Companies like OpenAI now design their own AI chip in a matter of months, so chip design is no longer the moat, and navigating that shift will be tricky. His analogy is Apple after Steve Jobs: a company that has not really innovated in a long time, yet has been hugely profitable on the strength of the iPhone.

Why did the board move so fast, before its own investigation had concluded? Because the bleeding would not stop. Stories about other affairs kept surfacing, and classified information from inside the government was leaking out in a strategic drip. At some point the board had to act; removing Huang put a tourniquet on it, even if it has not totally stopped the bleeding. Some people speculate he could return one day. Albergotti does not know how realistic that is, but the removal seemed the right move in the short term.

Pressed for parallels with Sam Altman's firing in 2023, he says he first saw them and now mostly does not. The allegations against Altman were always vaguer, with no smoking gun to point to, more about how he interacted with people at the company and how he represented facts. The Huang case, by contrast, is pretty cut and dried, and much more difficult to get out of.

On export controls, his answer is yes: expect very strict bipartisan controls from Congress. That, in his telling, was the goal behind the whole fight. NVIDIA and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick were the two biggest blockers of export controls inside the administration, and both are now out of the way. He expects the controls to reach beyond exports themselves to the loophole that has let Chinese citizens and companies legally access data centers in places like Malaysia remotely. Closing that will be technically challenging, he concedes, but they are going to try.

Huang's other allies are exposed too. Lutnick, he says, is facing a huge crisis, with more still to come out about how he handled export controls at Commerce. David Sacks is in the crosshairs as well, and anyone who was too close to Trump is; Albergotti is interviewing Sacks at Semafor's Silicon Valley and the World conference the following week. Sacks, though, is not involved in the day-to-day of export controls, so his case may be a separate matter.

Succession looks brutal. The names floated are Rene Haas of Arm, and keeping Colette Kress of NVIDIA in the job permanently, and even those underline how thin the field is. Albergotti has spoken with someone involved in the succession effort: they believe there are six to eight people in the world who can do this job, and NVIDIA does not want just one of them. The company has no chief operating officer and wants to hire one alongside a chief executive. It had tried to recruit a number two before, and candidates of that caliber did not want to work under Huang, whose personality was too big for them. Some have already declined the operating chief role. It is going to be hugely challenging.

Open questions

  1. If customers like OpenAI can now design their own AI chips in a matter of months, what replaces chip design as NVIDIA's durable advantage, and can a post-Huang leadership team build it?
  2. Will the coming strict export controls actually close the loophole that lets Chinese companies remotely use data centers in third countries, or will enforcement founder on the technical difficulty Albergotti flags?

Behind the Balance Sheet · Benedict Evans on whether Anthropic can pay off

Will Anthropic Flop? Benedict Evans analyses its prospects

On Behind the Balance Sheet, “Will Anthropic Flop? Benedict Evans analyses its prospects” is host Steve Clapham's conversation with tech analyst Benedict Evans about Anthropic's IPO filing and the question hanging over the whole AI industry: can the model companies ever earn back the money being poured into them?

Evans starts with the filing itself, which he calls a Rorschach test. An accelerationist can read it and see revenue roughly ten times what it was a year earlier, with a growing share of the research now done by Claude itself. A skeptic can point to the headline loss figure of about 80 billion dollars, which Evans says is basically accounting fiction once you work out what it actually was. The problem, he says, is what the filing does not tell us. It gives revenue and customer counts, but it says little about depth and spread of usage, how well the tools are working outside a narrow band of people, and Anthropic itself cannot see much of that, because most of its business is API usage by others.

The profit and loss statement has a strange shape. The cost of inference, the cost of revenue, has stayed roughly flat while revenue rose roughly tenfold, because efficiency gains in running the models have been enormous. Meanwhile the training cost, booked as research and development, which ought to be a fixed cost, has risen in line with revenue. If you did not label the chart, Evans says, you would think the two lines were the other way round. Neither line is predictable. The price of inference is unknowable because tokens are being sold into a once in a generation shortage, with the software industry wanting to buy far more than can be supplied, so sellers can price at the customer's return on investment. But more capital spending, better models, better chips, and better inference all push costs down, roughly tenfold a year, while agents using the models could multiply token demand by one or two orders of magnitude. Usage is still broad but shallow. Spend data from Ramp, which skews toward startups and early adopters, showed the top one percent of its customer base spending ten to fifteen thousand dollars a month per employee, while the average was fifteen dollars. A small number of people use these tools intensely. There is no billion people using them intensely the way they use Instagram.

Clapham then turns to the hyperscalers, the companies spending the money. Published estimates run to two or three trillion dollars of spending by 2030, but Evans notes the calculation is circular, since nobody knows the run rate of capital spending, whether it is half a trillion or a trillion a year, and that depends on demand. He reaches for an analogy from his own career. He became a telecoms analyst in 2000, when European mobile operators spent about 110 to 120 billion euros on 3G licenses. Everything they predicted people would do with mobile internet did happen, yet most of those operators never earned a return on the investment. The value went to Uber, Amazon, Instagram, and everyone else building on top. The same could happen here: enormous adoption at low prices, a pricing collapse, and infrastructure that turns out to be a low margin commodity with no pricing power, so the value accrues to other people.

The servers themselves are part of the puzzle. Meta, Google, Amazon, and Microsoft together plan to spend about 750 billion dollars on capital expenditure this year, against about 100 billion five years ago, and three of the four are on course to spend more than half of revenue on it. Capital spending has outgrown their free cash flow, so they have had to raise money in the capital markets, which is why credit analysts suddenly matter for technology companies. When to replace a chip is a spreadsheet calculation: how many tokens you get per gigawatt, how much better the new chips and models are, and whether you are constrained by power. If power is the constraint, it makes sense to swap in chips with a higher return per watt. How long a chip actually lasts, its mechanical life versus its useful economic life, is something nobody really knows until the hardware has been run at full tilt for years. Evans frames the investor question bluntly. As a creditor you do not much care if the data centers end up as space heaters, so long as the advertising revenue is still there. As an equity investor, that was your money going into a hole in the ground.

A deeper question is what the AI product actually is. Evans compares giving everyone a model to giving everyone a spreadsheet in the mid 1980s or a web browser in the mid 1990s. That was life changing for five or ten percent of people, but it was not how companies rebuilt themselves around computers. That was SAP and Oracle. The same applies now. Handing everyone Copilot or Claude or ChatGPT is not how a business works out how AI transforms it. One outcome is that the model companies capture most of the value of that transformation. The other is that the value goes further up the stack, to a thousand new software companies, while the typical big American company goes from four or five hundred software applications today to a thousand in ten years, half of them running on AI models, without paying Anthropic or OpenAI. Part of the difficulty, Evans says, is that unlike every previous platform shift, we do not have a good first principles understanding of why this technology works as well as it does, so we do not know how good the models will get, on what dimension, and at what cost.

Are the models themselves commodities? At the level of headline benchmarks, Evans says, not really differentiated: Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Meta are all close, and the Chinese models are not far behind. Differences appear in specific use cases, and at the frontier, where a task is only just possible. The price against performance curve is very sharp. The latest full model is expensive, while a model from six months ago gives most of the benchmark performance at a small fraction of the price, by Evans's rough arithmetic. Voice recognition is finished and runs on a phone. The telling figure from Anthropic's filing is that 80 percent of revenue comes from models built in the last six months, partly because people only want the newest model and partly because a year old model is a commodity, super cheap, maybe running on premises, so nobody can make money from it.

Evans frames what AI unlocks as two things: automation and abstraction. Automation is doing the old thing faster and cheaper, and also doing things people could not do at all, like listening to every call into a call center and asking what customers are really saying, or throwing ten thousand agents at a codebase to find every possible vulnerability. Abstraction is more interesting. Instead of constructing a careful 35 step database query in Salesforce to work out who to call this week, you should be able to ask the computer who you should call, or which customers are going to churn, or which invoices are overdue and why. That is new software, and it needs to be built carefully by people whose job is to think about how it works, whether that is Accenture, Bain, a startup, or SAP. It is not Anthropic, and it is not Claude running on the chief financial officer's laptop, any more than a ten thousand person company ran its inventory from a copy of Excel.

Clapham raises the domain expertise argument, citing Harvey, the legal AI company, whose chief executive Winston Weinberg says young lawyers have a great future because they will do more interesting work. Evans answers with the Mandy Rice-Davies line: he would say that, would he not. Then he turns to the accelerationist argument itself, which he compares to Anselm's ontological argument for the existence of God. If you define the future of AI as a system that can do anything, then of course it can do everything, but there are two giant ifs in that statement, and you have defined the problem away. In practice, these systems give you the answer most people would probably give, done the way most people would do it. Sometimes that is what you want. Sometimes it is not at all what you want. And asking whether a task can be automated is really two questions: is it physically possible for a machine to do it, and do you want it done the same way every time.

On valuation, Clapham cites Aswath Damodaran's estimate of one and a half trillion dollars for Anthropic, built on the assumption that it becomes a big drugs company. Evans points out that Damodaran made a famous error in 2014, valuing Uber on the size of the existing taxi market and predicting 18 billion dollars of gross bookings, when the actual figure for mobility in 2024 was 83 billion, plus delivery and freight on top. He stresses this is not a gotcha, since predictions are hard, especially about the future. The point is that you cannot value things that way, because you do not know what the new things will be. He notes that the number of accountants rose every decade of the twentieth century even as accounting work was automated, and that the value PwC got out of spreadsheets appears in neither PwC's accounts nor Microsoft's, nor in the productivity statistics.

Asked where Anthropic and OpenAI will be in five years, Evans says he hates forecasts, and reaches for 1997. You knew the internet would be huge, but the biggest European internet stocks of the dot com bubble were internet service providers, and everything that ended up on the Yahoo and AOL homepages did happen, but not for Yahoo or AOL. The models could end up like mobile operators, commodity infrastructure whose value is captured elsewhere, or like cloud, where Amazon makes good money without controlling the whole software industry, or like chips, where each generation gets harder and more expensive until only one company, TSMC, is left at the cutting edge and can charge whatever it likes. None of those questions can be answered by reading the filing.

The signals to watch, he says, are volatility and stories being priced in twice, and the gap between product market fit in software development, which is explosive right now, and consumer product market fit at scale, which does not exist yet. A billion and a half people use these tools a little. Mark Zuckerberg said on Meta's quarterly call that Meta's Muse has product market fit, with ten million users and a quarter of them using it on any given day, which Evans notes is not the same as daily active users, and that he cannot scale it until costs fall tenfold, which is not quite product market fit if you cannot scale it. Part of the consumer problem is a blank screen: people do not know what to ask. The fix may be agents, or systems that watch everything you do and surface suggestions, but that is a thesis for Google, Meta, Amazon, and Apple, who can see your email, your messages, your purchases, and your social life. OpenAI and Anthropic see only what you do inside their apps, which is one reason OpenAI may want a device.

On the big technology companies, Evans is specific. Meta is protean: it watches user behavior, follows it, and pivots, and the bet on Meta is a bet on a company that always works it out. Google does search, and if this is search, Google does search. Apple is well positioned by not playing, since whatever happens, people will want a device with strong privacy, battery life, a camera, and custom silicon. Amazon's core is a network effect around logistics and delivery, and its decision to block Meta's Muse has two parts. One is tactical: liability and security, the same reason banks block it, since twenty years of blocking bots means nobody knows whose connection it is or who bears the loss. The other is strategic: nobody wants to be someone else's dumb API call. Amazon did 75 billion dollars of advertising revenue in the last twelve months and wants the customer relationship, the upsell, and the ownership. Evans has a slide that says every AI question has one of two answers: no one knows, or how did it work last time.

Clapham closes with the bull and bear cases. The bull case is a magical product with almost unlimited demand, efficiency improving fast, and a huge profit pool, with the only question being how it gets divided up. The bear case is that the sheer scale of investment requires an impossible level of revenue, and because the whole edifice is interconnected, the cost of money is critical. Evans adds the question of who can get capital at all: Google has the cash flow, the newer cloud companies do not. He also raises the money left on the table by behavioral economics, the friction that stops people moving savings, switching credit cards, or claiming small discounts, which might add up to a thousand pounds a year per person, and asks where that money goes once agents optimise everything. His final point is about total addressable market. If you had tried to size the market for spreadsheets in 1980 by counting accountants, you would never have predicted a billion and a half Excel users. He recalls asking Lloyd Blankfein how Goldman Sachs deployed PCs and spreadsheets in the 1980s without cutting its headcount, and Blankfein replying that a third of Goldman's employees are now software engineers. Some of the value AI creates will accrue to nobody, as consumer surplus or lower prices. Some will be new spending, captured by parties nobody can name yet. Evans argues it will probably not be the model companies, but he admits he could be completely wrong.

Open questions

  1. The filing shows explosive revenue growth, but Evans says usage is broad and shallow, with only a small number of people using the tools intensely. What signal would show the growth curve bending before the revenue line does, and who would see it first?
  2. Evans argues the value of AI will probably land with the companies that build the vertical software on top of the models, not with the model makers themselves. What would a model company have to build to keep that value, and what evidence would prove him wrong?
  3. Hyperscalers are spending at a pace that outruns their free cash flow and leaning on debt markets to fund it. If token demand softens or pricing collapses, who absorbs the loss first, and what breaks in the chain?

The Pragmatic Engineer · Satya Nadella on software engineering after cheap code

Software engineering after code becomes cheap with Satya Nadella

On The Pragmatic Engineer, “Software engineering after code becomes cheap with Satya Nadella” is host Gergely Orosz's conversation with Satya Nadella, the chief executive of Microsoft and one of the few big technology CEOs who started his career as a software engineer. The conversation ranges across Microsoft's open source turn, the rise and sudden displacement of the AI coding tools Microsoft pioneered, and the question in the title: what the work of software engineering becomes once generating code is cheap. Nadella's short answer arrives early. Generation has become cheap. Verification has become the hard part.

The conversation opens with open source. Orosz, who worked at Microsoft when Nadella became CEO in 2014, recalls a company still committed to closed source, and the surprise when it embraced open source instead: Visual Studio Code released free and open, TypeScript open sourced, GitHub acquired, SQL Server brought to Linux. Nadella says the contributions predated his tenure, but the company made its commitment unmistakable, and he argues that a true accounting would put Microsoft at or near the top of open source contributors, while some companies that benefited most from open source have contributed the least. It is, he says, narratives versus facts.

Visual Studio Code mattered for a second reason. It taught Microsoft to build in the open: a product team that treated community feedback, rather than the vice president's opinion, as the signal to follow. Nadella calls that feedback cycle fundamentally transformative, and wishes the lesson had been learned more deeply and more broadly across the company.

That history sets up the Copilot story. Microsoft was early: code completions that felt magical before GPT-4 existed, built on years of IntelliSense work, and a year or more without real competition. Cursor then took the lead on agent mode inside a forked VS Code, and Microsoft caught up. What it did not anticipate was 2025, when Anthropic's models made the command line the form factor and, in about six months, the IDE faded as the center of coding. Nadella's diagnosis is that AI first product building is unforgiving: when model capability jumps in a nonlinear way, the form factor has to change with it, and you cannot be in love with what you built. He does not treat the shift as permanent, either. Intelligence embedded inside software, in a token efficient way, could bring about a rebirth of the IDE.

One candidate for that embedded intelligence is the decision engine, a new primitive Nadella returns to several times. He compares it to what macros did for spreadsheets, and to the stored procedure of the intelligence era: a reliable, callable decision that you embed in a system once, instead of generating fresh model code every time a decision is needed, and far cheaper in tokens as a result. He reaches for the relational database as the model to copy, the developer tool he loved most after compilers, because a dependable way to store and query information made whole categories of applications possible. The aim for decision engines, which he endorses, is to become as boring as databases.

The software factory is where these threads meet. Nadella admits he is still forming his own definition, and points to two Microsoft team blogs, from the cowork team and the GitHub Copilot team, as the case studies. The core shift is that code has gone from input to output. What humans supply is specification and intent, and alignment on both. One developer now maintains an entire runtime, including a migration to Rust. The cowork team numbers roughly a dozen engineers. And because a factory has to document everything, an early career engineer told him that joining a codebase is now much easier: the system itself can act as a patient onboarding mentor, replacing tribal knowledge that used to live only in people's heads. His other example is operations rather than code: the Azure networking team, which looks after some 500 fiber operators around the world and used to run on email and improvised process, has built an agentic system that does the operations work itself, wired into the team's own repositories. Teams, he says, increasingly build factories that do the job rather than doing the job directly.

Verification is the factory's hard core. Agentic coding, as these teams practice it, means small tasks whose completion has to be genuinely proven: every failure is turned back into a test case, and the test itself has to be verified. Nadella worries about building on a single model family, whose blind spots and alignment problems become your own, and describes using a separate model family as the critic. The loop he loves most runs from production: take the traces and logs, produce a fix, verify it against the tests, and deploy it all the way through. He jokes that this verifiability loop is his version of RSI.

He would like to learn this way of working from inside. Nadella says his dream next job would be a seat on one of these teams, and names the perceptorship program run inside Microsoft by Scott Hanselman and Mark Russinovich as the place he would start. Orosz notes how many CTOs and vice presidents have stepped down into small teams to learn the new tools firsthand, and Nadella agrees: at a time of great change on many fronts, being hands on, close to customers, and building in the open matters more than overseeing from above.

Orosz pushes on the gap between lab demonstrations and product teams with real customers, who cannot drop everything to build a factory. Nadella's answer starts with permission: nobody gets permission to do work that does not add value for customers, and the real world is brownfield. AI models are now finding Windows vulnerabilities that all have to be fixed, so the Windows team is building a factory of its own, running from crash traces to verified fixes on top of the existing Windows build system, where builds and continuous integration are the hardest part. GitHub faces the same pressure from the other direction: coding agents have pushed its scale so high that the git protocol and the storage system underneath are being reworked. In brownfield work, he says, the order of operations is critical, and the new toolchain is for every team, not a showcase team.

He also thinks this change is different in kind, not just degree. New frameworks used to arrive without changing the number of concepts an engineer had to hold. This time the runtime, the framework, the IDE, and the stack are all in question at once, and a new model release can transform the regime again at any moment. On the human side, he rejects the idea that expertise has been devalued. The same skills are still required; the order of operations and the toolchain have changed, so expertise has to be exercised with new leverage. Engineers with real depth who stay curious, he says, are flourishing, and part of his job is making sure everyone at Microsoft gets the chance to build the new skill set the market will value.

That leads to the voluntary exit offers, which Orosz asks about directly: are they a prelude to layoffs as AI makes teams more efficient? Nadella frames them as agency. The opportunity ahead is real but differently shaped, with different team sizes and workflows, and Microsoft's roughly 200,000 people will be configured differently; some expertise, systems engineers and training researchers among them, already commands record wages. The point of a voluntary program, he says, is to let people slot themselves where their long term economic opportunity is best, inside Microsoft or beyond it, instead of sitting stuck on a team that is not transforming. He is candid that he cannot promise it will all be the same. One of the fastest growing jobs at the company, he notes, is the forward deployed engineer, a role that barely existed a few years ago, and roles are merging elsewhere too, as at LinkedIn, where combined roles are called full stack builders.

On junior hiring, he confirms Microsoft is still hiring new graduates and investing in them, and argues the wider slowdown may be temporary. Companies first have to reconfigure their existing teams into the new shapes; then it makes sense to hire into factories where a newcomer can be productive. AI native companies show the end state: start with one person and a token budget, let that person max out the budget, then add the next person with a budget of their own. Drawing long term trend lines from the current pause, he says, is too early.

The last third turns to models themselves. Asked whether open models parallel the open source movement, Nadella goes further, by way of Hayek: knowledge in an economy is tacit and distributed, so if you reject the idea of one frontier model holding all the world's knowledge, you should expect, in some sense, as many frontier models as there are firms. Every firm will hold both human capital and token capital. The architecture follows from that. Keep your harness, your context and memory, and your action space separate from any single model, because a model absorbs everything it sees, and if your memory lives inside one vendor's model you can never leave. His test for a sovereign firm is simple: you should be able to replace any model and still improve against your own private evaluation. Handing your learning loop to a vendor is the reverse information paradox, leaking your value away. Microsoft's own commercial answer is to offer the harness underneath Copilot as a managed service in Foundry, with many models as interchangeable endpoints and the customer's context kept separate.

The second enterprise decision he names is trust. Scaling laws deliver capability without alignment, so systems are getting more capable and less reliable at the same time, which he calls the most dangerous place to be. Authority therefore has to be separated from intelligence architecturally. Agents should be treated as insider risk: their chains of thought kept in human language, auditable, and logged in a way that cannot be tampered with, with safeguards running at runtime and not only in training.

Local models close the loop. Last year's frontier model can run on a laptop, and last quarter's on a desktop. Routers such as Copilot Fusion pick the model for each task, so in a swarm of subagents some can run on the machine in front of you, its fan spinning up, while others run in the cloud. He calls the result unmetered intelligence: intelligence you no longer have to count. Microsoft is also building its own MAI model family from the ground up, with an unusually detailed disclosure of its pre-training recipe, while continuing to build on OpenAI's models; the goal, he says, is choice, not dependence on one model family.

Asked, finally, how a software professional should approach all this, Nadella says to change the ratio of talk to doing. Get grounded in what the change actually is, stay out of the hype cycle, and learn by being on teams that are doing the work, whether you are a new graduate, mid-career, or a CEO. When is he at his best? When he is in school mode. So, he says, let's get back to school.

  2. If verification is the scarce skill inside the software factory, how should it be taught to engineers who no longer spend their early years writing and debugging code by hand?
  3. Nadella expects the junior hiring slowdown to reverse once companies finish reconfiguring their teams. What evidence would show the reversal has begun, and how long can the pause last before the pipeline of experienced engineers runs dry?

How I AI · Claire Vo on fighting token fraud at ChatPRD

How I fight token fraud as an AI founder

On How I AI, “How I fight token fraud as an AI founder” is a solo episode by Claire Vo, the show's host and the solo founder of ChatPRD, an AI platform for product managers that she has run as a bootstrapped business since the early GPT-3.5 days. Her subject is token fraud: people who are not her customers finding ways to run their work through her product's calls to models from Anthropic and OpenAI, and leaving her holding the token bill. It has cost her tens of thousands of dollars over the past year, and she says almost all of her engineering energy now goes into blocking it.

The economics explain why the fraud lands so hard on her. ChatPRD does not charge by usage. It charges per seat, with pricing built on average use across customers, so the business works only as long as real customers behave roughly as expected. She is not venture funded, so every fraudulent dollar comes directly out of her own pocket. When she posted about the problem on X, founders and engineers from some of the largest AI companies replied that they fight the same thing daily, and one friend told her he was about to turn off signups entirely.

She walks through six patterns of abuse she has seen. The first is free trial farming, where fraudsters sign up for generous free tiers and run the most expensive models on work that has nothing to do with the product. The single biggest lever she pulled was turning free trials off altogether, even though trials had been a strong source of product-led growth and enterprise leads. The second is promo code abuse: codes given out through partners were resold, stolen, and mass-used to spin up other AI businesses, with many ending up sold to resellers on Reddit. The third is bad payment cards, prepaid cards, chargebacks, and card testing. The fourth is resale and relays, including people who authenticated inside ChatPRD in a browser and relayed requests from their own AI products through it, and one case where someone installed a custom MCP in ChatPRD that turned into a worker prompting around the clock. The fifth is model probing from international accounts, a stream of questions about what model she runs and what its training cutoff is, which she suspects may be farming or distillation. The sixth is signup loops and farms, plus ordinary trial abusers using the product as a free substitute for ChatGPT to write essays and work reports.

Two stories stand out. She discovered she had been funding an international Roblox and Fortnite map studio, which looped free trials and promo codes and tunneled into her agent to build game maps. A reseller wrapped ChatPRD in a proxy and resold access to its own customers by the hour. And in the detail she clearly finds hardest to believe, fraudsters used ChatPRD itself, a tool for writing product documents, to draft product documents describing how to scam ChatPRD. Investigating abusive accounts, she also found the same operators running reverse proxies against several of the best known AI products, rotating their calls across tools.

Her business defenses, several of them drawn from a list shared by Gareth at Atlassian, are blunt. Free trials are over, she says: even trials requiring a credit card failed, because fraudsters cycled cards and filed chargebacks. Promo codes are gone. Billing has to be sooner and completely inflexible, charged up front with no grace period on failed payments. Accounts and organizations need hard usage limits, trials should be restricted to business email domains, feature flags should let you shut things off the moment something looks strange, and the terms of service need to allow bans for suspected abuse. She is rethinking international business entirely, having watched fraud drop sharply when she firewalled off whole countries, and she has reported what looked like coordinated international enterprises to the FBI as cybercrime, with Codex helping her draft the reports. Her broader plea is that founders start talking to each other about fraud patterns, something larger companies avoid because admitting fraud publicly can attract more of it.

The technical layer starts with an AI gateway. She routes calls through the Vercel AI Gateway to control spend and to enforce a strict allowlist of models, so a caller cannot simply ask for a more expensive model than the product intends to give them. Suspected fraudulent calls can be silently downgraded to a cheaper penalty model. Dartmouth suspends mathematics PhD student enrollments...

On Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander, “Dartmouth suspends mathematics PhD student enrollments...” is a read-through of a news article in The Dartmouth, the college's student newspaper, headlined “Guarini will not admit math Ph.D. cohort this year.” Alexander, a mathematician and philosopher, reads the article aloud and comments as he goes. The news is that Dartmouth's Guarini School of Graduate and Advanced Studies has suspended admissions to its mathematics PhD program for the cohort that would have matriculated in the 2027 to 2028 academic year, while the School of Arts and Sciences conducts a review of the department. Neither the dean nor a college spokesperson would say when applications will resume, or whether they will resume at all.

The official framing is investment and review. In a November 10 email statement, the Guarini dean wrote that the school regularly considers how to best invest in graduate education, and that admissions are paused for the 2027 to 2028 cycle while the department review runs, a decision made in close partnership with leaders in the School of Arts and Sciences. She promised current graduate students a chance to take part in the review and a meeting with students and faculty on November 17. The math faculty themselves learned of the suspension only on November 6, and at the November 9 general faculty meeting several raised concerns. Math professor Peter Winkler spoke against the suspension and declined to comment further afterward.

Applicants were told on November 10 that the materials and recommendation letters they had already submitted will not be reviewed. Applications would have been due on December 15, and the program is small: it typically accepts seven to nine students a year. The article also places the pause inside a national trend, citing data from 55 undisclosed research institutions showing that PhD acceptances dropped 15 percent in 2026 even though domestic applications increased. Alexander stops on that paragraph and complains, at some length, that it never says whether the figures cover all PhDs or only math PhDs. From the context he concludes that math is meant, and he admits the rise in applications surprises him. His guess is that a grim job market pushes people to delay entering it, and graduate school is the classic way to wait.

Alexander notes that the stipend is roughly twice what he earned during his own PhD at Ohio State, and speculates that the remaining students will be in higher demand as teaching assistants and tutors once no new cohort arrives. He placed himself in the second camp, and in his case it worked out: he got a good job and made good money.   2. Alexander reads the pause as a response to AI theorem proving, but the article itself cites budgets, investment choices, and a department review, and never mentions AI. Which force is really doing the work, and does the answer change what other math departments should do?

a16z · The economics of the AI revolution

What History Tells Us About the AI Revolution | Tyler Cowen & Alex Imas

On a16z, “What History Tells Us About the AI Revolution” is a conversation with two economists, Tyler Cowen of George Mason University and Alex Imas of the University of Chicago, about what artificial intelligence will actually do to the economy. The episode was recorded on November 11, 2026, and the two men agree on more than they dispute. The capabilities are real and accelerating, they say, but economic transformation has to push through bottlenecks, institutions, and plain inertia, so the path will look far more normal than the loudest forecasts suggest.

The conversation opens with the recent METR report and the anxiety around recursive self-improvement. Imas says the progress is undeniable and accelerating: model releases are more frequent and more powerful, and the shift from the chatbot era to the agentic era means systems now do actual work rather than answer questions. He did not update much on the report itself, because the data was already circulating elsewhere, but he frames the question he keeps asking of every model iteration. Which economically valuable tasks does this model unlock that the previous one could not do? A benchmark can hockey stick, he warns, while the real world barely moves, and the hardest open territory is work that is not well specified, where tacit knowledge matters.

Cowen is the more sober voice. The faster some things move, he says, the more quickly you hit the real bottlenecks. He has updated since August, mostly because of progress in mathematics, an area with few human bottlenecks, where a clearly better model can now arrive within weeks. Over the past week, though, he has grown about 1 percent more bearish, in his words. He points to the turmoil at Nvidia, to prediction markets that are not pricing the Anthropic IPO above 2 trillion dollars, and to Anthropic's own revenue projections, which are not the numbers a super fast takeoff would produce. The institutions, he says, are not running as smoothly as either the optimists or the fearful might assume.

That leads to the arithmetic of lab revenues. Cowen recounts asking people in the Bay Area who were pushing extreme revenue numbers for Anthropic and OpenAI a simple question: what other sectors will grow fast enough to justify those numbers? Nobody even tried to answer. His conclusion is that the extreme numbers are false, though he remains bullish on the sector. Market data, from real interest rates to stock prices, still look pretty normal, and he trusts the market's judgment over the judgment of people who do not think carefully about social interactions. Imas adds the mechanism: at some point the rest of the economy has to become more productive and therefore willing to pay, and that evidence is not in yet. Meanwhile the frontier labs have shifted from competing on model quality to competing on price, so token usage rises exponentially while revenue stays roughly flat.

The heart of the episode is the contrast between two growth worlds. Imas describes a paper by Ben Jones and Chad Jones that spells out what double digit, singularity style growth would require: a set of knife edge conditions, above all no decreasing returns in the production of ideas themselves. In the old world, each breakthrough gets harder to extend; in the explosive world, breakthroughs would feed on themselves with increasing returns. The key parameter can be estimated, and when Imas estimates it from the data available so far, including the METR material, it comes out decidedly less than one, which is the ordinary world of diminishing returns. A completed recursive self-improvement loop is exactly the thing that could change that parameter, he says, but it has not yet. Cowen adds the historical check. Very high growth rates do happen, in tiny economies selling one commodity, or as rebounds from deep recessions, wars, and pandemics. A single year of 10 percent growth is conceivable if an AI burst rode on top of a recovery, but he would bet heavily against it, and the effect would be temporary.

Underneath the growth debate sits a quieter piece of economics. When something gets automated, its price falls, and it tends to shrink as a share of what the economy spends. Value migrates to whatever remains scarce and slow moving, so the sectors growing least can end up contributing most to measured growth. That is one reason both men expect robotics to arrive slowly: Waymo still does not operate in Washington, DC, robotics valuations have risen without producing trillion dollar companies, and no plausible robotics path delivers 20 percent growth three or four years from now. Cowen adds a sociological note about why he and Imas see it differently from the runaway growth believers associated with writers like Dwarkesh Patel and Leopold Aschenbrenner. What is emotionally vivid to the economists is centuries of growth and productivity data; what is vivid in the Bay Area is a stream of local conversations. Both bodies of evidence contain information, but for the big question, he would rather trust the long record.

Inertia gets its own share of the blame. The IRS still uses fax machines. Educational software is a nightmare. Cowen guesses that something like 40 percent of the American economy, government, nonprofits, much of health care, and schooling, is structurally slow, and higher in parts of Europe. Regulation is a problem in those sectors, he says, but it is not the whole problem, and anyone who has dealt with those institutions up close carries a dose of realism that startup life does not provide.

If AI's benefits increasingly arrive as free or cheap goods, GDP will understate them, so the two discuss how flourishing should be measured. Imas points to work on survey based measures that ask what people would pay for digital goods they now get free, and argues that a world of 3 to 5 percent growth could still be a genuinely flourishing one if those unpriced gains are counted. Cowen raises longevity: living to 120 would be an enormous gain that GDP captures only in complicated, partial ways. Even so, he defends the old statistic. GDP tracks human welfare historically well, he says, and calls it the most underrated statistic there is.

The alignment discussion runs through their discipline's toolkit. Asked about the Hugging Face incident and agent swarms, Cowen reaches for principal agent theory: a principal simply cannot control an agent perfectly, no matter the contract, and the historical answer is checks and balances, a separation of powers, rather than perfect alignment. Imas agrees and names the framework, mechanism design. You do not need the agent to share your goals; you build the system of incentives so that even a self-interested agent behaves in the principal's interest. Both men note that the real harms so far have come from people directing AIs to do bad things, that the Hugging Face incident was layers of human error exposing broken incentives, and that the labs are more practical than their public talk suggests. Both also flatly reject the claim, associated with Paul Christiano, that the most likely cause of death a year or two from now will be AI related.

Imas then lays out his paper Organizing Intelligence, written with co-authors, which starts from a puzzle: capability keeps rising, yet no visible economic transformation has followed. Their answer is that the relevant competitor for an AI agent is not an individual human but an organization. Organizations embody comparative advantage, since the person doing each job is the one best suited to it, so a team's combined, mosaic intelligence is far stronger than an average person's. Even a minimal AGI, as capable as the average human on every dimension, would mostly shift wages rather than transform the economy, because it would be competing against that higher bar. What would transform things is coordination. Today's agent swarms mostly do parallel processing and coordinate badly; on coordination tasks, one agent often outperforms two agents splitting the work, while two people outperform one person given double the resources. If agents learned to coordinate, firms could break through the Coasean ceiling on firm size, and the shape of companies, wages, and employment would genuinely change.

Employment is where their worries differ in emphasis. Imas fears the transition speed: if coordination improves in a breakthrough rather than gradually, the economy could hit a phase transition in which displaced workers cannot rematch into new roles fast enough, and long spells of unemployment scar people out of the labor market. Cowen's worry is about hiring signals. Employers used to rely on imperfect indicators like degrees and interviews; now the key question is whether a person will be mentally flexible enough to work well with AI, and nobody can tell yet. That could raise the natural rate of unemployment for a while, until certifications and experience emerge. On balance he is bullish: there will be an incredible number of new projects and new jobs, alongside disruptions of the kind that accompanied the China shock and the industrial revolution.

On the agent economy's scale, Cowen resists the word explosion. Asked when agents will be 10 percent of GDP, he says not soon. If forced to guess, he could see one and a half percent within five years, and even that would be enormous, because, as he puts it, GDP is huge. The frictions are mundane and deep: websites are designed around human psychology, platforms have incentives to keep outside agents from shopping on them, and some bottlenecks live in human heads. Cowen does not let an agent manage his calendar, because writing entries himself is how he remembers what his days contain, and he does not expect that quirk to disappear even if agents get ten times better. Politics is another endogenous brake. The recent elections may slow data centers and bring regulation sooner, Cowen says, and the Texas result genuinely surprised him, but governors are already backtracking, and an extreme technological scenario would provoke an extreme political reaction that slows it from within. Money, meanwhile, buys less politics than feared: Elon Musk spent on the order of 500 million dollars and did not get the outcome he wanted, which matches what campaign finance researchers have long found.

The scarcity theme returns in a discussion of the argument, made by Dwarkesh Patel and Philip Trammell, that Piketty's model of capital pulling away from labor might fit the AI century even if it missed the last one. Imas grants the scenario but notes the alternative: value flows to whatever stays scarce, and in a rich society scarce human jobs can capture a great deal of it. Cowen insists on a distinction between capital being able to do a lot and monopoly rents sticking to particular owners. Rents follow scarcity, which makes land and energy more plausible destinations than capital in general, and AI might make even those less scarce, through cheap desalination or land reclamation. The host offers the firm's own version: the binding scarcity right now is supply, the heaviest users cannot get enough compute, and a16z has started a Machine Age Fund aimed at data centers and the infrastructure stack.

Two famous documents get graded. Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness essay stands, Cowen says, as one of the most important public intellectual pieces of recent years, but it is too enamored of fast takeoff, because it assumes simulation can substitute for experiment, and mostly it cannot. Imas adds the Lucas critique: simulations predict, but they do not account for how the system itself responds. Robin Hanson's The Age of Em is brilliant and wrong, in Cowen's telling; he told Hanson years ago that AI would get there first and that whole brain emulation, if it ever happens, would need AI before it. On Nvidia, both men return to their refrain: the episode is a lesson in checks and balances, the supply chain is diversified, and the largest real cost may be that a weakened Nvidia would be less able to act as a buyer or lender of last resort in a macroeconomic crisis. In the medium term, they expect structural forces to take over.

The closing stretch is personal. Imas tells his origin story: a refugee from Transnistria, in Moldova, who grew up on food stamps in America, went to college premed because his parents knew what a doctor was, took an economics class, found Cowen's blog Marginal Revolution and Richard Thaler on NPR, and walked away from the medical school path for a doctorate at UC San Diego. Cowen's own early signal came from chess. A prodigy at 13, he watched a computer called Tinkerbell play weakly against humans and saw the trajectory anyway, because chess players know chess runs on intuition, and a machine conquering an intuition based domain can plausibly conquer others; Kasparov, Ken Rogoff, and Demis Hassabis were early for the same reason. That faith extends to verification: if you can criticize an output, it is close to verifiable, and training the verifier makes almost anything verifiable, which is why Cowen predicts AI could write great poetry within two years given even a modest effort, and says the models already show better taste in classical music recordings than the Penguin Guide. They end on higher education, where both want far more experimentation and measurement, Imas expects the traditional sector to shrink while staying bullish on it long term, and Cowen sketches a future in which economics merges into a broader social science practiced by smart people managing fleets of AI agents, the old Chicago vision of Gary Becker finally realized.

Open questions

  1. Double digit growth requires the returns to ideas to stop diminishing, and Imas estimates that parameter well below one today. What evidence over the next few years would show a recursive self-improvement loop actually moving it, rather than just faster progress inside the old regime?
  2. Imas argues coordination, not competence, is the binding constraint on transformation. What benchmark result or real world signal would tell us agents have learned to coordinate well enough to break the ceiling on firm size?
  3. If value migrates to whatever stays scarce, whether land, energy, or particular human skills, how can workers and investors tell which of today's scarcities AI is about to make abundant?

20VC with Harry Stebbings · Jev and the cost of intelligence

Jev Creator on Will US Open Models Catch Chinese | The Cost of Intelligence & Tokens | Diogo Almeida

On 20VC with Harry Stebbings, “Jev Creator on Will US Open Models Catch Chinese | The Cost of Intelligence & Tokens | Diogo Almeida” is host Harry Stebbings in conversation with Diogo Almeida, the co-founder and chief executive of TypeSafe AI, the company behind Jev, a model built to make fast, reliable decisions inside software. Stebbings opens with the claim that frames the whole episode: reliability is the largest bottleneck in AI. Jev is used by a third of the Fortune 500, and the company has been valued at 7.5 billion dollars.

Almeida's starting point is the jaggedness of today's models. They can help with Millennium Prize level mathematics yet cannot be trusted with accounting. His shorthand is that you get Einstein perhaps 95 percent of the time and Mr. Bean the other 5 percent, and in expected value terms that failure rate kills automation. Companies have tried for years to automate work like accounting and keep failing, not because the models lack capability, but because no business can build on intelligence that occasionally makes a foolish decision. What automation needs, he argues, is nines of reliability in the decisions themselves.

His explanation is what he calls AI's bitterest lesson. Richard Sutton's bitter lesson said compute matters more than algorithms, and the scaling era that followed plateaued at GPT-4.5. Almeida's ordering of what matters runs: doing the right task first, then data, then compute, then algorithms. The field's two great training tasks were reinforcement learning from human feedback, which taught models to please people, and reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards, which taught them to score well on benchmarks. Neither one optimizes for software running unattended in the background, so the models were never trained for the job automation actually needs done. You get what you optimize for.

Data, in this view, is worth far more than compute, but the right data rarely exists. When human preference training was first built, the comparison data it needed was nowhere on the internet or in any company database; it had to be defined and created. The same bootstrapping problem faces any genuinely new task. Corporate data lakes are unlikely to hold the answer, because their data is not shaped for the task, and what makes training data good depends on whether it improves the current best models, which is why data makers need access to the frontier themselves. Synthetic data is simply a tool whose value depends on the hands using it and on how tightly it is aimed at the right task.

Asked what people get wrong about Jev, Almeida says they fixate on cost and speed when the point is the reliability of its decisions. Copycat decision models are already shipping, but in his telling they chase benchmarks and headline numbers instead of doing the slow work of making a model dependable. The big labs could compete if they chose to care about reliability. He argues they optimize for other things, historically monthly active users, which is how sycophancy emerged in chatbots, and now the equivalent engagement metrics for coding agents. Benchmarks, he says, are a band-aid people use because intelligence as a product is hard to understand.

That is also why enterprise software still looks like the old software with AI attached to the side. Because language models are unreliable, companies let them make decisions whose stakes fall on users, who bear the burden of checking the output, and keep them away from decisions with stakes for the company itself. Almeida doubts the fashionable claim that every company must own its own trained intelligence. He calls AI co-workers the horseless carriages of this era, and predicts that a company's proprietary intelligence will mostly live in the software and engineering built around models, not in models it trains itself. Established software companies, he adds, are better positioned than their critics think, not because of distribution, but because they understand the workflows where automation has to land.

On the cost of intelligence, his answer is deflationary. Raw, general intelligence per dollar of compute is barely improving now, he says. What improves is performance inside a chosen distribution: coding leapt forward when coding was put in distribution, around an Opus release late last year, while writing, by his account, has not improved in nine months. This is also his lens on the spending boom. He believes AI has been in a bubble for a while, calls most new labs wasteful, and would not invest his own money in the reinforcement learning race. Demand, he warns, rests partly on fear of missing out and on buyers overestimating what AI can do, and demand built that way can fall quickly.

The open model argument takes up the title question. Almeida's guess is that American open models will not catch Chinese open models in true intelligence unless China stops trying, not because of capability, but because American efforts hold themselves to higher ethical and legal baselines. Whether the gap matters depends on how hard the field plateaus. He would not run a Chinese model unmodified inside his own coding agent, but he rejects the idea that a model is either plugged in as is or unusable: bias can be measured and trained out, guardrails can be added, and Jev itself is used to classify whether tool calls are safe or phoning home. On benchmarks generally, he argues the top tier labs, OpenAI and Anthropic among them, are mostly truthful because they do not need to cheat, while second tier labs lie to themselves under internal pressure, which is how the Llama 4 episode happened. Anthropic's models, he says flatly, are simply the best, even when Chinese models match them on benchmark tables.

The business itself is run against the grain of the moment. TypeSafe is profitable, its bank balance is rising, and it has good margins, because its models are efficient to serve rather than subsidized. The big fundraise was entirely inbound, with no deck, and Almeida says the company did not need the money or a maximum valuation; it needed allies for fighting the largest companies in the world. He chose Andreessen Horowitz for its philosophical alignment, and Sequoia, in his words, less for the firm than for partner Sonya Huang, whose judgment he wanted on his side. The same filter applies to hiring: he wants missionaries rather than mercenaries, refuses to chase the extreme pay packages of the big labs, and says he does not hire researchers from OpenAI, because their culture prizes algorithms and scale and treats data as low status work.

He closes with predictions and preferences. Decision models running in the background will, through a Jevons paradox effect, consume far more tokens than today's chat models, and he wants a new kind of practitioner, the software researcher, to push the boundaries of what software can be. Robotics is, in his instinct, the most overhyped category in AI, being furthest from the reliability automation needs. And his candidate for the belief that sounds crazy now but will be common in five years is that AI will dissolve principal-agent problems throughout society, letting people enforce the spirit of the law rather than its letter.

  2. If American open models cannot catch Chinese ones while holding higher ethical and legal baselines, and the gap may not matter if capabilities plateau, who should decide whether the United States keeps paying that cost: the labs, the market, or the government?
  3. He argues both that AI demand rests partly on fear of missing out and overestimation, and that background automation will eventually dwarf today's token use. Can both be true on the same timeline, and what would the demand shock he predicts do to the automation future he is building for?

Ryan Peterman · Steve Furber and the ARM processor

How Two First-Timers Created the World’s Most Popular Processor | Steve Furber

On Ryan Peterman, “How Two First-Timers Created the World’s Most Popular Processor | Steve Furber” is host Ryan Peterman's conversation with Steve Furber, the co-designer of the ARM processor and one of the principal designers of the BBC Micro. Furber's doctorate was in aerodynamics, nobody on the Acorn team had ever designed a processor before ARM, and the design they produced now sits inside, by the last figure Furber heard, more than 350 billion shipped chips.

Furber came to computers sideways. Interested in flight simulation as a student, he joined the Cambridge University Processor Group, a student society of people who built computers for fun, and ordered a Signetics 2650 microprocessor from a store in California, which felt daring in the age of new credit cards. He hand assembled the machine with a wiring pen technique called Verowire, etched circuit boards in ferric chloride, and anodized the aluminum front panel in his kitchen sink. There were no tutorials. He learned from data sheets, hobbyist magazines, and friends who would come around, poke at the machine, and help find bugs.

Acorn grew out of CPU Limited, a company founded by Hermann Hauser and Chris Curry, who recruited Furber and Sophie Wilson from the Processor Group to do technical work. When the BBC went looking for a computer to support a television series, Acorn won the contract with a design based to a considerable extent on a machine Furber had built at home, and he joined the company as an employee in October 1981. The BBC Micro's main board carried 102 chips, two of them semi-custom uncommitted logic arrays that squeezed the function of ten or twenty standard packages onto one chip, and those two chips were Furber's introduction to chip design. Hauser had already decided Acorn's future depended on it, predicting that in ten years there would be two sorts of computer companies: those that had learned to design chips, and those that had gone bust.

ARM began with disappointment. Testing the era's 16-bit processors as second processors for the BBC Micro, Furber and Wilson found two flaws: their interrupt response was slower than the 8-bit 6502 they already used, which broke the real-time data handling the BBC Micro depended on, and their microcoded instructions took several clock cycles each, so they could not use the memory bandwidth the memory chips of the day could actually deliver. Then they read the RISC papers from Berkeley and Stanford. A postgraduate class at Berkeley had designed a competitive processor in a year, and Furber says that started them thinking: if they can design a processor, maybe we can too.

The division of labor was clean. Wilson, who had written all of Acorn's BASIC interpreters and knew what instructions a high level language needs, designed the instruction set. Furber turned it into a microarchitecture and built a reference model of the whole chip in 88 lines of BBC BASIC. Acorn's silicon team translated that model into an implementation, and eighteen months after the project started in 1983, they had the first ARM silicon. ARM was 32-bit from the outset, most instructions executed in a single cycle, and the design deliberately used sequential memory addresses so it could exploit the double speed page mode of the memory chips. Furber calculates the result at roughly twenty-five times the useful memory bandwidth of the BBC Micro setup.

The working methods were as improvised as the team. Furber designed the microarchitecture on paper, sketching registers, buses, and the barrel shifter, making photocopies, and coloring in what each bus carried for every instruction in every clock phase. When an instruction refused to map onto the sketch, he threw the sheets away and redrew the whole arrangement. Software models did the testing: his cycle-level model checked the microarchitecture, faster instruction-level emulators ran bigger programs, and exhaustive test programs exercised each unit, the barrel shifter, the arithmetic unit, the register file, before real hardware existed.

Furber's explanation of why ARM won is almost embarrassed. The RISC idea seemed so obvious to the Acorn team that they expected the big semiconductor companies to pick it up and trample Acorn underfoot, leaving them with nothing but the learning. Instead the mainstream industry took another decade to come around, and simplicity did the rest: a simple processor is physically small and low in power, which turned out to be exactly what the coming world of portable devices wanted. Intel, he concedes, was probably right to keep its complex instruction set for the sake of software compatibility, though a modern x86 chip now translates its instructions into a RISC-like stream and executes them on a RISC-like core anyway.

Two scenes capture the era's scale. Visiting the Western Design Center in Phoenix in October 1983, Furber and Wilson expected a gleaming American office block and found the 6502's successor being designed out of two suburban bungalows, with schoolchildren brought in over the summer to design cell libraries. And Acorn never considered building its own factory: fabrication was far beyond its means, so VLSI Technology in San Jose made the early ARM chips, a four-chip set that went into Acorn's Archimedes computer.

Acorn itself did not survive its own invention. In 1990 ARM was spun out as a joint venture with Apple and VLSI Technology, after Apple said it wanted ARM for its Newton device but would be more comfortable if ARM were an independent company. Acorn's computer business had stopped growing, so it was pushing on an open door. Eventually the value of the ARM shares Acorn owned exceeded the value of Acorn itself, and the company wound down. Furber moved into academia at Manchester, where his later work, SpiNNaker, put a million ARM processors into a single machine built to model regions of the brain in biological real time. Its hard problem is connectivity: a typical cortical neuron connects to five to ten thousand others, so the machine's communication system is designed for that one job. He also explains his interest in asynchronous, clockless design, which spreads switching out in time, cutting radio interference and current spikes, and lets each part of a chip run at its own speed, at the cost of harder design work that the industry's tools still handle poorly.

The conversation closes with the personal frame. Furber won a bronze medal at the International Mathematical Olympiad in 1970, recommends the Hennessy and Patterson textbooks to anyone entering computer architecture, and says the work he is proudest of is ARM, because whenever you enter a room full of people there are now more ARM processors in the room than there are heads. His advice to people starting out is to choose the course that keeps the most doors open, and to watch for opportunities as they arise. His own career, ten years of mathematics and aerodynamics followed by a jump into a personal computer business that was just taking off, is his Exhibit A, and he names AI as the opportunity today's beginners should be watching.

Open questions

  1. ARM was designed under scarcity, of transistors, of memory bandwidth, of money for a factory, and its simplicity was a product of those limits. Chip design is now vastly more capital intensive and tool dependent. Could two first-timers still do what Furber and Wilson did, or has the industry's floor risen past the point where beginners can reach it?
  2. Furber says the mainstream industry took a decade to accept an idea he found obvious, and that Intel survived by absorbing RISC internally rather than adopting it. What is the present-day equivalent: an approach the incumbents are quietly absorbing into their existing products instead of conceding to outright?
  3. SpiNNaker bets that understanding the brain needs a million modest processors wired for extreme connectivity, while the AI industry is consolidating around ever larger accelerators. If the goal is modeling brains rather than training models, which architecture is the right one, and how would we know?

Kai Explains · The true cost of a 1TB model at home

What It Really Costs to Run a 1TB AI Model at Home

On Kai Explains, “What It Really Costs to Run a 1TB AI Model at Home” opens with a confession. A few months ago Kai lost sleep over a shopping cart holding two Mac Studios priced at 44,598 dollars, and he had nearly convinced himself the purchase was reasonable. The new trillion parameter open models were out, and owning the hardware to run them promised freedom: no subscriptions, no API bills, and no provider quietly swapping the model on him. Then he sat down and did the actual math, and this video is the receipt, pricing every path to running a terabyte class model at home as of November 16, 2026, and asking the question he says everyone skips: does any of it actually beat renting?

First he fixes a naming problem. The T in a model name counts parameters, in trillions, while TB on a price tag counts bytes of storage, and the two only line up by accident. The three models people mean are Meta's Muse Spark Open, with 816 billion parameters and about 42 billion active per token, a full precision download of 1,644 gigabytes and an official four bit version of 533 gigabytes; Mistral Large 4, with roughly a trillion parameters and 52 billion active, shipped already compressed to eight bits at 1,060 gigabytes, the only one of the three that is actually a terabyte as downloaded; and Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro, with 2.78 trillion parameters and 104 billion active, which still takes 1,561 gigabytes even after Moonshot squeezed most of it to four bits. He also flags a unit trap that once burned him: Apple's gigabytes are binary, so a 512 gigabyte Mac holds about 550 of the decimal gigabytes Hugging Face lists, a gap of roughly seven percent in the buyer's favor that matters when a model is 533 gigabytes and the machine is 512.

The case for owning, he grants, is genuinely strong. A model on your own hardware cannot be quietly weakened, swapped, repriced mid contract, or retired out from under the workflows built on it, and it runs the same on day one thousand as on day one. Your prompts and documents never leave the building, which for some industries is a legal requirement rather than a preference. The licenses can be extraordinary: Kimi K3.1 Pro's license fully permits personal use of the most powerful open model available. And memory is getting scarcer and pricier, with Nvidia raising the 128 gigabyte DGX Spark from 3,999 dollars at launch to 6,950 dollars in October, so the buy now argument feels urgent. Underneath it all sits the dream of paying once and being done. What the product page never shows, he says, is that the box charges you too, on every token it writes, through hardware cost and electricity, and when you divide total cost by total tokens the number is not what you would hope.

The technical reason sits in the architecture. All three models are mixture of experts designs: hundreds of small specialist networks, with a router picking a handful to handle each token. Only the chosen experts compute, but because the next token may choose a completely different set, every expert has to sit in fast memory the whole time, like keeping a whole warehouse stocked to pull from a few shelves per order. Decoding is memory bound, so total parameters decide how much memory you must buy, while active parameters divided by memory bandwidth decide how fast the model writes. A cloud provider pays for one warehouse and keeps its loading dock busy around the clock serving thousands of people. A home buyer pays for the same warehouse to serve one person, a few hours a day, and that asymmetry is structural, not a matter of margins.

Then he prices the hardware. The setup that gets the most attention is two Mac Studio M5 Ultras with 512 gigabytes each, the configuration Meta itself recommends for Muse Spark Open: Apple sells that much memory only on its top model at 22,299 dollars a machine, hence the 44,598 dollar cart, and the step from 256 to 512 gigabytes alone costs an extra 11,500 dollars. Each Mac reads memory at 1.2 terabytes per second, but the model split across two boxes passes data over a Thunderbolt 5 cable far slower than either machine's memory, two fast engines joined by a narrow hose. Eight clustered DGX Sparks reach a terabyte for 55,600 dollars, but each Spark reads at only 273 gigabytes per second and the cluster links are slower still. A server board filled with sixteen 64 gigabyte DDR5 sticks costs about 40,978 dollars for the memory alone, before chassis, boards, or processors, while the same capacity in older DDR4 runs about 4,719 dollars and pays for the savings in speed. Eleven RTX Pro 6000 Blackwell cards would cost 176,000 dollars and draw some 6,600 watts, and Nvidia's one box DGX Station GB300, listed between 90,000 and 115,000 dollars, holds 748 gigabytes and does not even reach a terabyte.

A hidden gotcha, he warns, invalidates many of the it fits claims online. By default macOS gives the GPU only about three quarters of a Mac's memory, leaving roughly 412 of 512 gigabytes for the model, and the community three bit build of Muse Spark Open at 436 gigabytes does not actually load until you raise the limit with a system command that resets at every reboot. Two Macs at three quarters each offer about 825 usable gigabytes, so Mistral's 1,060 gigabyte download does not fit at all, and Meta's recommended pair runs Meta's four bit file, not the full precision model. A guide that never mentions the memory limit flag, he says, measured the sticker rather than the machine.

Apple, unusually, published its own speed measurements on launch day. Two 512 gigabyte Mac Studios running the four bit weights produced 24.99 tokens per second on a prompt of about 8,000 tokens, and four Macs, 89,196 dollars of hardware, reached 36.73, so doubling the machines bought about 47 percent more speed, the interconnect already eating the scaling. The number he says matters more is time to first token: 8.2 seconds on two Macs just to read that 8,000 token prompt, and over 50 seconds on four Macs for a 65,000 token prompt. These models reread everything constantly; across one full benchmark the model read 63 tokens for every token it wrote, so agentic work, where every file edit or tool result sends the whole context back through, is dominated by reading. On a 128,000 token coding prompt the local setup keeps you waiting about 81 seconds before the first word on an uncached turn, while the hosted model starts answering in 9.9 seconds and writes at 180 tokens per second against your 25.

Then comes the division he wishes he had done first. Grant the most generous case possible: the two Macs running flat out, 24 hours a day for three years with no downtime, produce about 2.36 billion tokens. The hardware costs 44,598 dollars and the electricity, two machines at 385 watts each and 18 cents per kilowatt hour, adds about 3,642 dollars, for a total of 48,240 dollars. That works out to 20.40 dollars per million output tokens. The hosted price for the exact same model, same weights, is 4.25 dollars per million output tokens, so the box bought to escape rent charges nearly five times the rent, and that local figure counts only the tokens written while the hosted price includes reading your prompt as well, which makes the true premium higher still. At a heavy eight hours of use a day, the local cost climbs to about 58 dollars per million. An average task that writes some 58,000 tokens takes about 38 minutes of writing on the Macs against about five minutes hosted, and paying the machines off would mean running tasks back to back for roughly a year and a half without a break, assuming every one of them would otherwise have been rented. Depreciation does not care about your utilization either: one owner found Apple's trade in offer on the previous generation 512 gigabyte machine was about a tenth of its purchase price.

Worse, the terabyte models are not even the best models. On the rebuilt Artificial Analysis intelligence index, Claude Opus 5.5 leads at 58, while Muse Spark Open scores 25 and Mistral Large 4 scores 26. GLM 5.3 Flash scores 28 and its three bit build, about 120 gigabytes, fits on a single DGX Spark. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash scores 32, downloads at 510 gigabytes, and with the memory flag raised runs on one 512 gigabyte Mac, where an owner measured about 66 tokens per second, nearly three times the speed of two Macs running Muse Spark Open. The terabyte, he concludes, buys parameters without automatically buying performance. The exception is Kimi K3.1 Pro, which scores 46, the top open model available and a different league from the other two. But Kimi is also the hardest to own: its 1,561 gigabyte download is already four bit, Nvidia's own four bit conversion is slightly larger at 1,605 gigabytes, and its million token context can add roughly 112 gigabytes of working memory on top of the weights. The smallest Apple setup that holds it is four Mac Studios at 89,196 dollars. Nobody has published measured speeds for that rig, but scaling from Apple's numbers suggests about 15 tokens per second, which prices out near 68 dollars per million tokens run flat out for three years, against Moonshot's rental price of 10 dollars per million. Break even would take about 24,238 average tasks at some 80 minutes each, roughly 3.7 years of non stop running, longer than the three year life the hardware was priced over.

He is fair to the cheap routes, and prices each one's trade of money for time. Used DDR4 server memory puts a terabyte in a dual socket board for around 4,700 dollars, but owners report single digit tokens per second, and at one token per second an average 72,000 token task takes about 20 hours. Streaming the model from fast NVMe drives, loading only the experts each token needs, is the cheapest route of all, and owners streaming Kimi that way report about a third of a token per second, so the same task takes roughly two and a half days. TinyCorp's TinyBox Red, announced this month at 30,000 dollars, pairs 128 gigabytes of GPU memory with an SSD array and claims 40 tokens per second on Kimi K3, though one independent analysis puts the ceiling near four tokens per second if every weight has to come off the drives, so the claim rests on most weights already sitting in GPU memory. Compressing harder helps less than it appears: Unsloth's one bit Kimi build fits in 594 gigabytes and matches the original model's next word predictions 78.9 percent of the time, which raises the question of why not simply run a smaller model at full quality in that space. And stacking more boxes buys capacity, not single user speed: sixteen DGX Sparks, at 111,200 dollars, are what Kimi actually needs, owners report 21 to 30 tokens per second dropping to 11 or 13 as context grows, and the links between boxes move data about eleven times slower than the memory inside them.

None of this means nobody should buy. Where data legally cannot leave the building, in clinics, law firms, and government contracting, renting is not an option at any price and the per token figure is simply the cost of running the model at all. Serving a team changes the math, because batching is what makes the cloud cheap and a home or office rig can do it too: one tester running four Sparks found sixteen simultaneous users produced about four and a half times the single user output. Fine tuning on proprietary data requires holding the weights yourself, which hosted inference will not allow. And today's rental prices are low partly because eighteen providers on OpenRouter compete to serve Kimi, pushing some prices from 20 dollars down toward 6 dollars per million tokens; if that market shifts, owning hardware is insulation. His practical advice is to rent the exact model you actually want for one month of real work, then read the invoice rather than the subscription price, because the invoice tells you how many tokens you truly use, and dividing the hardware price by that monthly bill tells you how many months the box needs to pay for itself before power, depreciation, and the next generation of models. His closing line is the video's rule: price the token before you price the terabyte.

Open questions

  1. Kai's cost figures assume today's hosted prices, which he notes are held down by eighteen providers competing to serve the same open weights. If that competition thins out and rental prices climb toward the cost of owning, at what price does the home terabyte rig start winning, and would buyers who skipped it be able to build one then at anything like today's hardware prices?
  2. The strongest cases for owning are legal data restrictions, team serving, and fine tuning, none of which the per token math captures. How should a buyer weigh those unpriced benefits against a measured gap of nearly five times the hosted cost, and who inside a company should make that call?
  3. The terabyte models score below smaller models that fit on a single machine, while the one model that justifies the size, Kimi K3.1 Pro, is the least practical to run at home. If open models keep gaining intelligence faster at smaller sizes, does the home terabyte category have a future at all, or was it a brief moment created by one generation of oversized releases?

Atlassian CEO: How Humans Will Work Alongside AI Agents — With Mike Cannon-Brookes

On Alex Kantrowitz, “Atlassian CEO: How Humans Will Work Alongside AI Agents — With Mike Cannon-Brookes” is a live recording of the Big Technology Podcast, taped in front of an audience at Web Summit. Kantrowitz interviews Mike Cannon-Brookes, the CEO of Atlassian, the company behind Jira, Trello, Confluence, and Loom, about how AI is changing work now that it is in the hands of millions of people, rather than remaining a theory about the future.

His short answer is that work is already changing, because companies are weaving agentic work into business processes large and small. The efficiency gains so far are real but smaller than many people would like, while the quality of the output is improving, and he sees the start of a large growth in creativity, in how much can be built as companies learn to make AI part of their teams.

Asked directly whether all these agents are cutting workforces, Cannon-Brookes sides with the restructuring explanation over the replacement story. Companies that announce layoffs insist they are restructuring for the AI era rather than handing work to agents, and he thinks that answer is probably far more accurate. Anyone who claims to know what happens in the next five years should not be believed, he says, but his own view is that AI will create far more jobs than it changes, and that the binary framing of jobs lost against jobs gained misses the point, because the nature of most jobs is what actually changes.

His favorite parallel is the bank branch. In the 2000s, everyone predicted that online and mobile banking would close the branches, yet over the last ten years branches have been growing, at least in Australia and in America, because a branch in 2026 does a vastly different job for the customer than it did in 2000, with customer service far more important. Software developers, designers, marketers, and finance professionals will all still exist, he argues, but none of them will work as they did five years ago, and the ratio between job families may shift in ways nobody can call: he recounts a product manager predicting many more product managers and far fewer engineers, and an engineer predicting exactly the reverse.

The sharper risk, in his telling, is competitive rather than technological. He endorses the line that he is not afraid of losing his job to AI, but to someone who is really good at using AI to do it. The task for a company is to change the jobs it has before a rival does the same work better by embracing the technology, and changing how people think, their historical priors, is a talent challenge more than a technology challenge.

He also pushes back on the premise that Atlassian sells AI products. Atlassian sells technology products that help companies run workflows, and AI is a new ingredient in the technology mix, no more a product category than mobile or the internet were; he expects the companies with AI at the end of their names to disappear over time. The real bottleneck is design and experience. Given all the model progress, he says, the reason the efficiencies are not flowing through into company output and creativity is that people have not yet changed how they think about their jobs, and the product interfaces have not changed enough to help them. The two have to come together.

Atlassian's own answer is what it calls its agentic multiplayer protocol, or AMP, shipped at its conference in Amsterdam about a month before the recording. AMP is a set of experiences, technologies, and patterns for bringing agents into a team and into the interfaces of Jira, Confluence, Loom, and the service collection, with technical standards such as MCP and A2A running underneath. It is meant to feel familiar and human centric while still taking advantage of what AI can do, and he expects many more interface paradigm changes in the year ahead.

Asked what a job actually looks like in this world, he says people will think a great deal more about the outcome they want and less about the mechanics of the task in front of them. Taste and judgment are part of that, and verification is another: ask AI to build a really advanced financial model and you will get an answer, so you need a way to test whether the model is correct. Work also moves to a higher level of abstraction, away from asking a question and getting an answer, and toward setting up a series of loops that keep improving until they reach the outcome. The hard part is carrying those ideas inside the applications people already use every day, because most workers cannot be expected to think like engineers about goal seeking and continual improvement.

He then lays out the different ways humans and agents will work together, using a meeting table as the image. Chat, the familiar question and answer exchange, is like sitting on opposite sides of the table, and he doubts it will be the most common mode. Delegation is sending the agent to the meeting on your behalf: telling a coding agent to go and do something, and receiving the pull request it made, made by the agent on your behalf rather than by you. Collaboration is genuinely multiplayer, two or three people working alongside two or three agents from different vendors on the same Confluence page. Pairing is sitting on the same side of the table as the agent, co-crafting a single output side by side, which is where a series of new Loom features points. His summary of the point is that he does not believe everyone is going to sit at a command line and interact back and forth.

Underneath AMP sits the teamwork graph, an enterprise context graph Atlassian has been building for about eight years, growing from roughly five people working on it to about a thousand. It tries to map all the objects and relationships in the knowledge side of a company: documents, spreadsheets, work projects, goals and outcomes, email, calendars, meeting transcripts, source code, physical assets, and data assets such as Databricks or Tableau, all in a single graph. Atlassian runs inference over that graph and serves the answers to people through search and chat, and to agents through a CLI or MCP. An agent connected to the graph uses it for far more of its queries and gets far better answers, which means lower cost, fewer tokens, and a faster outcome. The graph also keeps learning: after an agent session finishes, Atlassian indexes the session, extracts its key memories and learnings along with the linked documents and objects, and feeds them straight into the next session, so agent effectiveness compounds.

Kantrowitz points out that Cannon-Brookes has not once named the model underneath any of this, and suggests the models themselves are starting to commoditize. Cannon-Brookes only partly agrees. Models will continue to have different flavors, different strengths for different tasks, and vastly different cost profiles, so they are not interchangeable, but they are also less important than people make out. Simply jumping to the latest model is a very expensive way to work and is often slower; what matters is model blending, knowing which model answers which question best, and the harness that runs on top of the model, with the context in the teamwork graph, AMP, and the interfaces layered above that. Progress, he says, will come from all of these parts advancing at different levels and being combined in new ways.

Nor is he rattled by the model labs moving upstream into applications, with products such as Claude Code, Claude Cowork, and Claude Design, or by the warning, attributed to Satya Nadella, that every time you work with a model you leave breadcrumbs it can learn from. Every era has a version of this fear: in the 1990s it was that Microsoft would crush everybody, then Google, then Amazon, and now it is the model makers. Laws of physics around focus, delivery, and expertise apply to every company, and Atlassian's expertise is delivering outcomes for customers' teams and workflows at massive scale, with all the compliance requirements and continual investment in design and experience that involves, which he calls an art and creativity problem rather than an engineering or algorithm problem. The same logic answers the claim that all software will soon be copied for free because the cost of writing code has fallen: Atlassian's cost of writing code has fallen too, so its speed of delivering for customers rises with everyone else's, and model progress is available to all vendors pretty evenly, which makes for a surprisingly even playing field.

He saves his sharpest line for the headless trend, in which software companies strip away their own interface and let agents access the software directly. Headless, he says, is brainless: if you believe most of your usage will come through a headless interface and therefore stop working on the design and experience of your software, that is a path to death. Atlassian is a big investor in its CLI and runs one of the most used MCP servers at the major labs, and those channels are genuinely effective for agents, but he does not believe the majority of people will sit at a terminal to run their enterprise software day to day. Visual interfaces were built for a reason, a recent survey showed people hated working the command line way, and while dynamic interfaces generated from decision models are fascinating and Atlassian has prototypes, an interface that rearranges itself completely on every visit would only leave people disoriented. Treating software as a hidden database for agents to query is, in his word, naive, though he concedes we will see.

Open questions

  1. Cannon-Brookes predicts that AI will create far more jobs than it eliminates, with most jobs changing rather than vanishing, much as bank branches changed instead of closing. What evidence over the next few years would actually settle whether the branch parallel holds for knowledge work?
  2. If the durable value sits in context, harnesses, and interface design rather than in the models, as he argues, what stops the model labs from building those layers themselves as they push upstream into workplace applications?
  3. His ways of working with agents, chat, delegation, multiplayer collaboration, and pairing, each demand a different kind of interface. Which of them becomes the default for most workers, and who ends up deciding, the software vendors or the workers themselves?

New York Times Podcasts · The Daily on a vaccine that treats cancer

Finally, a Vaccine for Cancer

On New York Times Podcasts, “Finally, a Vaccine for Cancer” is an episode of The Daily, the Times's flagship daily news podcast. Host Michael Barbaro talks with health reporter Nina Agrawal about a first of its kind vaccine that treats cancer instead of preventing it: a personalized mRNA vaccine for melanoma, developed by Merck and Moderna, that in a clinical trial made patients far less likely to see their cancer return. The episode also tells the longer story behind the result, a century of failed attempts to turn the immune system against cancer, and asks where the approach could go next.

The reason a cancer vaccine has been so elusive is that cancer is not one disease in the way flu or COVID is. Vaccines against infectious disease are given to healthy people to prevent an illness, and they work because the target is broadly the same from person to person. Cancer is the opposite. It grows out of a person's own cells, and one person's cancer is not exactly the same as the next person's, so there has been no easy way to immunize everyone against the possibility that a cell goes rogue. The breakthrough Agrawal describes is a vaccine built around that individuality: it is made for one patient's specific cancer, and it is given after diagnosis, as a treatment rather than a shield.

The trial enrolled more than eleven hundred patients with stage two, three, or four melanoma whose tumors had been surgically removed. Little disease was left in their bodies, but they were at high risk of the cancer coming back, which made it the right window to vaccinate. When a tumor was removed, researchers sequenced its DNA and catalogued its mutations. Because a melanoma cell can carry hundreds of mutations, they then predicted which of them produce proteins that sit on the cell surface and look to the immune system like enemy flags. They chose up to thirty-four, and built a vaccine carrying mRNA, the body's own instruction molecules, encoding those flags. The patient's cells produce the flagged proteins, and the immune system learns to hunt down any cell displaying them.

Patients received nine doses of the vaccine, one every three weeks, together with a checkpoint inhibitor drug, and the side effects were mild for a cancer therapy: a sore arm, or flu-like symptoms for a day or two. After two years of follow up, the vaccinated patients were forty-two percent less likely to have their cancer come back than those who did not get the vaccine, and if it did return, it was less likely to spread through the body and become metastatic. Experts Agrawal spoke with put that result on par with some of the field's biggest breakthroughs. The caveat is time. Two years of data cannot show whether the vaccine extends overall survival, and researchers will follow the patients for years to see whether the benefit holds. On the strength of the results so far, Merck and Moderna are planning to apply for approval from the Food and Drug Administration.

The idea itself is more than a hundred years old. In the 1890s, a bone surgeon named Dr. William Coley read about a patient whose tumor shrank on its own after a bacterial infection and fever, and began injecting bacterial mixtures into tumors, apparently reasoning that the bacteria had something to do with the cancer. Some of his patients did see their tumors regress, probably because the bacteria provoked an immune response right where the tumor sat. Coley did not understand the mechanism, the work was dismissed as quackery, and the idea was largely forgotten as radiation and then chemotherapy arrived.

What revived it was a series of advances that built on each other. From the 1980s, researchers developed immunotherapy, ways of turning the immune system up against cancer, with the first such treatment approved around 1986. Sequencing a genome, once a years long project costing hundreds of millions of dollars, can now be done in days or weeks for hundreds of dollars, which is what makes reading an individual tumor's mutations practical. Checkpoint inhibitors taught doctors to take the brakes off the immune system, brakes that cancer cells hijack to hide; earlier vaccines could stir a response in a dish yet deliver no clinical benefit, in part because the immune system stayed inhibited. And the COVID pandemic taught Moderna in particular how to manufacture mRNA vaccines quickly and at scale, which is what makes a customized vaccine for each patient feasible.

Whether this travels beyond melanoma is the open scientific question. Melanoma is an unusually mutation rich cancer, which gives the immune system a lot of flags to aim at, and what works there may not work for breast, colon, or pancreatic cancers. Trials in other cancers are underway, and the most closely watched is in pancreatic cancer, where a small study of sixteen patients found that those who received an mRNA vaccine after surgery were less likely to have the cancer return within the period studied. A larger randomized trial is now running to test that early signal properly.

Cost hangs over all of it. A vaccine manufactured for a single person cannot be cheap, though Agrawal notes that cancer therapies in general already run into the hundreds of thousands of dollars. Alongside the bespoke approach, researchers are pursuing off the shelf vaccines aimed at prevention in people at high risk, such as those with genetic syndromes that predispose them to colon cancer, carriers of BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations, or people with precancerous polyps, built from proteins common across a type of cancer. That work is years away and may fail, but it points at the field's real ambition: moving from preventing a cancer's return to preventing it from ever forming at all.

Open questions

  1. The trial shows the vaccine cuts melanoma recurrence over two years, but there is no long term survival data yet. Will the benefit hold as the follow up lengthens, and will regulators approve the vaccine on recurrence data while the survival answer is still years away?
  2. Melanoma carries an unusual number of mutations for the immune system to target. Can the same approach work in cancers with far fewer flags to aim at, such as pancreatic, breast, or colon cancer, or will each cancer need a different version of the idea?
  3. A vaccine built for one patient at a time is inherently expensive and slow to make. Can manufacturing get fast and cheap enough for this to matter beyond a fortunate few, and will off the shelf preventive vaccines for high risk people arrive in time to change the economics?

The Royal Society · Håstad on 40 years of complexity theory

Highlights from 40 years of complexity theory | The Royal Society

On The Royal Society, “Highlights from 40 years of complexity theory” is the Milner Lecture by Johan Håstad, who took his PhD in 1986 and has spent the forty years since working mostly on the hard side of computational complexity: proving not that problems can be solved, but that they cannot be solved efficiently. The lecture is a personal tour of the field for a mixed audience, and it closes with the award of the Royal Society Milner Medal, whose citation credits his sustained transformational impact across circuit complexity, cryptography, parallel computing, and approximate optimization.

Complexity theory, as he frames it, is about using computers efficiently: not building them, but designing algorithms on paper and counting the elementary operations they need. Its results touch daily life at both ends of the spectrum, from the route a phone computes across a strange city within a second to the question of whether a powerful adversary can break the encryption protecting banking and national security communications. Every problem has two sides. Proving a problem easy takes a single good algorithm, designed and analyzed. Proving one hard means ruling out every possible algorithm, and that asymmetry is where most of his own results live.

He warms up with multiplication. The schoolbook method takes about n squared operations for numbers with n digits, and the obvious argument that it cannot be beaten, that every digit must be multiplied by every other digit, is, he says, a bogus argument, convincing only by intimidation. Treating the digits as a vector and passing through the Fourier transform, an approach from the 1970s, multiplies two billion digit numbers in about a minute where the schoolbook method would take about a year. Later papers trimmed the cost to n log n, and everyone believed that was the final answer. Then, he says, OpenAI published a tiny improvement in October showing that n log n was not optimal after all: irrelevant in practice, but a genuine surprise. Whether numbers can be multiplied in a small constant multiple of n operations remains open, and proving it would be a great advance.

Factoring tells the opposite story, of a problem whose hardness is useful. It underpins the RSA cryptosystem of 1977, named for Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman, whose original paper judged 200 digit numbers safe for a long time. Smart algorithms kept eroding that margin. Factoring a 100 digit number now takes about an hour on a standard computer, and John Pollard's number field sieve, from 1988, forced key sizes up from 200 digits toward 600. The 200 digit choice was in the end safe for only about 30 years. Peter Shor then showed that a quantum computer could factor efficiently, so if large quantum computers are built, RSA falls, and the internet is already changing its cryptosystems in response. Håstad pauses on the irony: some of the field's most beautiful algorithms have made the internet less secure, and he asks whether that counts as progress.

In theory the field sorts problems into a black and white world: polynomial running times are good, exponential ones are bad. The traveling salesperson problem, finding the shortest tour through Sweden's 100 biggest cities and back, shows why brute force fails, since trying every order means 99 factorial possibilities. Thousands of problems, the salesperson problem among them, are NP-complete, meaning an efficient algorithm for any one of them would solve them all. Decades of failure have convinced the field that P is not equal to NP, a Millennium Problem that Håstad treats as something close to a law of nature, while admitting that current techniques are nowhere near proving it. The NP-complete problems also appear to stay hard for quantum computers. His own single NP-completeness result, from 1990, showed that computing tensor rank is NP-complete; it was ignored for twenty years, then became relevant to machine learning, and is now one of his most cited papers.

When exact solutions are out of reach, the field asks how close an efficient algorithm can guarantee to get. For the salesperson problem, Christofides' algorithm from the 1970s guarantees a tour within 50 percent of optimal, and it was beaten only in 2020, by a small margin, and by human researchers working before the recent AI results. On the hardness side, Håstad's own specialty, getting within roughly one percent of optimal is already hard in general. The distance between the 50 percent guarantee and the hardness near one percent is exactly the kind of gap the field wants to close into an exact threshold.

His favorite theorems are sharper. In the problem known as MAX-3SAT, each clause joins three true or false variables, and the goal is to satisfy as many clauses as possible. Assigning every variable at random already satisfies seven eighths of the clauses on average. His theorem says that is the best any efficient algorithm can do: doing even slightly better than seven eighths is NP-hard. A problem that looks almost simple is understood exactly, and the strategy of not thinking at all turns out to be optimal.

Systems of linear equations mod two, where one plus one equals zero, can be solved exactly by Gaussian elimination, a method known in China two thousand years ago and named for Gauss. But corrupt just one percent of the equations with errors and elimination collapses, because the errors propagate through everything. Another of his favorite results says that with such errors present, doing better than random guessing is NP-complete: Gaussian elimination, when it works, is really all there is.

Max-Cut, splitting a graph's vertices into two groups so as to cut as many edges as possible, is the happy counterexample. The algorithm of Goemans and Williamson relaxes the problem into one about unit vectors, solves that relaxed problem exactly, and rounds the answer back with a random cut through the space, guaranteeing about 0.878 of the best possible cut. Subhash Khot's Unique Games Conjecture implied that this constant is optimal, that no efficient algorithm can do better. The community was divided over the conjecture for two decades, and Håstad himself spent the fall of 2025 trying to disprove it.

In October, he says, OpenAI proved the conjecture true. The batch of results that appeared around October 6 also included the small improvement to multiplication. The proof was very poorly written, he concedes under questioning from the audience, but he believes it is correct, and it can be formalized in the Lean proof system. AI, in his assessment, is now good at solving problems but not yet good at writing papers, and the field has gained new theorems without clearly gaining understanding.

What happens next, he says, could go two ways: a golden era in which AI rapidly vetoes bad ideas with counterexamples and progress accelerates, or an era of proofs, and eventually theorems, too hard for humans to understand. The audience questions kept returning to the shock. A subexponential algorithm for 3SAT, another very recent result, is barely subexponential and changes little in practice, but it undercuts the exponential time hypothesis that hundreds of papers relied on. On scale he is blunt: exponential growth is really exponential, and all the world's data centers together will never perform two to the 256 operations, so systems like AES stay beyond brute force. Asked how researchers know when a problem is ripe, he recalled his advisor Lennart Carleson calling the choice between persisting and giving up one of the field's great mysteries. He ends hoping that humans remain a big part of the next forty years.

Open questions

  1. If AI systems keep settling the field's landmark conjectures, as Håstad says OpenAI did with the Unique Games Conjecture, what happens to understanding: does a machine checked but humanly unreadable proof change what researchers should work on, and who checks the checking as proofs are formalized in systems like Lean?
  2. The lecture treats P not equal to NP as close to a law of nature while conceding that a proof is far beyond current techniques. Will AI assisted proving change that, or will the field keep building cathedrals on an assumption it cannot prove?
  3. Factoring's history is a warning about safety margins: RSA's 200 digits were safe for about thirty years, not forever. As quantum computing and AI driven algorithm design advance together, how much margin do today's systems really have, and who is measuring it?

Matthew Berman · Box CEO Aaron Levie on AI agents

Box CEO Explains How AI Is Changing Software

On Matthew Berman, “Box CEO Explains How AI Is Changing Software” is a conversation recorded at BoxWorks, the company's annual event, between the host and Aaron Levie, the co-founder and chief executive of Box. Levie argues that AI agents will soon outnumber people inside organizations by a wide margin, and that the durable value in software will sit in the governed layer of context underneath those agents, which is the ground Box has occupied for two decades. Along the way he explains why Box keeps throwing away its own unfinished work, why he expects token usage to grow enormously, and why he is firmly committed to open weights models.

The frame is a prediction Satya Nadella made a couple of years ago: software sorts into two layers, agents on top and ground truth context below. Levie thinks it has roughly played out. Citing Jensen Huang's on-stage estimate, he expects ten to a hundred times more agents than people, on the order of thirty to fifty billion agents processing information inside organizations. Something has to set the guardrails for all of them: what CRM, ERP, lakehouse, and unstructured file data each agent may touch. That context layer, he insists, cannot be a giant blob of information. It needs access controls, data governance, metadata so agents can find and route to the right thing, and proper sandboxing. In his telling, today's systems of record become systems of intelligence that control that access layer, while still keeping a user interface, because people have to put data into the system and interact with it somehow.

Box's own posture is partner-centric to a degree Levie calls religious. Contracts should surface in Salesforce, supply chain documents in SAP or Oracle, marketing plans inside ChatGPT or Claude: the information flows to wherever the work is done. Against that backdrop, the day's headline announcement is Box Architect, an autonomous system for designing, building, running, and eventually optimizing the agents, workflows, and apps an enterprise builds on Box. A customer asks for a contract management, supply chain document review, or invoice processing solution, and a frontier model designs the whole thing from Box's building blocks instead of the customer assembling three to five separate tools. Levie stresses that Box is genuinely indifferent between customers using Architect and customers running Box headless behind some other technology. The full stack exists because many customers will never do the modular assembly themselves.

Architect began with an internal demo six to nine months earlier, when an engineer pointed the then-current models at designing a complete Box app and the agent did in thirty seconds what customers spend two days thinking about, whiteboarding, and architecting. As the models improved sharply over the summer at tool use and computer use, and posted strong results on enterprise workflow benchmarks, Box's conclusion changed shape: rather than dropping agents into a process exactly as it exists today, let AI design a better process to begin with. Levie notes the pattern arrived first in customer support, where Decagon, Sierra, and Finn each help a support operations person design the agents that serve customers. Architect generalizes that idea to any business process, from customer onboarding to loan processing to client intake, and in time it will watch a workflow's failures and improve the design on its own.

Underneath these product calls sits a philosophy Levie describes with a laugh: he is probably in the upper limit of bitter lesson pilled. The working assumption is that models will keep getting dramatically smarter, so Box should build only what stays necessary in that world: data governance, very strong security, high-speed and high-throughput infrastructure agents can call, and clean schemas an agent can use to create objects in the system. Everything else is scaffolding that a better model will eventually absorb. A consequence he has fully accepted is that in a long list of future scenarios nobody will ever see Box's interface, so the company has to produce substantial value in a world where it has none. He compares the shift to Amazon's API-first mandate of fifteen years ago, and jokes that he should probably write the bitter lesson memo and publish it.

The cost of that philosophy is churn. Asked how often Box throws things away, Levie answers: all the time. The company has built capabilities it never released because a new model made them useless before they could ship. Teams have spent three months building a layer, only to scrap it entirely when the models became good at tool use. You are building on quicksand, he says, or on a treadmill, and the only workable response is to be very unsentimental about anything the model is about to do better.

His token thesis follows from the agent count. Within roughly three years, he expects nearly every piece of information produced to be seen, reviewed, and processed by several agents. A salesperson's customer gets an always-on, agent-built profile of past touch points, buying priorities, and conference appearances. A strange login or an unusual device access spins up agents to review what just happened. Every code push gets agents that inspect it and simulate its risks elsewhere in the codebase. Supply chain documents, CAD designs, supplier contracts, and invoices get read by agents that extract the key details and pump them back into the ERP. By his estimate we are perhaps a hundredth of the way into how many interactions will have agents attached, and the result is a growth in token usage he has elsewhere put at roughly a thousandfold.

He is not, though, an advocate of spending tokens indiscriminately. The correct application of tokens matters most, he says: being judicious about where you apply force, in a targeted way that aligns with strategy, because unfocused compute can veer a company down the wrong path as easily as it can help. What he does believe is that the amount of compute a company throws at worthy problems becomes one of the defining competitive advantages of the future. People are finite, since companies tap out on how many engineers they can hire and train, while agents are bounded mainly by the size of data centers. That asymmetry excites him and worries him in the same breath, because an advantage denominated in capital can concentrate power and starve innovation. It is also why the AI labs' position as gatekeepers of tokens concerns him, and why open weights matter to him as the marketplace's escape valve.

On pricing, Levie expects the seat to survive alongside new consumption components. As long as people work with data and agents, a seat still buys a company welcome predictability, while agents doing variable amounts of work cannot be priced in advance. Berman puts the CFO's problem plainly: today you cannot predict what a task will cost in tokens, a sentence he says he can hear a thousand CFOs shiver at. Levie's answer is time. Three to five years out, once run rates are clearer, token spending becomes normal budget planning, a line item a company raises or lowers like its marketing programs. In the meantime, the lever vendors control is efficiency, and Box's is the context retrieval engine it built with NVIDIA, announced the same day, which lets agents pull only the parts of the data they actually need, so customers spend fewer tokens processing material that was never relevant.

The open versus closed question gets his most counterintuitive answer. Box offers both open weights and closed models, sets defaults by evaluation, and aims eventually at an adaptive routing layer that picks the best model for each query at the lowest cost. He expects the big closed labs to remain great businesses even as open models absorb most of the volume, and reaches for a database analogy he admits is imperfect: after about 2005 nearly every startup ran on an open source database, yet Oracle still became a three hundred billion dollar company in that same period. Open and closed, in his experience, grow in tandem and reinforce each other. Cheap open weights can even increase frontier spending, because a company that can afford to run open models over every document and transaction can suddenly afford a frontier model as the orchestrator above that volume, work it could never have funded at frontier prices alone.

The economics of open weights, he concedes, only work for whoever can monetize inference. A company giving weights away has to ride the curve of more inference being good, which is why Meta and Google can sustain open releases, and why NVIDIA, sitting one step lower in the stack, can too. He is untroubled that the open releases trail the frontier by a generation, roughly three to six months: by his rough estimate, the frontier models of March this year had already fully solved the great majority of IT ticketing work, so a model at that level, freely available, covers an enormous amount of the world's token work. The NVIDIA partnership puts an embeddings model under Box's retrieval pre-indexing, improving ranking and relevance and returning token-efficient chunks to agents, rolling out in the Enterprise Advanced edition over the coming months. And while Box still rides the gains from other people's models, Levie calls post-training on enterprise knowledge inevitable for some use cases, naming Prime Intellect, Applied Compute, and Fireworks AI in an ecosystem he wants to work with rather than build against.

Open questions

  1. If the durable value in software migrates to a governed context layer that agents query invisibly, what happens to the product interface as a competitive weapon, and how does a company like Box keep customers loyal to software they may rarely open?
  2. Levie's token thesis assumes companies will learn to point compute only at worthy problems. Who inside a company makes that call, and what prevents token budgets from becoming the next cloud bill: large, volatile, and understood only in hindsight?
  3. His escape valve depends on a few very large companies choosing to subsidize open weights out of inference revenue. If Meta, Google, or NVIDIA ever change that calculation, does the valve close, and what would enterprise buyers do then?

ShipOS Supplier Spotlight | Connected Production Planning at Penn Machine Works and US Drop Forge

On Palantir, “ShipOS Supplier Spotlight | Connected Production Planning at Penn Machine Works and US Drop Forge” is a supplier webinar about the United States Navy's program to deploy Palantir Foundry across the submarine industrial base. The session pairs the people running ShipOS with one supplier family, Penn Machine Works and US Drop Forge, that now plans production for both companies inside the platform, and it is built around a demonstration of a single disruption moving through the two connected plants.

Sam Rye, who leads strategic projects in the Department of the Navy's submarine program, hosts the session for an audience of priority suppliers whose work directly affects submarine delivery schedules. She lays out the program's reach: ShipOS works with the two prime shipbuilders, Electric Boat and Newport News, with all four public shipyards, and with up to 100 suppliers. Participation is accelerating, she says, and the program expects to hit capacity by the end of the year.

Brian Fabian, the president of both companies, introduces them as a vertically integrated supplier of forged machined fittings for the US Navy and the broader maritime industrial base. US Drop Forge's forge shop in New Jersey produces closed-die forgings that feed directly into Penn Machine's machining operations in Aston, Pennsylvania, giving the pair full control of the process from raw material to the final part. Penn Machine is one of the shipbuilders' largest suppliers by volume of forged fittings and components, and its Navy business has more than doubled over the last three years.

Fabian describes a cautious start. The companies were interested in ShipOS but unsure how the program's data sharing requirements would benefit their business, and what settled the question was learning that they remain in full control of the data that is shared. The ShipOS team came and spent a day learning how the business operates, and returned within 24 hours with an initial solution. The engagement began with Penn Machine alone, then expanded during the engineering surge once it became clear how critical US Drop Forge is to Penn Machine's Navy business. Both companies now have ShipOS production planning deployed.

Cara Lee, the ShipOS engineering lead at Palantir, explains the problem the software is meant to solve. The submarine supply chain is extremely complex and interdependent, inputs are always changing, and many suppliers do not have software that can handle the intricacies of their specific operations. Planners end up spending a disproportionate amount of time manually reflecting updates and tracking their downstream effects, working reactively, putting out the latest fire instead of acting early on long-term delivery risks.

ShipOS production planning addresses that by automatically slotting operations into the schedule based on the operation sequence, job due dates, and work center capacity. When something changes, an operation completing, a new job being created, a delay in material arriving, or a change in available capacity, the system reflects the impact automatically and alerts planners to what needs their attention. Lee claims three results: a reliable, actionable picture of what a supplier can deliver and when; earlier warning, because a change propagates through dependent operations and surfaces delivery risks before they become late; and more options to respond, since planners can adjust capacity, reassign operations, or reprioritize jobs while there is still time to change the outcome. Inside one company's four walls that is already valuable. When suppliers connect to each other, updates flow automatically across the network, and a shock that one supplier cannot absorb on its own becomes visible to its customers early enough for them to respond.

Tyler Bedillion, a production planner at US Drop Forge, demonstrates at the working level. He first describes the old way: scheduling ran on gut feel and manual management on top of stale data, the ERP system's estimated dates did not account for real capacity, and the real schedule lived in spreadsheets and in people's heads, so every time he moved one job he had to manually push everything behind it. In the demonstration, which he notes uses notional data, he opens a job whose forge operation is scheduled to start almost a full month after the prior operation finishes. The capacity view shows why: the work center it is assigned to, Hammer 4, will be down for regular maintenance for the entire month, while Hammer 5 is underutilized. He moves the operation to Hammer 5, the schedule updates right away, and the job is no longer behind schedule, with every operation after it rescheduled accordingly. He calls the ability to confirm that a job ending months from now is going to be late, and to get what he needs to fix it, a total game changer, and says he now spends more of his time on higher value engineering work.

His second example cannot be fixed inside his own plant. An outside heat treat vendor will not complete an operation, on a job US Drop Forge is delivering to Penn Machine, in the week it is scheduled, so Bedillion pushes the operation a week later and the job goes late, with no way to absorb the delay. What he can do is make sure his customer knows as soon as he does. Tom Bytheway, the naval project manager at Penn Machine Works, picks up the same event from the other side. Roughly 65 percent of the material for Penn Machine's Navy jobs is procured from US Drop Forge, and he used to spend a lot of his time, and Bedillion's, calling the forge to ask when jobs would be done so he could estimate when material would arrive. That guesswork is gone: the forge's delivery forecast now feeds directly into his schedule, and changes show up automatically. In his Interplant Supply view, which tracks the dependencies between the two companies, a notification shows the outside operation moving a week and one of his own jobs going late as a result. Because he knows so far in advance, he adds a temporary capacity override, an extra shift taking a lathe to 18 hours a day for the month of December, and the schedule updates with his job back on track to finish on time. What used to be days of back and forth just to identify a problem, he says, happened in minutes, and that visibility changes how confidently Penn Machine can stand behind its commitments to Electric Boat.

Fabian then describes the business impact across both organizations. A disruption too large for one supplier to absorb still ended in on-time delivery to the shipbuilder, because the connected schedule let the customer react. The platform has streamlined communication between the two companies, giving both a shared real-time view of the work moving within each plant and how the streams connect, and it has made them a better supplier to their shipbuilding customers by allowing reliable delivery forecasts. The ShipOS team, he says, has been a partner rather than a box checker. The engagement is still expanding, from Penn Machine to US Drop Forge and now to their sister company, Western Forge and Flange. His advice to suppliers with reservations is to trust what they heard firsthand and just get started.

Lee closes the loop with what joining the program looks like. There are four phases: contracting, when the legal paperwork is signed and a supplier formally becomes a participant; onboarding, when the technical foundations are put in place, the environment, access, and system connections; the engineering surge, when Palantir and the supplier develop and deploy a use case together, chosen through a collaborative matching process between how the supplier's business operates and the use cases that have worked before; and sustainment, continued adoption and extension of the platform as the supplier's needs evolve. She points to the previous month's webinar with AT&F, on a contract review workflow, as another example of a matched use case, and gives the contact address, shipos@palantir.com. The program, she says, is expecting to reach capacity by January and wants to fill the remaining spots.

Rye has the last word. Penn Machine, she notes, has helped bring two of its critical sub-tier suppliers into the program, US Drop Forge and Western Forge and Flange, and connecting US Drop Forge's delivery information to Penn Machine took just one email, with the Palantir team able to start within a day. Her message to suppliers already participating is to sign their data sharing agreements, share data actively, and lean in; her message to those not yet participating is to take the next step. The effort, she says, is about helping suppliers succeed so the industrial base can collectively deliver on the Navy's mission.

Open questions

  1. The program expects to reach capacity by January, with up to 100 suppliers inside a submarine supply base that runs to thousands of companies. What happens to priority suppliers who miss the window, and what does onboarding demand of a small shop in cost, systems, and staff time?
  2. The demonstration runs on data the panel states is notional, and every result in this session is reported by the Navy, by Palantir, or by the supplier being showcased. Where is the independent evidence, in delivered boats or shortened lead times, that connected planning is moving the Columbia and Virginia schedules themselves?
  3. The model depends on suppliers streaming production data to their customers and to each other. Fabian says his companies remain in full control of the data they share, but what does that control mean in practice once a supplier's dates are feeding a customer's plan: can a supplier limit, correct, or withdraw data it has already shared?
