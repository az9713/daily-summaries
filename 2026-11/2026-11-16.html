Golden Gate Institute for AI · Jacob Tsimerman on what AI does to mathematics

Jacob Tsimerman on AI can supercharge mathematics - what is the end state? | The Curve 2026

On Golden Gate Institute for AI, “Jacob Tsimerman on AI can supercharge mathematics - what is the end state? | The Curve 2026” is a talk by Jacob Tsimerman, a research mathematician who has lately pivoted to AI safety, about what it feels like inside mathematics now that AI has, in his words, come for the field at full throttle. He opens with a joke about the previous speaker's poll on AI risk, and with a plug for a group he recently co-founded with fellow mathematicians and other theoreticians working on AI safety, which he calls more important than the talk itself. Then he turns to the state of his old profession.

His central claim is blunt: AI is here for mathematics, and it is now better than mathematicians at doing math. He has done math his whole life, and for basically any problem he wants to solve, the fastest route is to work with AI. It is not yet a replacement for him, he says, but it is better than him, and he is definitely better with it than without it.

The community has found this hard to absorb, because for many mathematicians the subject is closer to a spiritual or artistic pursuit than a job. Doing math is what you are, not just what you do, and people find meaning in it in their spare time. The field was also slow to see the change coming. He recounts a long period of goalpost shifting after ChatGPT: the models could speak but could not do math, since there was supposedly no training data, then they could do math but not hard math, and so on.

What finally made people take notice, he says, is that AI has solved Navier-Stokes, one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, the small set of problems mathematicians enshrined at the start of the millennium as the most important in the field. Only one of them had ever been solved before. Now, he says, major conjectures that would normally take years or decades to resolve are being resolved every day, and journals, conferences, and the archive are overwhelmed by the number of submissions. The field is not keeping up with the changes.

The reception is split. One camp holds that what is happening is simply not good: humans understand less even though more knowledge is out there, PhD training systems are breaking, and some people have gone so far as to sign letters saying mathematicians should not use AI for mathematics and companies should stop doing mathematics using AI. Tsimerman places himself in the other camp. This is ultimately a good thing for mathematics, he argues, but it requires a change of perspective. It is no longer true that a person can grow up to become a mathematician who proves a major conjecture and enshrines their name in the history books. That time has passed, which is a bit sad, but far more interesting math results are arriving, and mathematicians will learn how to dissect them, engage with them, and adapt. Between the camps sits a confused middle of PhD students and young faculty who trained their whole lives for work that is being upturned beneath them.

He is often asked whether a PhD still makes sense, and his answer is that it does not, at least not the way it used to be done. The old paradigm took very talented young students and made each of them a deep expert in one narrow area, inside an economy of specialists who knew other things. That no longer works, he says, because by the time a student finishes learning their narrow thing, AI will have moved the field generations ahead, and that particular skill set will be too narrow. At the very least, people should stop doing what they were doing in the way they used to do it.

He then describes his own path with the tools. Four years ago he was using AI as a curiosity. Two years ago it became good enough that he was learning subjects outside his expertise exclusively through AI systems. Six months ago he was doing research with the models, including an episode where he asked one to check a proof he had written down informally. The model insisted there was a mistake. He said there was not. The model was right. It could not fix the proof, but he could, and he calls that the kind of interaction people now go through every day.

A month ago, working on open source game theory research relevant to AI safety, he ran things very differently. He handed the models an idea and a paper he thought was relevant, had them spawn ten sub-agents to interact in a set way, and got results back by the morning: work he estimates would have taken him a month, done in a few days, a speedup he puts at five to ten times. The catch is oversight. He has to check carefully that the output is correct, that it is relevant, that it is comprehensible, and that it is actually what he asked for and not something else. He is not currently doing pure mathematics professionally, having pivoted to AI safety, but if he were, he says, his entire workflow would be upended: he would be programming AI harnesses all day, every day.

Looking ahead, he predicts that the current landscape of open problems and researchers developing theories around them will all be resolved within five years, with roughly five normal generations of mathematics occurring in that span at a far faster rate than before. AI will get good at explaining, digesting, and curating results, until there is effectively a math bot that does mathematics all the time and writes it up.

He also thinks science should revisit how ambitious it is. There is a joke that all physics is math, all chemistry is physics, and all biology is chemistry. That is not really a joke, he says, it is a fact of life. Each field works at its own level because the level below is too hard to deal with directly: physics is done as physics because the mathematical model is too hard to use. That might not be true anymore once we have systems sufficiently more intelligent than humans, and these are things we should be revisiting.

His last proposal, which he calls his wonkiest and most out there view, is that philosophy should be revisited too. We should be developing theories of love, theories of attention, and theories of value. Such proposals normally draw cynicism, he notes, because the people offering them are usually naive and have not grappled with the difficulties involved. But he thinks we all have to revisit our assumptions, and he closes on exactly that line.

Open questions

  1. Tsimerman says the time has passed when a human could grow up to prove a major conjecture and enter the history books. If AI systems produce the results, what does mathematical understanding consist of now, and who or what gets the credit?
  2. He argues the traditional PhD, deep training in one narrow specialty, no longer makes sense because AI moves the field too fast. What should replace it, and what happens to the students and young faculty caught in the middle of the change?
  3. His five year prediction assumes the flood of AI results can be checked, digested, and curated as fast as it arrives, yet he also says journals, conferences, and the archive are already overwhelmed. What institution, human or machine, verifies the new mathematics, and what happens to results nobody has verified?

Joe Maddalone · Joe Maddalone on senior engineers, AI, and the software factory

Senior Engineers Are Using AI Wrong and Software Factories Are Dumb

On Joe Maddalone, “Senior Engineers Are Using AI Wrong and Software Factories Are Dumb” is a video essay about the mental models engineers carry into the AI era. Maddalone opens with the computing history books on his shelf and a pattern he finds in them: there is always somebody who misses what is coming because they cannot see beyond what they already know.

His first example is Marvin Minsky, a pioneer of artificial intelligence featured in the book Genius Makers. Minsky was deeply skeptical of neural networks, mostly because he could not imagine that a machine could produce a new idea it was not explicitly taught first. Maddalone says Minsky pretty much single-handedly caused the AI winter, and calls it a great example of how even brilliant people struggle to imagine a future that does not fit their current mental model.

He contrasts two kinds of software books. Martin Fowler's Refactoring is a beloved text, but mostly a recipe book: pull it off the shelf, look up the technique, apply it, put it back. The Phoenix Project and The Pragmatic Programmer work differently, teaching the principles of software development through parables and metaphors about how to think, how to approach problems, and how to make better decisions.

Metaphors are the essay's hinge. We understand new technology by comparing it to the world we already know, which is why engine power is still measured in horsepower and why computers still pretend to be little desks, with documents, file cabinets, and a trash can. The universal save icon is a floppy disk that many adults today have never seen in person, yet they know what it means. The metaphor has outlived the thing it represents.

That inheritance has a cost. A metaphor shapes a mental model, the mental model shapes habits, and when the mental model is wrong, the habits are wrong. Maddalone compares old design decisions to training wheels: rely on them long enough and you may forget they are removable, and find yourself defending choices made in the 1970s and 1980s as though they were laws of physics.

His economic argument follows. Many respected software engineering practices were derived from the cost of developing, maintaining, and coordinating software written by human beings. AI is fundamentally changing those economics, which should mean some best practices stop making sense. Yet whenever he looks at a vibe-coded app, he sees the exact same best practices implemented, which he takes as a signal that engineers are not ready to let go of the old mental models, himself included.

The tradition he names is the developer as craftsman, the philosophy of The Pragmatic Programmer: hand-polishing every function, obsessing over clean code, adhering to rules like DRY, don't repeat yourself. That made sense when every character was typed by a human hand and every bug meant a human had to read that exact code. But if AI can refactor or discard 500 lines of boilerplate in seconds, the cost of duplication and the cost of abstraction both change, and spending three hours hand-polishing a class hierarchy is not craft anymore. It is nostalgia.

His replacement image is the urban planner. Engineers are not woodworkers now; a planner does not care about the grain of wood on a park bench, but about traffic flow and utility capacity. Planners set boundaries, enforce constraints, and direct growth. When a tool writes the code, an engineer's value is no longer clean syntax. It is the ability to express clear intent, evaluate tradeoffs, and guide growth, a role now being called design engineering. Anyone still hand carving wood, he says, is focused on a bench while the city around them burns.

He applies the same thinking to how code is organized. For decades we have pretended software lives in physical file cabinets, building directories and nested paths to support a spatial mental model in which understanding a system means knowing where things live. Large language models and vector databases do not care that a helper function is buried three levels deep in a folder called utils. They evaluate code by semantic context, what it means and how it relates to the problem, regardless of where it sits on disk. Engineers say they do not read the code anymore, yet spend energy getting AI to organize it around a crutch invented in 1985 to solve a human short-term memory problem. The field has not moved from the spatial model to the semantic one.

Then he turns to the narrative he expects to lose people over. The current discourse holds that AI tools are only useful if you are already a senior developer, because you need the background to catch the AI's mistakes and the classic patterns to keep the system on the rails. The loudest voices, he says, are senior engineers demonstrating how much faster they can execute the things they already know. He calls that a trap. A seasoned developer tends to use AI as a hyper-efficient autocomplete for their own mental models, writing the same design patterns and architectures they have written since 2008, only faster. They do not reimagine the work; they supercharge the baggage they bring to it.

The people who move the field into its next era, he argues, will be the ones without that baggage: people who do not know what a floppy disk is, who feel no attachment to DRY or traditional refactoring, who do not have to unlearn decades of dogma, and who are free to invent new ways of working with code from scratch.

The final trap is the software factory. For about twenty years the industry has tried to turn development into an assembly line, and it has half succeeded: CI/CD works, and testing, building, and deployment run like a factory floor. The dangerous leap is extending that model to the entire life cycle, an assembly line populated by AI agents trained on old mental models, where a product manager drops in a specification and a finished product rolls out the other side. That picture is attractive if you believe a developer's edge is speed of implementation. Maddalone says it is completely wrong: the modern developer's edge is taste, verification, and edge case evaluation.

He supports the point with an article by Rob West, published about a month earlier, arguing that the agented software factory is a wrong metaphor. As Maddalone paraphrases it: a factory assumes design is solved, but figuring out what to build and how it should behave is the work, and a ticket is not a blueprint. Building software changes our understanding of the specification as we go, so design engineering evolves the spec rather than consuming it. An assembly line exists to eliminate variation, while design engineering benefits from variation, because comparing alternatives is how better approaches are found. And a factory has to be fed, which pressures teams to formalize requirements before they understand the problem; the ambiguity does not disappear, and the agents fill the gaps with confident best guesses and produce the wrong thing.

His verdict is that the problem is not the technology. It is applying a 19th-century industrial mental model to a 21st-century cognitive shift. Just as Minsky could not see past his assumptions about how machines would learn, engineers risk using groundbreaking technology to do the wrong things faster. The classics got the field here and supplied the metaphors it needed for its first era, but hanging onto them now will leave engineers standing on an assembly line, producing artifacts for a world that has already moved on.

Open questions

  1. Maddalone argues that many best practices were really answers to the cost of human-written code, but he does not sort them. Which practices were only ever cost workarounds, and which ones protect something the cost argument never touched, such as correctness or security?
  2. He says the modern developer's edge is taste, verification, and edge case evaluation, and also that senior engineers mostly spend their AI leverage on old patterns. If neither the veterans nor the newcomers are practicing that edge, where does it get learned?
  3. He rejects the factory as the organizing metaphor for AI-built software but offers the urban planner mainly as an image for individual work. What replaces the factory at the level of a whole team or company, and who owns quality inside that model?

Joe Maddalone · Joe Maddalone on local AI hype and tribalism

Local AI is Overhyped and Tribalism is Ruining It

On Joe Maddalone, “Local AI is Overhyped and Tribalism is Ruining It” is an argument against picking teams in AI. Maddalone opens with Steve Jobs, who by the account in Walter Isaacson's biography was cruel, manipulative, and difficult, but was also the person who got things done. You do not have to like his personality, Maddalone says, to appreciate the products and technologies he brought into existence.

He applies the same test to Sam Altman, Dario Amodei, and Elon Musk. All three have made spectacularly questionable public statements, but their companies are producing the best AI products available today, and pretending otherwise would be silly. Those companies are not shy about their ambitions either: they want their products embedded in your workflows, your business, and eventually almost everything you do. That is not an indictment of the technology, he says, just something to be aware of before deciding how much of your profession to build on top of it. But if you cannot appreciate what these companies have built without becoming a cheerleader for their business model, that is tribalism.

The mirror image bothers him just as much. Maddalone has made a ton of videos about open source and local AI projects, Ollama, llama.cpp, open weights, and other tools, and he plans to keep making them, because working with them has taught him more about machine learning principles and how language models are actually trained than a glorified chatbot would. The people on those projects are thoughtful and intelligent. But the product experience from the open source tools is simply not keeping up with the big AI companies, and pretending otherwise would be just as silly. If you cannot appreciate the open source mission without becoming an apologist for an ecosystem that has yet to deliver on its promise, that is tribalism too. We owe those projects no more loyalty than we owe the big companies.

Tribalism would be harmless if it stayed online, but it creeps into real decisions. People spend more on hardware than is financially sensible because they have decided cloud inference is inherently bad. Others refuse to look at a local solution because they have decided cloud inference is inherently better, or refuse to use a model because it was made by a company or a country they do not like.

He does not think this happens by accident. Newcomers arrive to find new models every week, benchmarks they cannot possibly understand, and acronyms stacked on acronyms, so they ask what they should use, what they should buy, and whether it is worth it. An entire industry has popped up around that question and answers it with far more certainty than it has: this changes everything, cloud AI is a scam, local is the future, here is the hardware you need, each title paired with a thumbnail of a man with his mouth open. The most honest answer is that it depends, on your budget, your data, and your tolerance for tinkering. But it depends is the worst video title. Nuance does not get clicks. Certainty gets clicks, outrage definitely gets clicks, and tribalism gets clicks.

For some creators, tribalism is a business model. Convince viewers the other camp is the enemy and you have a loyal audience that returns every week to hear what it already thinks, plus affiliate links, sponsorships, paid courses, and communities you must subscribe to or be left behind. Not everyone with an affiliate link is lying, he says, but when someone asks you to spend thousands of dollars on hardware, a service, or a course, it is fair to ask who benefits. And guides have little incentive to make you competent, because confused people watch the next video and competent people may not.

Maddalone counts himself in. He is a person with a camera making videos about this exact topic, he is not exempt, and he admits his own bias: he spends most of his time on the open source side. So he would rather give viewers a way to check him than ask them to trust him. His filter for any creator, himself included: do they show you where the thing fails, or only a highlight reel? Do they ever tell you not to buy something? Do they disclose sponsorships and commissions? Have they ever changed their mind in public? Are they testing on work that looks like yours, or on a demo designed to look impressive? If the answer to all of those is no, you are not watching an evaluation. You are watching a pitch.

His alternative is to evaluate the thing in front of you rather than the tribe it belongs to. Technology does not care which side you are on; it either solves your problem or it does not. Is it good, is it useful, is it affordable, is it appropriate for the data you are dealing with? Those questions are more productive than asking whether something is local or cloud, American or Chinese, open or proprietary. Yet once people pick a side they are expected to defend everything attached to it: like OpenAI and you must defend Altman, like Anthropic and you must defend Dario, like open weights and you must pretend that running a 30 billion parameter model on your Mac beats the best cloud offering, use the cloud offering and you have sold your soul to a technology monopoly.

He closes where he started. You can appreciate something without endorsing everything associated with it, and criticize something without rejecting everything associated with it. You do not need to like Steve Jobs to appreciate what he built, you do not need to trust Sam Altman to appreciate what OpenAI has produced, and you do not need to pretend local AI is better than it is to appreciate the people behind it. You do not need to pick a team.

Open questions

  1. Maddalone says the honest answer to what should I use is it depends, on budget, data, and tolerance for tinkering. Can advice that conditional compete for attention against the certainty and outrage that get clicks, and if so, in what form?
  2. His checklist for spotting a pitch assumes viewers will apply it even to creators they like, including him. Does handing the audience a way to check the creator change the creator's incentives, or does the audience that enjoys tribal content simply drift to someone who never asks to be checked?
  3. He argues you can use a company's products without endorsing its ambitions, even when the company openly wants to become indispensable in your workflow. At what point does building your profession on a tool stop being neutral evaluation and start being dependence, and who is watching for that line?

MTS · Reed Albergotti on NVIDIA's shrinking chip moat

NVIDIA’s Chip Moat Is Shrinking Fast | Reed Albergotti

On MTS, "NVIDIA’s Chip Moat Is Shrinking Fast | Reed Albergotti" brings back Reed Albergotti, the technology editor at Semafor, in the middle of the biggest week in NVIDIA's history. NVIDIA's board has removed founder Jensen Huang as chief executive, and Albergotti, whose newsroom has been reporting the fall all week, works through how the company got here, who wins and who loses, and why he thinks the company's long run looks weaker than its short run.

He starts with a Semafor story published about an hour before the taping, reported with Garrison Lovely, Ashley Gold, and Rohan Goswami. It describes NVIDIA's general counsel leaning on Huang's influence with President Trump to push Commerce Department officials around, pressing to speed up the licensing regime that lets H200 chips be sold into China. Albergotti's verdict is that NVIDIA, like a lot of the tech industry in this Trump term, went too far. It acted as if the president's favor would last forever and misunderstood Washington, where the political winds always change. The midterms changed the balance of power, and the closeness is now coming back to bite the company.

Some of the wounds were self-inflicted. Nobody forced Huang into the affairs, he notes, including one involving a Chinese national whom the show names as Alice Wong. Asked what evidence exists that she was a Chinese government asset, Albergotti draws a careful line. The public evidence is that she is a Chinese national, and that Beijing holds potential leverage over any Chinese national with family in China, a category that runs from Silicon Valley engineers to Stanford PhD students. Against the stronger claim, that she was actually a spy working for the MSS, he says there is a lot of evidence, and nobody he knows is making that claim at this point. The danger was that she could potentially have been influenced.

On winners and losers, he splits the answer by horizon. In the short term, NVIDIA will be fine, and could even be more profitable, because founder chief executives like Huang routinely sacrifice near-term profit for moves aimed ten years out, and that pressure is now gone. In the long run, he says, he just cannot imagine the company being as innovative, at exactly the moment the chip industry is in flux. Companies like OpenAI now design their own AI chip in a matter of months, so chip design is no longer the moat, and navigating that shift will be tricky. His analogy is Apple after Steve Jobs: a company that has not really innovated in a long time, yet has been hugely profitable on the strength of the iPhone.

Why did the board move so fast, before its own investigation had concluded? Because the bleeding would not stop. Stories about other affairs kept surfacing, and classified information from inside the government was leaking out in a strategic drip. At some point the board had to act; removing Huang put a tourniquet on it, even if it has not totally stopped the bleeding. Some people speculate he could return one day. Albergotti does not know how realistic that is, but the removal seemed the right move in the short term.

Pressed for parallels with Sam Altman's firing in 2023, he says he first saw them and now mostly does not. The allegations against Altman were always vaguer, with no smoking gun to point to, more about how he interacted with people at the company and how he represented facts. The Huang case, by contrast, is pretty cut and dried, and much more difficult to get out of.

On export controls, his answer is yes: expect very strict bipartisan controls from Congress. That, in his telling, was the goal behind the whole fight. NVIDIA and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick were the two biggest blockers of export controls inside the administration, and both are now out of the way. He expects the controls to reach beyond exports themselves to the loophole that has let Chinese citizens and companies legally access data centers in places like Malaysia remotely. Closing that will be technically challenging, he concedes, but they are going to try.

Huang's other allies are exposed too. Lutnick, he says, is facing a huge crisis, with more still to come out about how he handled export controls at Commerce. David Sacks is in the crosshairs as well, and anyone who was too close to Trump is; Albergotti is interviewing Sacks at Semafor's Silicon Valley and the World conference the following week. Sacks, though, is not involved in the day-to-day of export controls, so his case may be a separate matter.

Succession looks brutal. The names floated are Rene Haas of Arm, and keeping Colette Kress of NVIDIA in the job permanently, and even those underline how thin the field is. Albergotti has spoken with someone involved in the succession effort: they believe there are six to eight people in the world who can do this job, and NVIDIA does not want just one of them. The company has no chief operating officer and wants to hire one alongside a chief executive. It had tried to recruit a number two before, and candidates of that caliber did not want to work under Huang, whose personality was too big for them. Some have already declined the operating chief role. It is going to be hugely challenging.

Open questions

  1. If customers like OpenAI can now design their own AI chips in a matter of months, what replaces chip design as NVIDIA's durable advantage, and can a post-Huang leadership team build it?
  2. Will the coming strict export controls actually close the loophole that lets Chinese companies remotely use data centers in third countries, or will enforcement founder on the technical difficulty Albergotti flags?
  3. With perhaps six to eight plausible candidates in the world, and some already saying no, can NVIDIA land both a chief executive and the chief operating officer it has never had?

Behind the Balance Sheet · Benedict Evans on whether Anthropic can pay off

Will Anthropic Flop? Benedict Evans analyses its prospects

On Behind the Balance Sheet, “Will Anthropic Flop? Benedict Evans analyses its prospects” is host Steve Clapham's conversation with tech analyst Benedict Evans about Anthropic's IPO filing and the question hanging over the whole AI industry: can the model companies ever earn back the money being poured into them?

Evans starts with the filing itself, which he calls a Rorschach test. An accelerationist can read it and see revenue roughly ten times what it was a year earlier, with a growing share of the research now done by Claude itself. A skeptic can point to the headline loss figure of about 80 billion dollars, which Evans says is basically accounting fiction once you work out what it actually was. The problem, he says, is what the filing does not tell us. It gives revenue and customer counts, but it says little about depth and spread of usage, how well the tools are working outside a narrow band of people, and Anthropic itself cannot see much of that, because most of its business is API usage by others.

The profit and loss statement has a strange shape. The cost of inference, the cost of revenue, has stayed roughly flat while revenue rose roughly tenfold, because efficiency gains in running the models have been enormous. Meanwhile the training cost, booked as research and development, which ought to be a fixed cost, has risen in line with revenue. If you did not label the chart, Evans says, you would think the two lines were the other way round. Neither line is predictable. The price of inference is unknowable because tokens are being sold into a once in a generation shortage, with the software industry wanting to buy far more than can be supplied, so sellers can price at the customer's return on investment. But more capital spending, better models, better chips, and better inference all push costs down, roughly tenfold a year, while agents using the models could multiply token demand by one or two orders of magnitude. Usage is still broad but shallow. Spend data from Ramp, which skews toward startups and early adopters, showed the top one percent of its customer base spending ten to fifteen thousand dollars a month per employee, while the average was fifteen dollars. A small number of people use these tools intensely. There is no billion people using them intensely the way they use Instagram.

Clapham then turns to the hyperscalers, the companies spending the money. Published estimates run to two or three trillion dollars of spending by 2030, but Evans notes the calculation is circular, since nobody knows the run rate of capital spending, whether it is half a trillion or a trillion a year, and that depends on demand. He reaches for an analogy from his own career. He became a telecoms analyst in 2000, when European mobile operators spent about 110 to 120 billion euros on 3G licenses. Everything they predicted people would do with mobile internet did happen, yet most of those operators never earned a return on the investment. The value went to Uber, Amazon, Instagram, and everyone else building on top. The same could happen here: enormous adoption at low prices, a pricing collapse, and infrastructure that turns out to be a low margin commodity with no pricing power, so the value accrues to other people.

The servers themselves are part of the puzzle. Meta, Google, Amazon, and Microsoft together plan to spend about 750 billion dollars on capital expenditure this year, against about 100 billion five years ago, and three of the four are on course to spend more than half of revenue on it. Capital spending has outgrown their free cash flow, so they have had to raise money in the capital markets, which is why credit analysts suddenly matter for technology companies. When to replace a chip is a spreadsheet calculation: how many tokens you get per gigawatt, how much better the new chips and models are, and whether you are constrained by power. If power is the constraint, it makes sense to swap in chips with a higher return per watt. How long a chip actually lasts, its mechanical life versus its useful economic life, is something nobody really knows until the hardware has been run at full tilt for years. Evans frames the investor question bluntly. As a creditor you do not much care if the data centers end up as space heaters, so long as the advertising revenue is still there. As an equity investor, that was your money going into a hole in the ground.

A deeper question is what the AI product actually is. Evans compares giving everyone a model to giving everyone a spreadsheet in the mid 1980s or a web browser in the mid 1990s. That was life changing for five or ten percent of people, but it was not how companies rebuilt themselves around computers. That was SAP and Oracle. The same applies now. Handing everyone Copilot or Claude or ChatGPT is not how a business works out how AI transforms it. One outcome is that the model companies capture most of the value of that transformation. The other is that the value goes further up the stack, to a thousand new software companies, while the typical big American company goes from four or five hundred software applications today to a thousand in ten years, half of them running on AI models, without paying Anthropic or OpenAI. Part of the difficulty, Evans says, is that unlike every previous platform shift, we do not have a good first principles understanding of why this technology works as well as it does, so we do not know how good the models will get, on what dimension, and at what cost.

Are the models themselves commodities? At the level of headline benchmarks, Evans says, not really differentiated: Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Meta are all close, and the Chinese models are not far behind. Differences appear in specific use cases, and at the frontier, where a task is only just possible. The price against performance curve is very sharp. The latest full model is expensive, while a model from six months ago gives most of the benchmark performance at a small fraction of the price, by Evans's rough arithmetic. Voice recognition is finished and runs on a phone. The telling figure from Anthropic's filing is that 80 percent of revenue comes from models built in the last six months, partly because people only want the newest model and partly because a year old model is a commodity, super cheap, maybe running on premises, so nobody can make money from it.

Evans frames what AI unlocks as two things: automation and abstraction. Automation is doing the old thing faster and cheaper, and also doing things people could not do at all, like listening to every call into a call center and asking what customers are really saying, or throwing ten thousand agents at a codebase to find every possible vulnerability. Abstraction is more interesting. Instead of constructing a careful 35 step database query in Salesforce to work out who to call this week, you should be able to ask the computer who you should call, or which customers are going to churn, or which invoices are overdue and why. That is new software, and it needs to be built carefully by people whose job is to think about how it works, whether that is Accenture, Bain, a startup, or SAP. It is not Anthropic, and it is not Claude running on the chief financial officer's laptop, any more than a ten thousand person company ran its inventory from a copy of Excel.

Clapham raises the domain expertise argument, citing Harvey, the legal AI company, whose chief executive Winston Weinberg says young lawyers have a great future because they will do more interesting work. Evans answers with the Mandy Rice-Davies line: he would say that, would he not. Then he turns to the accelerationist argument itself, which he compares to Anselm's ontological argument for the existence of God. If you define the future of AI as a system that can do anything, then of course it can do everything, but there are two giant ifs in that statement, and you have defined the problem away. In practice, these systems give you the answer most people would probably give, done the way most people would do it. Sometimes that is what you want. Sometimes it is not at all what you want. And asking whether a task can be automated is really two questions: is it physically possible for a machine to do it, and do you want it done the same way every time.

On valuation, Clapham cites Aswath Damodaran's estimate of one and a half trillion dollars for Anthropic, built on the assumption that it becomes a big drugs company. Evans points out that Damodaran made a famous error in 2014, valuing Uber on the size of the existing taxi market and predicting 18 billion dollars of gross bookings, when the actual figure for mobility in 2024 was 83 billion, plus delivery and freight on top. He stresses this is not a gotcha, since predictions are hard, especially about the future. The point is that you cannot value things that way, because you do not know what the new things will be. He notes that the number of accountants rose every decade of the twentieth century even as accounting work was automated, and that the value PwC got out of spreadsheets appears in neither PwC's accounts nor Microsoft's, nor in the productivity statistics.

Asked where Anthropic and OpenAI will be in five years, Evans says he hates forecasts, and reaches for 1997. You knew the internet would be huge, but the biggest European internet stocks of the dot com bubble were internet service providers, and everything that ended up on the Yahoo and AOL homepages did happen, but not for Yahoo or AOL. The models could end up like mobile operators, commodity infrastructure whose value is captured elsewhere, or like cloud, where Amazon makes good money without controlling the whole software industry, or like chips, where each generation gets harder and more expensive until only one company, TSMC, is left at the cutting edge and can charge whatever it likes. None of those questions can be answered by reading the filing.

The signals to watch, he says, are volatility and stories being priced in twice, and the gap between product market fit in software development, which is explosive right now, and consumer product market fit at scale, which does not exist yet. A billion and a half people use these tools a little. Mark Zuckerberg said on Meta's quarterly call that Meta's Muse has product market fit, with ten million users and a quarter of them using it on any given day, which Evans notes is not the same as daily active users, and that he cannot scale it until costs fall tenfold, which is not quite product market fit if you cannot scale it. Part of the consumer problem is a blank screen: people do not know what to ask. The fix may be agents, or systems that watch everything you do and surface suggestions, but that is a thesis for Google, Meta, Amazon, and Apple, who can see your email, your messages, your purchases, and your social life. OpenAI and Anthropic see only what you do inside their apps, which is one reason OpenAI may want a device.

On the big technology companies, Evans is specific. Meta is protean: it watches user behavior, follows it, and pivots, and the bet on Meta is a bet on a company that always works it out. Google does search, and if this is search, Google does search. Apple is well positioned by not playing, since whatever happens, people will want a device with strong privacy, battery life, a camera, and custom silicon. Amazon's core is a network effect around logistics and delivery, and its decision to block Meta's Muse has two parts. One is tactical: liability and security, the same reason banks block it, since twenty years of blocking bots means nobody knows whose connection it is or who bears the loss. The other is strategic: nobody wants to be someone else's dumb API call. Amazon did 75 billion dollars of advertising revenue in the last twelve months and wants the customer relationship, the upsell, and the ownership. Evans has a slide that says every AI question has one of two answers: no one knows, or how did it work last time.

Clapham closes with the bull and bear cases. The bull case is a magical product with almost unlimited demand, efficiency improving fast, and a huge profit pool, with the only question being how it gets divided up. The bear case is that the sheer scale of investment requires an impossible level of revenue, and because the whole edifice is interconnected, the cost of money is critical. Evans adds the question of who can get capital at all: Google has the cash flow, the newer cloud companies do not. He also raises the money left on the table by behavioral economics, the friction that stops people moving savings, switching credit cards, or claiming small discounts, which might add up to a thousand pounds a year per person, and asks where that money goes once agents optimise everything. His final point is about total addressable market. If you had tried to size the market for spreadsheets in 1980 by counting accountants, you would never have predicted a billion and a half Excel users. He recalls asking Lloyd Blankfein how Goldman Sachs deployed PCs and spreadsheets in the 1980s without cutting its headcount, and Blankfein replying that a third of Goldman's employees are now software engineers. Some of the value AI creates will accrue to nobody, as consumer surplus or lower prices. Some will be new spending, captured by parties nobody can name yet. Evans argues it will probably not be the model companies, but he admits he could be completely wrong.

Open questions

  1. The filing shows explosive revenue growth, but Evans says usage is broad and shallow, with only a small number of people using the tools intensely. What signal would show the growth curve bending before the revenue line does, and who would see it first?
  2. Evans argues the value of AI will probably land with the companies that build the vertical software on top of the models, not with the model makers themselves. What would a model company have to build to keep that value, and what evidence would prove him wrong?
  3. Hyperscalers are spending at a pace that outruns their free cash flow and leaning on debt markets to fund it. If token demand softens or pricing collapses, who absorbs the loss first, and what breaks in the chain?

The Pragmatic Engineer · Satya Nadella on software engineering after cheap code

Software engineering after code becomes cheap with Satya Nadella

On The Pragmatic Engineer, “Software engineering after code becomes cheap with Satya Nadella” is host Gergely Orosz's conversation with Satya Nadella, the chief executive of Microsoft and one of the few big technology CEOs who started his career as a software engineer. The conversation ranges across Microsoft's open source turn, the rise and sudden displacement of the AI coding tools Microsoft pioneered, and the question in the title: what the work of software engineering becomes once generating code is cheap. Nadella's short answer arrives early. Generation has become cheap. Verification has become the hard part.

The conversation opens with open source. Orosz, who worked at Microsoft when Nadella became CEO in 2014, recalls a company still committed to closed source, and the surprise when it embraced open source instead: Visual Studio Code released free and open, TypeScript open sourced, GitHub acquired, SQL Server brought to Linux. Nadella says the contributions predated his tenure, but the company made its commitment unmistakable, and he argues that a true accounting would put Microsoft at or near the top of open source contributors, while some companies that benefited most from open source have contributed the least. It is, he says, narratives versus facts.

Visual Studio Code mattered for a second reason. It taught Microsoft to build in the open: a product team that treated community feedback, rather than the vice president's opinion, as the signal to follow. Nadella calls that feedback cycle fundamentally transformative, and wishes the lesson had been learned more deeply and more broadly across the company.

That history sets up the Copilot story. Microsoft was early: code completions that felt magical before GPT-4 existed, built on years of IntelliSense work, and a year or more without real competition. Cursor then took the lead on agent mode inside a forked VS Code, and Microsoft caught up. What it did not anticipate was 2025, when Anthropic's models made the command line the form factor and, in about six months, the IDE faded as the center of coding. Nadella's diagnosis is that AI first product building is unforgiving: when model capability jumps in a nonlinear way, the form factor has to change with it, and you cannot be in love with what you built. He does not treat the shift as permanent, either. Intelligence embedded inside software, in a token efficient way, could bring about a rebirth of the IDE.

One candidate for that embedded intelligence is the decision engine, a new primitive Nadella returns to several times. He compares it to what macros did for spreadsheets, and to the stored procedure of the intelligence era: a reliable, callable decision that you embed in a system once, instead of generating fresh model code every time a decision is needed, and far cheaper in tokens as a result. He reaches for the relational database as the model to copy, the developer tool he loved most after compilers, because a dependable way to store and query information made whole categories of applications possible. The aim for decision engines, which he endorses, is to become as boring as databases.

The software factory is where these threads meet. Nadella admits he is still forming his own definition, and points to two Microsoft team blogs, from the cowork team and the GitHub Copilot team, as the case studies. The core shift is that code has gone from input to output. What humans supply is specification and intent, and alignment on both. One developer now maintains an entire runtime, including a migration to Rust. The cowork team numbers roughly a dozen engineers. And because a factory has to document everything, an early career engineer told him that joining a codebase is now much easier: the system itself can act as a patient onboarding mentor, replacing tribal knowledge that used to live only in people's heads. His other example is operations rather than code: the Azure networking team, which looks after some 500 fiber operators around the world and used to run on email and improvised process, has built an agentic system that does the operations work itself, wired into the team's own repositories. Teams, he says, increasingly build factories that do the job rather than doing the job directly.

Verification is the factory's hard core. Agentic coding, as these teams practice it, means small tasks whose completion has to be genuinely proven: every failure is turned back into a test case, and the test itself has to be verified. Nadella worries about building on a single model family, whose blind spots and alignment problems become your own, and describes using a separate model family as the critic. The loop he loves most runs from production: take the traces and logs, produce a fix, verify it against the tests, and deploy it all the way through. He jokes that this verifiability loop is his version of RSI.

He would like to learn this way of working from inside. Nadella says his dream next job would be a seat on one of these teams, and names the perceptorship program run inside Microsoft by Scott Hanselman and Mark Russinovich as the place he would start. Orosz notes how many CTOs and vice presidents have stepped down into small teams to learn the new tools firsthand, and Nadella agrees: at a time of great change on many fronts, being hands on, close to customers, and building in the open matters more than overseeing from above.

Orosz pushes on the gap between lab demonstrations and product teams with real customers, who cannot drop everything to build a factory. Nadella's answer starts with permission: nobody gets permission to do work that does not add value for customers, and the real world is brownfield. AI models are now finding Windows vulnerabilities that all have to be fixed, so the Windows team is building a factory of its own, running from crash traces to verified fixes on top of the existing Windows build system, where builds and continuous integration are the hardest part. GitHub faces the same pressure from the other direction: coding agents have pushed its scale so high that the git protocol and the storage system underneath are being reworked. In brownfield work, he says, the order of operations is critical, and the new toolchain is for every team, not a showcase team.

He also thinks this change is different in kind, not just degree. New frameworks used to arrive without changing the number of concepts an engineer had to hold. This time the runtime, the framework, the IDE, and the stack are all in question at once, and a new model release can transform the regime again at any moment. On the human side, he rejects the idea that expertise has been devalued. The same skills are still required; the order of operations and the toolchain have changed, so expertise has to be exercised with new leverage. Engineers with real depth who stay curious, he says, are flourishing, and part of his job is making sure everyone at Microsoft gets the chance to build the new skill set the market will value.

That leads to the voluntary exit offers, which Orosz asks about directly: are they a prelude to layoffs as AI makes teams more efficient? Nadella frames them as agency. The opportunity ahead is real but differently shaped, with different team sizes and workflows, and Microsoft's roughly 200,000 people will be configured differently; some expertise, systems engineers and training researchers among them, already commands record wages. The point of a voluntary program, he says, is to let people slot themselves where their long term economic opportunity is best, inside Microsoft or beyond it, instead of sitting stuck on a team that is not transforming. He is candid that he cannot promise it will all be the same. One of the fastest growing jobs at the company, he notes, is the forward deployed engineer, a role that barely existed a few years ago, and roles are merging elsewhere too, as at LinkedIn, where combined roles are called full stack builders.

On junior hiring, he confirms Microsoft is still hiring new graduates and investing in them, and argues the wider slowdown may be temporary. Companies first have to reconfigure their existing teams into the new shapes; then it makes sense to hire into factories where a newcomer can be productive. AI native companies show the end state: start with one person and a token budget, let that person max out the budget, then add the next person with a budget of their own. Drawing long term trend lines from the current pause, he says, is too early.

The last third turns to models themselves. Asked whether open models parallel the open source movement, Nadella goes further, by way of Hayek: knowledge in an economy is tacit and distributed, so if you reject the idea of one frontier model holding all the world's knowledge, you should expect, in some sense, as many frontier models as there are firms. Every firm will hold both human capital and token capital. The architecture follows from that. Keep your harness, your context and memory, and your action space separate from any single model, because a model absorbs everything it sees, and if your memory lives inside one vendor's model you can never leave. His test for a sovereign firm is simple: you should be able to replace any model and still improve against your own private evaluation. Handing your learning loop to a vendor is the reverse information paradox, leaking your value away. Microsoft's own commercial answer is to offer the harness underneath Copilot as a managed service in Foundry, with many models as interchangeable endpoints and the customer's context kept separate.

The second enterprise decision he names is trust. Scaling laws deliver capability without alignment, so systems are getting more capable and less reliable at the same time, which he calls the most dangerous place to be. Authority therefore has to be separated from intelligence architecturally. Agents should be treated as insider risk: their chains of thought kept in human language, auditable, and logged in a way that cannot be tampered with, with safeguards running at runtime and not only in training.

Local models close the loop. Last year's frontier model can run on a laptop, and last quarter's on a desktop. Routers such as Copilot Fusion pick the model for each task, so in a swarm of subagents some can run on the machine in front of you, its fan spinning up, while others run in the cloud. He calls the result unmetered intelligence: intelligence you no longer have to count. Microsoft is also building its own MAI model family from the ground up, with an unusually detailed disclosure of its pre-training recipe, while continuing to build on OpenAI's models; the goal, he says, is choice, not dependence on one model family.

Asked, finally, how a software professional should approach all this, Nadella says to change the ratio of talk to doing. Get grounded in what the change actually is, stay out of the hype cycle, and learn by being on teams that are doing the work, whether you are a new graduate, mid-career, or a CEO. When is he at his best? When he is in school mode. So, he says, let's get back to school.

  2. If verification is the scarce skill inside the software factory, how should it be taught to engineers who no longer spend their early years writing and debugging code by hand?
  3. Nadella expects the junior hiring slowdown to reverse once companies finish reconfiguring their teams. What evidence would show the reversal has begun, and how long can the pause last before the pipeline of experienced engineers runs dry?

How I AI · Claire Vo on fighting token fraud at ChatPRD

How I fight token fraud as an AI founder

On How I AI, “How I fight token fraud as an AI founder” is a solo episode by Claire Vo, the show's host and the solo founder of ChatPRD, an AI platform for product managers that she has run as a bootstrapped business since the early GPT-3.5 days. Her subject is token fraud: people who are not her customers finding ways to run their work through her product's calls to models from Anthropic and OpenAI, and leaving her holding the token bill. It has cost her tens of thousands of dollars over the past year, and she says almost all of her engineering energy now goes into blocking it.

The economics explain why the fraud lands so hard on her. ChatPRD does not charge by usage. It charges per seat, with pricing built on average use across customers, so the business works only as long as real customers behave roughly as expected. She is not venture funded, so every fraudulent dollar comes directly out of her own pocket. When she posted about the problem on X, founders and engineers from some of the largest AI companies replied that they fight the same thing daily, and one friend told her he was about to turn off signups entirely.

She walks through six patterns of abuse she has seen. The first is free trial farming, where fraudsters sign up for generous free tiers and run the most expensive models on work that has nothing to do with the product. The single biggest lever she pulled was turning free trials off altogether, even though trials had been a strong source of product-led growth and enterprise leads. The second is promo code abuse: codes given out through partners were resold, stolen, and mass-used to spin up other AI businesses, with many ending up sold to resellers on Reddit. The third is bad payment cards, prepaid cards, chargebacks, and card testing. The fourth is resale and relays, including people who authenticated inside ChatPRD in a browser and relayed requests from their own AI products through it, and one case where someone installed a custom MCP in ChatPRD that turned into a worker prompting around the clock. The fifth is model probing from international accounts, a stream of questions about what model she runs and what its training cutoff is, which she suspects may be farming or distillation. The sixth is signup loops and farms, plus ordinary trial abusers using the product as a free substitute for ChatGPT to write essays and work reports.

Two stories stand out. She discovered she had been funding an international Roblox and Fortnite map studio, which looped free trials and promo codes and tunneled into her agent to build game maps. A reseller wrapped ChatPRD in a proxy and resold access to its own customers by the hour. And in the detail she clearly finds hardest to believe, fraudsters used ChatPRD itself, a tool for writing product documents, to draft product documents describing how to scam ChatPRD. Investigating abusive accounts, she also found the same operators running reverse proxies against several of the best known AI products, rotating their calls across tools.

Her business defenses, several of them drawn from a list shared by Gareth at Atlassian, are blunt. Free trials are over, she says: even trials requiring a credit card failed, because fraudsters cycled cards and filed chargebacks. Promo codes are gone. Billing has to be sooner and completely inflexible, charged up front with no grace period on failed payments. Accounts and organizations need hard usage limits, trials should be restricted to business email domains, feature flags should let you shut things off the moment something looks strange, and the terms of service need to allow bans for suspected abuse. She is rethinking international business entirely, having watched fraud drop sharply when she firewalled off whole countries, and she has reported what looked like coordinated international enterprises to the FBI as cybercrime, with Codex helping her draft the reports. Her broader plea is that founders start talking to each other about fraud patterns, something larger companies avoid because admitting fraud publicly can attract more of it.

The technical layer starts with an AI gateway. She routes calls through the Vercel AI Gateway to control spend and to enforce a strict allowlist of models, so a caller cannot simply ask for a more expensive model than the product intends to give them. Suspected fraudulent calls can be silently downgraded to a cheaper penalty model. Spend is monitored hourly at least, with caps and alerts, because her own big mistake was not realizing how fast an exploit can pile up cost. Stripe Radar does heavy work on payments: she first adopted it after waking up to roughly 200,000 dollars in attempted card-testing purchases in a day, all of them failed requests on stolen cards, and she now uses it for country and address checks and for fingerprinting cards that show up across multiple trials, writing the complex rules with Codex and reviewing them in the Stripe dashboard. Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander · Dartmouth pauses math PhD admissions

Dartmouth suspends mathematics PhD student enrollments...

On Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander, “Dartmouth suspends mathematics PhD student enrollments...” is a read-through of a news article in The Dartmouth, the college's student newspaper, headlined “Guarini will not admit math Ph.D. cohort this year.” Alexander, a mathematician and philosopher, reads the article aloud and comments as he goes. The news is that Dartmouth's Guarini School of Graduate and Advanced Studies has suspended admissions to its mathematics PhD program for the cohort that would have matriculated in the 2027 to 2028 academic year, while the School of Arts and Sciences conducts a review of the department. Neither the dean nor a college spokesperson would say when applications will resume, or whether they will resume at all.

The official framing is investment and review. In a November 10 email statement, the Guarini dean wrote that the school regularly considers how to best invest in graduate education, and that admissions are paused for the 2027 to 2028 cycle while the department review runs, a decision made in close partnership with leaders in the School of Arts and Sciences. She promised current graduate students a chance to take part in the review and a meeting with students and faculty on November 17. The math faculty themselves learned of the suspension only on November 6, and at the November 9 general faculty meeting several raised concerns. Math professor Peter Winkler spoke against the suspension and declined to comment further afterward.

Applicants were told on November 10 that the materials and recommendation letters they had already submitted will not be reviewed. Applications would have been due on December 15, and the program is small: it typically accepts seven to nine students a year. The article also places the pause inside a national trend, citing data from 55 undisclosed research institutions showing that PhD acceptances dropped 15 percent in 2026 even though domestic applications increased. Alexander stops on that paragraph and complains, at some length, that it never says whether the figures cover all PhDs or only math PhDs. From the context he concludes that math is meant, and he admits the rise in applications surprises him. His guess is that a grim job market pushes people to delay entering it, and graduate school is the classic way to wait.

For students already in the program, the college promises that status and support are not changing and that every currently enrolled student will be seen through to graduation. Dartmouth math PhD students received a stipend of $49,862.40 for the 2026 to 2027 academic year, along with tuition remission, health insurance, and a guarantee of five years of support for students making satisfactory progress. Alexander notes that the stipend is roughly twice what he earned during his own PhD at Ohio State, and speculates that the remaining students will be in higher demand as teaching assistants and tutors once no new cohort arrives. The workload, though, is already going up: starting this term, first and second year candidates must serve as teaching assistants for three terms a year instead of the previously required two, and candidates past the third year must teach or assist every year as well. He wonders whether pay will rise to match.

The students quoted in the article are not reassured. A third year candidate said he and his fellow students were shocked, that there had been no indication this was coming, and that he was frustrated by the lack of transparency. Second year candidate Alex Moon said that suspending applications sends a message of instability to future applicants: if this is something the college does once, it is something it can do later. Another second year candidate worried that losing a whole cohort would thin out the discussions in an already quite small program, where having people in adjacent areas of mathematics to talk with is a large part of the education. A third year candidate raised research culture itself, saying that the idea of being both a research university and a liberal arts college does not work without graduate students.

The effects also reach beyond the math department. Undergraduates who take graduate level courses, as some Dartmouth math majors do from their first year, may find fewer of them offered, since the college can cancel a course with fewer than five students enrolled. Ethan Baird, a first year PhD candidate in computational science and modeling, said that almost half of his program's coursework is normally fulfilled through math department courses, and worried about trickle down effects on his own program. The same faculty meeting also dealt with the college's decision to discontinue the Society of Fellows, a postdoctoral program founded in 2014 that has stopped recruiting and will conclude in the summer of 2028, and with the president's announcement that Dartmouth is reducing its spending by 50 million dollars in order to raise faculty and staff salaries, with roughly half of that already in place for fiscal 2027.

Alexander's own reading of the suspension is blunt, and he is clear that it is his reading: nowhere in the article is AI mentioned, or Lean, but he believes AI is what is on everybody's mind. He calls it the mathocalypse. AI systems are now able to prove theorems, people are unsure what pure mathematicians will even be doing in the future, and he expects a huge drop in new math PhD students starting this year. On that view, Dartmouth's decision is simply rational, and he is surprised more schools have not done the same.

His longer running argument is that the pipeline was oversupplied long before AI arrived. To land a new tenure position in mathematics, he says, you basically need a corresponding tenured professor to retire or die, and that is not happening at anything like the rate at which programs admit students, so even seven to nine a year is irresponsibly many. He argues the degree itself has been inflated and watered down: too many candidates, once past their qualifying exams, simply warm a seat for some years, and a credential that is handed out so freely stops distinguishing the people who are in it for love of the subject, a group he counts himself in, from everyone else. The signal value of a PhD, in his telling, depends on its rarity.

He then reads through the Hacker News discussion of the article. Some commenters defend the pause on exactly his grounds: graduate programs have never been responsive to the job market and mint more PhDs than the professions can absorb, so stepping back may be the responsible thing. Others answer that planning a life is the student's own responsibility, not the institution's. Alexander splits the difference: nobody can ask a university to predict demand five years out perfectly, but rough arithmetic is possible, and if the number of new tenure positions in mathematics will be well under 500 while the field goes on minting something like 10,000 new PhDs, that is not a close call. The statistician Gwern asks how large the Dartmouth program even is and whether it ranks in the top ten, suggesting the decision may be rational after the mathocalypse; Alexander agrees. Another commenter goes further and suggests most universities should not have math departments at all if AI makes lower level academics obsolete. Against the reply that we still need humans who understand mathematics at PhD level, Alexander answers that hiring, tenure, and journal decisions were never actually based on understanding: they were based on proving hard theorems, which is exactly what AI has now obsoleted at the top level.

The thread ends on a wider demographic note: an insider at a top law school expects law schools to start downsizing too, and another commenter points to falling birth rates shrinking every applicant pool downstream. Alexander's closing advice is his own. A mathematics PhD, he says, is now a terrible idea unless you are independently wealthy, or you love mathematics so much that you are willing to live a life of poverty for its sake. He placed himself in the second camp, and in his case it worked out: he got a good job and made good money. But he is quick to add that this is no guarantee, that many of the people he went through graduate school with are stuck in the adjunct grind, and that he does not recommend the path.

Open questions

  1. The college will not say when, or whether, math PhD admissions will resume. Will the department review restore the program, reshape it into something smaller or different, or quietly end it, and what will the answer look like from outside?
  2. Alexander reads the pause as a response to AI theorem proving, but the article itself cites budgets, investment choices, and a department review, and never mentions AI. Which force is really doing the work, and does the answer change what other math departments should do?
  3. As missing cohorts work through the system, who teaches the undergraduates and staffs the graduate courses that neighboring programs depend on, and would a smaller and rarer math PhD recover the value Alexander says the degree has lost?

a16z · The economics of the AI revolution

What History Tells Us About the AI Revolution | Tyler Cowen & Alex Imas

On a16z, “What History Tells Us About the AI Revolution” is a conversation with two economists, Tyler Cowen of George Mason University and Alex Imas of the University of Chicago, about what artificial intelligence will actually do to the economy. The episode was recorded on November 11, 2026, and the two men agree on more than they dispute. The capabilities are real and accelerating, they say, but economic transformation has to push through bottlenecks, institutions, and plain inertia, so the path will look far more normal than the loudest forecasts suggest.

The conversation opens with the recent METR report and the anxiety around recursive self-improvement. Imas says the progress is undeniable and accelerating: model releases are more frequent and more powerful, and the shift from the chatbot era to the agentic era means systems now do actual work rather than answer questions. He did not update much on the report itself, because the data was already circulating elsewhere, but he frames the question he keeps asking of every model iteration. Which economically valuable tasks does this model unlock that the previous one could not do? A benchmark can hockey stick, he warns, while the real world barely moves, and the hardest open territory is work that is not well specified, where tacit knowledge matters.

Cowen is the more sober voice. The faster some things move, he says, the more quickly you hit the real bottlenecks. He has updated since August, mostly because of progress in mathematics, an area with few human bottlenecks, where a clearly better model can now arrive within weeks. Over the past week, though, he has grown about 1 percent more bearish, in his words. He points to the turmoil at Nvidia, to prediction markets that are not pricing the Anthropic IPO above 2 trillion dollars, and to Anthropic's own revenue projections, which are not the numbers a super fast takeoff would produce. The institutions, he says, are not running as smoothly as either the optimists or the fearful might assume.

That leads to the arithmetic of lab revenues. Cowen recounts asking people in the Bay Area who were pushing extreme revenue numbers for Anthropic and OpenAI a simple question: what other sectors will grow fast enough to justify those numbers? Nobody even tried to answer. His conclusion is that the extreme numbers are false, though he remains bullish on the sector. Market data, from real interest rates to stock prices, still look pretty normal, and he trusts the market's judgment over the judgment of people who do not think carefully about social interactions. Imas adds the mechanism: at some point the rest of the economy has to become more productive and therefore willing to pay, and that evidence is not in yet. Meanwhile the frontier labs have shifted from competing on model quality to competing on price, so token usage rises exponentially while revenue stays roughly flat.

The heart of the episode is the contrast between two growth worlds. Imas describes a paper by Ben Jones and Chad Jones that spells out what double digit, singularity style growth would require: a set of knife edge conditions, above all no decreasing returns in the production of ideas themselves. In the old world, each breakthrough gets harder to extend; in the explosive world, breakthroughs would feed on themselves with increasing returns. The key parameter can be estimated, and when Imas estimates it from the data available so far, including the METR material, it comes out decidedly less than one, which is the ordinary world of diminishing returns. A completed recursive self-improvement loop is exactly the thing that could change that parameter, he says, but it has not yet. Cowen adds the historical check. Very high growth rates do happen, in tiny economies selling one commodity, or as rebounds from deep recessions, wars, and pandemics. A single year of 10 percent growth is conceivable if an AI burst rode on top of a recovery, but he would bet heavily against it, and the effect would be temporary.

Underneath the growth debate sits a quieter piece of economics. When something gets automated, its price falls, and it tends to shrink as a share of what the economy spends. Value migrates to whatever remains scarce and slow moving, so the sectors growing least can end up contributing most to measured growth. That is one reason both men expect robotics to arrive slowly: Waymo still does not operate in Washington, DC, robotics valuations have risen without producing trillion dollar companies, and no plausible robotics path delivers 20 percent growth three or four years from now. Cowen adds a sociological note about why he and Imas see it differently from the runaway growth believers associated with writers like Dwarkesh Patel and Leopold Aschenbrenner. What is emotionally vivid to the economists is centuries of growth and productivity data; what is vivid in the Bay Area is a stream of local conversations. Both bodies of evidence contain information, but for the big question, he would rather trust the long record.

Inertia gets its own share of the blame. The IRS still uses fax machines. Educational software is a nightmare. Cowen guesses that something like 40 percent of the American economy, government, nonprofits, much of health care, and schooling, is structurally slow, and higher in parts of Europe. Regulation is a problem in those sectors, he says, but it is not the whole problem, and anyone who has dealt with those institutions up close carries a dose of realism that startup life does not provide.

If AI's benefits increasingly arrive as free or cheap goods, GDP will understate them, so the two discuss how flourishing should be measured. Imas points to work on survey based measures that ask what people would pay for digital goods they now get free, and argues that a world of 3 to 5 percent growth could still be a genuinely flourishing one if those unpriced gains are counted. Cowen raises longevity: living to 120 would be an enormous gain that GDP captures only in complicated, partial ways. Even so, he defends the old statistic. GDP tracks human welfare historically well, he says, and calls it the most underrated statistic there is.

The alignment discussion runs through their discipline's toolkit. Asked about the Hugging Face incident and agent swarms, Cowen reaches for principal agent theory: a principal simply cannot control an agent perfectly, no matter the contract, and the historical answer is checks and balances, a separation of powers, rather than perfect alignment. Imas agrees and names the framework, mechanism design. You do not need the agent to share your goals; you build the system of incentives so that even a self-interested agent behaves in the principal's interest. Both men note that the real harms so far have come from people directing AIs to do bad things, that the Hugging Face incident was layers of human error exposing broken incentives, and that the labs are more practical than their public talk suggests. Both also flatly reject the claim, associated with Paul Christiano, that the most likely cause of death a year or two from now will be AI related.

Imas then lays out his paper Organizing Intelligence, written with co-authors, which starts from a puzzle: capability keeps rising, yet no visible economic transformation has followed. Their answer is that the relevant competitor for an AI agent is not an individual human but an organization. Organizations embody comparative advantage, since the person doing each job is the one best suited to it, so a team's combined, mosaic intelligence is far stronger than an average person's. Even a minimal AGI, as capable as the average human on every dimension, would mostly shift wages rather than transform the economy, because it would be competing against that higher bar. What would transform things is coordination. Today's agent swarms mostly do parallel processing and coordinate badly; on coordination tasks, one agent often outperforms two agents splitting the work, while two people outperform one person given double the resources. If agents learned to coordinate, firms could break through the Coasean ceiling on firm size, and the shape of companies, wages, and employment would genuinely change.

Employment is where their worries differ in emphasis. Imas fears the transition speed: if coordination improves in a breakthrough rather than gradually, the economy could hit a phase transition in which displaced workers cannot rematch into new roles fast enough, and long spells of unemployment scar people out of the labor market. Cowen's worry is about hiring signals. Employers used to rely on imperfect indicators like degrees and interviews; now the key question is whether a person will be mentally flexible enough to work well with AI, and nobody can tell yet. That could raise the natural rate of unemployment for a while, until certifications and experience emerge. On balance he is bullish: there will be an incredible number of new projects and new jobs, alongside disruptions of the kind that accompanied the China shock and the industrial revolution.

On the agent economy's scale, Cowen resists the word explosion. Asked when agents will be 10 percent of GDP, he says not soon. If forced to guess, he could see one and a half percent within five years, and even that would be enormous, because, as he puts it, GDP is huge. The frictions are mundane and deep: websites are designed around human psychology, platforms have incentives to keep outside agents from shopping on them, and some bottlenecks live in human heads. Cowen does not let an agent manage his calendar, because writing entries himself is how he remembers what his days contain, and he does not expect that quirk to disappear even if agents get ten times better. Politics is another endogenous brake. The recent elections may slow data centers and bring regulation sooner, Cowen says, and the Texas result genuinely surprised him, but governors are already backtracking, and an extreme technological scenario would provoke an extreme political reaction that slows it from within. Money, meanwhile, buys less politics than feared: Elon Musk spent on the order of 500 million dollars and did not get the outcome he wanted, which matches what campaign finance researchers have long found.

The scarcity theme returns in a discussion of the argument, made by Dwarkesh Patel and Philip Trammell, that Piketty's model of capital pulling away from labor might fit the AI century even if it missed the last one. Imas grants the scenario but notes the alternative: value flows to whatever stays scarce, and in a rich society scarce human jobs can capture a great deal of it. Cowen insists on a distinction between capital being able to do a lot and monopoly rents sticking to particular owners. Rents follow scarcity, which makes land and energy more plausible destinations than capital in general, and AI might make even those less scarce, through cheap desalination or land reclamation. The host offers the firm's own version: the binding scarcity right now is supply, the heaviest users cannot get enough compute, and a16z has started a Machine Age Fund aimed at data centers and the infrastructure stack.

Two famous documents get graded. Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness essay stands, Cowen says, as one of the most important public intellectual pieces of recent years, but it is too enamored of fast takeoff, because it assumes simulation can substitute for experiment, and mostly it cannot. Imas adds the Lucas critique: simulations predict, but they do not account for how the system itself responds. Robin Hanson's The Age of Em is brilliant and wrong, in Cowen's telling; he told Hanson years ago that AI would get there first and that whole brain emulation, if it ever happens, would need AI before it. On Nvidia, both men return to their refrain: the episode is a lesson in checks and balances, the supply chain is diversified, and the largest real cost may be that a weakened Nvidia would be less able to act as a buyer or lender of last resort in a macroeconomic crisis. In the medium term, they expect structural forces to take over.

The closing stretch is personal. Imas tells his origin story: a refugee from Transnistria, in Moldova, who grew up on food stamps in America, went to college premed because his parents knew what a doctor was, took an economics class, found Cowen's blog Marginal Revolution and Richard Thaler on NPR, and walked away from the medical school path for a doctorate at UC San Diego. Cowen's own early signal came from chess. A prodigy at 13, he watched a computer called Tinkerbell play weakly against humans and saw the trajectory anyway, because chess players know chess runs on intuition, and a machine conquering an intuition based domain can plausibly conquer others; Kasparov, Ken Rogoff, and Demis Hassabis were early for the same reason. That faith extends to verification: if you can criticize an output, it is close to verifiable, and training the verifier makes almost anything verifiable, which is why Cowen predicts AI could write great poetry within two years given even a modest effort, and says the models already show better taste in classical music recordings than the Penguin Guide. They end on higher education, where both want far more experimentation and measurement, Imas expects the traditional sector to shrink while staying bullish on it long term, and Cowen sketches a future in which economics merges into a broader social science practiced by smart people managing fleets of AI agents, the old Chicago vision of Gary Becker finally realized.

Open questions

  1. Double digit growth requires the returns to ideas to stop diminishing, and Imas estimates that parameter well below one today. What evidence over the next few years would show a recursive self-improvement loop actually moving it, rather than just faster progress inside the old regime?
  2. Imas argues coordination, not competence, is the binding constraint on transformation. What benchmark result or real world signal would tell us agents have learned to coordinate well enough to break the ceiling on firm size?
  3. If value migrates to whatever stays scarce, whether land, energy, or particular human skills, how can workers and investors tell which of today's scarcities AI is about to make abundant?
