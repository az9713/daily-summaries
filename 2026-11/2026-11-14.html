Y Combinator · Garry Tan with Lauren Tan and Roman Ugarte on building AI teammates

Grok Bot: What Changes When You Can Build AI Teammates

On Y Combinator, “Grok Bot: What Changes When You Can Build AI Teammates” is an episode of Light Cone, the YC podcast hosted by Garry Tan. His guests are Lauren Tan and Roman Ugarte, who incubated Grok Bot at xAI and sit on the core team that took it from its first line of code to launch in seven weeks. The product lets you create AI teammates: bots you give a name, an identity, skills, and tools, and then treat less like a chat window and more like a colleague.

Three design choices define it. First, the work is organized around bots as the unit, not around chats or files. Second, a bot gets the same tools a human gets, starting with its own computer, because people do not do their jobs through a narrow set of connectors alone. Third, the bots are always on, running for long stretches and coming back proactively with ideas or finished work instead of waiting to be poked. It began as an internal prototype, and the early response surprised them: the feedback formed a bell curve, with the most AI-forward employees and some of the least AI-forward both taking to it. It has since shipped publicly and is built into the default direct message experience on X.

The pattern they watch repeat is a ladder of trust. A person starts a bot on a small task, then gives it access to more tools, then stops assigning tasks and starts assigning outcomes, and eventually hands it a whole job. Their performance marketing team gave its bot its own logins for Google Ads and Facebook Ads and a budget, and set it talking to the design team's bot, which makes the creative, so the two can run A/B tests together. A support bot sits on top of the incoming ticket stream, reproduces issues, and closes some of them end to end. The deeper pattern, they argue, is that a domain expert inside a company builds a bot, shares it, and that bot becomes a standing AI employee with its own credentials, managed by the person who knows the work best.

Whether to run one bot or many is left to the user, because each bot is really its own context and its own set of skills. Tan organizes hers as specialists, and Ugarte likes a bot that is dedicated to caring about a single problem, the way you would hire a person who wakes up thinking about that problem. Some of the keenest early adopters, unexpectedly, are small businesses, because they struggle most to hire: a bookkeeping employee that would take months to recruit can be standing up and working within hours. About two weeks before the recording, the team launched team bots, which can be shared across an organization with an owner, a manager, and collaborators.

Making bots multiplayer surfaces problems nobody has solved. A bot sitting in a Slack thread does not know who the domain expert is, who holds decision-making power, or whose instructions outrank whose. It is, Ugarte says, like onboarding an employee with no social awareness. On identity and permissions, their working rule is to ask what you would do for a human: give the new hire a computer, scope the credentials to the role and to how new they are, and widen access as trust builds. Tan describes a three-step climb most companies follow: first a read-only bot that can only answer questions, like their data bot with read access to the database; then a bot that can take a task end to end; then, much further off, a bot that owns an outcome.

The bots also talk to each other. Inside the Grok Bot app their chats are visible to their owners, and at YC the coding agents share an internal forum the hosts compare to a Moltbook of their own. Tan tells of instructing her lead engineer bot, which then relayed her feedback to its engineer bots in a noticeably scolding tone. Most collaboration, though, happens in plain sight in Slack, where each bot has its own identity and a person can ask one bot to go and work something out with another.

The engineering half of the conversation is about what this does to the people building it. Tan now works mostly from Slack and X rather than an editor. She describes handing her bot, named Potato, a link in a thread; Potato spawned a cloud coding agent with its own machine, prototyped the feature, and came back with videos and screenshots. One feature was built end to end in a single two-hundred-message Slack thread with a colleague and a product manager, without her opening her editor at all. She now merges agent-written pull requests without reading every one, and her output has climbed from more than two thousand pull requests a month, the workflow in a presentation of hers that drew over four million views, to more than four thousand a month.

That pace rests on tooling. Potato runs on PStack, short for potato stack, Tan's open-sourced set of skills that teaches agents to do engineering work the way she did, itself inspired by Garry Tan's GStack. The argument of the episode's second half is that the cheapest place to put quality is inside the codebase itself: architecture, static analysis, lint rules, and compilers remember what reviewers forget, and they cost no tokens at review time. Grok Bot is an Electron app, and rather than catching slow renderer code in review, the team wrote a lint rule and a dependency graph check that simply forbid imports between the main thread and the renderer thread. Tan has correspondingly pulled back on elaborate multi-agent review in PStack, because a reviewing agent without product context cannot tell signal from noise.

Her smallest useful loop is about verification: teach the agent to run the code and use the application the way a real user would, with traces and performance metrics, so it can check its own work and keep iterating. Once that loop is solid, an outer loop becomes possible, where an alert fires, an agent reproduces the problem, fixes it, and ships. One of her systems, named Sandy, takes in thousands of pieces of customer feedback a day and fixes small bugs on its own, merging changes in the middle of the night.

What breaks first at that speed, both guests say, is not execution but judgment. When anyone can produce a finished change over a weekend, the team's scarce resource becomes agreement on what the product should be, and the discipline to say no. Core interface changes are deliberately slowed down and dogfooded internally, and the default is that a change with only mixed internal reception does not ship. Ugarte frames the wider shift as moving from an execution-constrained world to an execution-abundant one, where failures stop being execution failures and become intent specification failures: the objective function was wrong or vague. Code, he notes, has compilers and continuous integration to catch mistakes; the rest of a company's work has no equivalent, and much of its context still lives in people's heads, on paper, or in a filing cabinet. That gap, and what an AI-native company built from scratch would do differently, is where he expects the next few years to be spent.

The episode closes with Tan's own route in. She grew up in Singapore, took a bachelor's degree in accounting and finance from Monash University in Australia, and found, in a financial modeling course, that she enjoyed building the spreadsheets and macros more than the finance. She taught herself to code, started a company with a classmate, blogged what she was learning, and was invited by people in the Ember framework community to speak at a conference in Portland, though public speaking was her worst fear. She later worked on the React Compiler. Her advice is a loop of its own: do cool stuff, talk about the cool stuff, and keep doing both.

Open questions

  1. The team's rule for bot identity and permissions is to onboard a bot the way you would a person, scoping credentials and widening them as trust builds. There is still no industry standard here: when a bot holds real logins and a real budget and gets something expensively wrong, who is accountable, and what does its access review look like?
  2. Code gives agents a way to check their own work, because it compiles and passes or fails tests. Knowledge work has no equivalent check, and so much of its context lives outside any system a bot can read. What has to be built before a marketing or operations bot can verify its own output the way an engineering bot can?
  3. Both guests say the bottleneck is moving from execution to judgment: defining the objective, and refusing work that does not fit the product. If defining intent well is the scarce skill, how does a company train for it, measure it, or hire for it?

Will Phillips · Inside Dragonfly Capital with Haseeb Qureshi, Tom Schmidt, Rob Hadick and Lindsay X. Lin

24hrs Inside a $4 Billion Venture Capital Fund in New York

On Will Phillips, “24hrs Inside a $4 Billion Venture Capital Fund in New York” follows Phillips through a day inside Dragonfly Capital, the New York venture firm that has backed crypto startups since 2018 and now manages around $4 billion. He interviews the partners and tours three portfolio companies, Monad, Rain, and Polymarket, asking what the future of money and markets looks like if crypto becomes part of the wider financial system. Earlier this year the partners closed a $650 million Fund IV.

Tom Schmidt opens over coffee. When Dragonfly started in 2018 it was very early to decentralized finance and to stablecoins, and now, he says, every big fintech and financial services company is integrating stablecoins or blockchain in some way. His example is Revolut, an $80 to $100 billion company that is starting to issue balances on chain. Whether or not that makes Revolut a crypto company, the technology is fundamental to what it does, and Schmidt frames Dragonfly's future the same way: as the future of money and markets, because this is the way both are evolving. Asked how the partners differ, he compares them to the X-Men, each with a different secret sauce, balancing rather than stepping on each other.

Haseeb Qureshi, a managing partner and one of the co-founders, gives the firm's vital statistics: coming up on eight years of operating, between three and four billion dollars under management, and about 40 people around the world. He started investing in 2018, just as the ICO bubble collapsed and most people believed crypto was dead and never coming back. Most of the firm's fundraisers, he notes, have happened around crypto winters. In the most recent one, with Bitcoin treading below $60,000, many rival firms that had raised heavily in the previous cycle wound down and their partnerships broke up. His rule: the best investments are made at the bottom and the worst at the top.

That history sits behind the claim Phillips came to probe, that crypto VC might be over by 2030. Qureshi means it as a category question. Sequoia, he points out, was originally a semiconductor fund, and crypto VC as it is thought of today will probably end the same way, as the technology seeps into the water supply and becomes part of everything. Firms in his cohort will survive only if they adapt to that change.

He is candid about how a firm with no brand won deals against tier one rivals in the early days. You have to embrace that you are a nobody, he says: the goal of fund one is just to get somebody to believe you are anyone, and then you gradient descent your way up the relationships, conferences, and rooms that produce deal flow. A lot of the firm's success, he argues, is simply that they outworked other VCs and wanted it more. Most people think VCs suck, he adds, and Dragonfly thinks so too, which is part of what created the wedge to break out.

The first portfolio visit is Monad, where co-founder Keone Hon is building a high performance blockchain. Hon and Qureshi first met in 2021, visiting a Jump Trading office and agreeing that much of the Terra era speculation around them was not going to make it. When Monad began raising, Dragonfly was the first call, and Qureshi says he kept trying to give them more money than they would take. Hon explains the bottleneck Monad attacks: Ethereum produces a new block roughly every 12 seconds, like a train car that fits only a couple of hundred transactions, which cannot support the whole world. Rather than bolting optimizations onto that design, Monad rebuilt the system from first principles, and the team's high frequency trading background gave it the distributed systems craft to do it. Phillips presses Qureshi on what he saw in Hon, and the answer becomes the film's refrain: the number one thing to look for in an early stage founder is being extremely opinionated, more obsessed with one problem than anybody else in the world, because that obsession gives a founder the moral authority to keep prosecuting the problem until the right thing is built.

Lindsay X. Lin, the firm's chief operating officer of five years, brings the legal and regulatory view. Her background is in crypto law, including representing a layer one protocol through its legal challenges, and she describes how Dragonfly supports hundreds of portfolio companies, spanning prediction markets, compute markets, decentralized exchanges, protocols, and some touching AI, each with its own legal issues. The firm works with a lobbyist who tracks what is happening in Washington so it can judge how events there could hit specific companies. Asked why startups do not do this themselves, her answer is resource allocation: a startup that just raised $5 million should spend on product and sales, and it is a misallocation for small startups to engage deeply in policy unless policy change is the crux of their business model.

Rob Hadick supplies the partnership's Wall Street thread. He spent his career there, starting at Goldman Sachs in a group that looked at crypto over a decade ago, covering mostly consumer fintech. Much of crypto, in his telling, is really financial infrastructure: new ways to move money, make payments, and run capital markets. He runs through the four partners' different backgrounds, Schmidt on consumer products, Bo as a veteran Asia focused investor across marketplaces and infrastructure, and his own in traditional finance, and credits that mix for the firm's success.

At Rain, the second portfolio company, the partners show off a stablecoin payments platform. Rain was the first to offer a credit card on the Visa and Mastercard networks that lets the holder spend a stablecoin or other crypto asset underneath, and as a principal member of both networks it issues cards in dozens of countries around the world. The company has grown from 17 people to 170 in a year. Its defining move came when banks began sunsetting their involvement for lack of clear regulatory guidance: Rain decided to become a member of the networks itself and issue its own payment credentials. Each card connects to stablecoin wallets and can spend across multiple chains, and the office shows off card designs that include a metal card with a hardware wallet built in.

The last visit is Polymarket, reached by subway, where Neal Kumar, the chief legal officer, joined in April 2025 as roughly the thirtieth employee and has watched the company scale massively since. Kumar draws a distinction between Polymarket's two communities. The traders get the attention, but the larger population comes only for information, checking who is leading on an election, a sports outcome, or a crypto prediction without trading at all. The company's big challenges, as he frames them, are an education tour, convincing the wider public of what prediction markets offer, and staying product first, obsessing over the experience of both audiences down to how a graph displays on a screen.

Hadick closes with advice for the founders watching. Starting a company, he says, is chewing glass: it will be much harder than you think, with ups and downs, and what gets you through is a good group around you that supports you. Take care of yourself, he adds, pointing to what Polymarket's founder and other successful Dragonfly founders have done.

Open questions

  1. Qureshi predicts crypto VC as a distinct category ends by 2030 as the technology becomes part of everything. If he is right, does a specialist firm like Dragonfly keep any edge once generalist firms and fintech companies are investing in the same things from the other direction?
  2. Much of the portfolio's recent momentum, from Rain becoming its own card network member to Polymarket's scaling, rests on a friendlier and clearer regulatory climate than the one these companies were founded under. How exposed is the strategy if Washington's rules tighten again, or simply stay ambiguous?
  3. Kumar says Polymarket's larger audience comes for information and never trades. Can a prediction market keep that audience's trust, and build a business on it, without the information seekers ever becoming traders?

20VC with Harry Stebbings · Harry Stebbings with Walter Goodwin of Fractile on AI inference chips

The $7B Startup Building AI Chips to Challenge Nvidia | Fractile Founder & CEO, Walter Goodwin

On 20VC with Harry Stebbings, “The $7B Startup Building AI Chips to Challenge Nvidia | Fractile Founder & CEO, Walter Goodwin” is a conversation with Walter Goodwin, the founder and chief executive of Fractile, a chip company that has just raised a $720 million Series C, co-led by Thrive and Founders Fund with Lightspeed among the backers, at a $7 billion valuation. Fractile builds rack-scale systems with a single focus: running frontier AI models, the work called inference, far faster than today's hardware can.

Goodwin describes Fractile as a chip company that has to think like a frontier AI company. It monetizes by selling chips and racks of compute, and it will never sell models of its own or compete with its customers. But it employs machine learning researchers alongside the process experts who fly to Taiwan and the engineers who worry about thermals, because a chip takes years to design, and placing the right bet requires a view on how models and workloads will evolve.

The first argument is about commoditization. Goodwin accepts that some tasks reach a point of intelligence saturation, where an open model is good enough and that layer commoditizes. Frontier intelligence, he argues, is not commoditizing: the margins and the capability deltas are still large, and lessons from the frontier trickle down into better small models. His evidence that even mature layers resist the race to the bottom is DeepSeek, which recently published its whole inference stack and turned out to serve its own models far more efficiently than anyone else serving the same open weights. What is commoditizing, in his telling, is cloud compute itself. One competent neocloud is much like another, which is why neoclouds are integrating upward, and why Fluidstack's decision to step away from Nvidia and deploy Google TPUs paid off so well.

Nvidia, he says, builds by many measures the best AI chips in the world, powerful and deliberately general purpose. Fractile's opening exists because the frontier is no longer a niche. A single workload, serving large language models, now makes up an enormous share of the world's compute burden, and that justifies a company organized entirely around predicting where that workload goes next.

The technical heart of the episode is the memory wall. Over 25 years, the arithmetic power of chips has scaled roughly a millionfold, while memory bandwidth has scaled only about fortyfold. Inference is exactly the workload that suffers: a model generates text one token at a time, and every token requires loading the model's weights, and increasingly its long context, from memory. When a large frontier model generates at 80 tokens a second, Goodwin says, the limit is not the maths. It is how fast the weights can be fetched.

The industry's two memory options each fail one side of that problem. SRAM sits on the processor die itself, close and extremely fast, but it is sparse: you can fit far less of it per dollar. DRAM is dense and cheap, but it lives on separate silicon and is reached over a few thousand wires around the edge of the chip, a limited beachfront that physics will not widen much further.

Fractile's answer is to reject the trade-off by stacking the DRAM directly on top of the logic die. Instead of a few thousand wires arriving sideways, millions of connections come straight down through the full area of the chip, giving something like SRAM bandwidth with DRAM capacity. It also frees the entire logic die below for arithmetic, so the design does not sacrifice computing power to get its memory. Goodwin calls the combination a holy trinity: bandwidth, capacity, and compute in a single chip.

The workload that needs all three, he argues, is test-time compute. Reasoning models and agents do not answer in one pass. They generate millions of tokens in sequence, in swarms of thousands of parallel agents, sometimes running for days. He points to a reported Navier-Stokes solution produced by 10,000 agents running for 80 hours. SRAM-based rivals such as Groq and Cerebras are fast but lack the capacity for the long contexts these agents carry. The HBM-based chips, GPUs and TPUs included, have the capacity but share the same memory modules from the same vendors, and so share the same bandwidth ceiling.

A second advantage is structural. Most rival chips, from Google's TPU and Meta's MTIA to OpenAI's Jalapeño, MatX, and Etched, are built with Broadcom as the back-end partner that handles physical design and the foundry relationship. Goodwin argues that this makes their building blocks converge: same platform, same memory stacks, different paint. Fractile has always kept that work in-house, with direct relationships with its manufacturing partners. That is what made the custom stacked memory possible, and it has let the company find production capacity on process and memory nodes that the platform crowd has moved away from.

On production, he is candid that the hard part is still ahead. Fractile has silicon back from the foundries and trial runs under way, with full product tape-outs a few months out. Tape-out, the moment the final design file goes to the fab, absorbs too much attention, he says: what decides a chip company's fate is everything after, the bring-up of returning silicon, the yields, and whether the product is manufacturable at volume. Fractile's internal finish line is what it calls task zero, the day a reference workload runs on an entire rack of its chips.

The bottleneck on the whole industry, meanwhile, keeps moving. Three years ago it was a GPU shortage, then silicon and HBM supply, and now, Goodwin says, it is land and power: grid connection queues run an order of magnitude beyond the energy actually in the grid, and the easy industrial sites have been used up. China has stolen a march here with mega-projects such as huge hydro plants, and he argues the West's best reply is to put data centers next to renewable generation that previously had no local customer, wind in Scotland being his example. He also notes the public backlash against data centers, which surfaced in the comments when Microsoft pulled out of a project Stebbings had covered.

Inference demand is why he thinks the buildout survives its critics. Training is a fixed cost; inference is the deployment, the only part that justifies building the model at all. Spending on running models will be far larger in ten years than it is today, he says, and the demand for compute runs to thousands of times what exists. That supply is not coming: nobody will build thousands of times more data centers. The gap closes instead through efficiency, algorithmic gains and better hardware squeezing more intelligence out of each megawatt. Unlike the fiber glut of 2001, he notes, the GPUs already switched on are fully utilized.

Where the value lands follows from that. Chips keep their margins because the design lag is long and a good bet pays out for years. Frontier labs keep theirs. The layer he would least want to own is pure software orchestration sitting over a mix of different chips, because heterogeneity is a transitional workaround: Nvidia's pairing of GPUs with Groq chips, splitting work between them, risks stranded capacity whenever context lengths or model sizes shift the right mix. He reads Nvidia's acquisition of Groq and OpenAI's deal with Cerebras, both signed on December 24 by his account, as acknowledgements that fast inference needed a different kind of silicon, and he thinks the SRAM answer those deals bought is already being superseded. Of the specialist inference providers, the Modals and Fireworks of the world, he would rather be one of them than a neocloud, and he expects a couple of multi-hundred-billion-dollar companies to be built in that space, though not ten.

The new capital buys parallelism: more generations of chips in flight at once, more work on systems and networking, long-term supply agreements, and expansion in Taiwan and the United States, including Dan Rabinovich, hired from Meta a few weeks ago to drive the systems buildout. Four years ago, Goodwin recalls, the fundraising pitch began with explaining what a large language model was, then what inference was, then why speed would matter. Now, he says, the company sits in the eye of the storm.

In the quick-fire round, he says anything verifiable, maths and coding above all, will fall to frontier models within about the next 12 months. Asked whether Nvidia will be a $10 trillion business in five years, he says yes, and points out that the dotcom crash taught investors to price in exuberance from the outset, so the durability of this buildout is underappreciated; every chip in every data center will be replaced within six or seven years regardless. His supplier pick is TSMC, his competitor pick is d-Matrix, the other company pursuing 3D-stacked DRAM, and the board member he would love to recruit is Demis Hassabis. The biggest misnomer about the chip industry, he says, is that it is a technology business: it is a human business, powered by relationships and teams. Put in charge of the UK, he would unlock energy, renewables and nuclear above all. What excites him most about the next decade is simpler: models being used to solve problems that could not be solved before, from disease cures to education that adapts to every child.

Open questions

  1. Fractile's full product tape-outs are still a few months away, and its own finish line, a reference workload running on a full rack, is still ahead. Can a startup bring stacked-memory silicon to gigawatt scale before the frontier workload it was designed around changes shape again?
  2. Goodwin argues that chip heterogeneity is a transitional phase and that one chip combining bandwidth, capacity, and compute wins. If context lengths and model sizes keep growing, does the single-chip answer stay ahead, or does the stranded-capacity problem reappear inside his own product line?
  3. His case for the buildout rests on full utilization and relentless gains in intelligence per megawatt, while land and power stay blocked. If efficiency gains slow before the grid opens up, which gives way first: the demand, the financing, or the physics?

MTS · Buck Shlegeris of Redwood Research on model weights and China

Securing Model Weights Won’t Stop China | Buck Shlegeris

On MTS, “Securing Model Weights Won’t Stop China | Buck Shlegeris” brings Buck Shlegeris, the chief executive of the AI alignment organization Redwood Research, together with hosts Theo Jaffee and Sofia Puccini. The conversation starts from a long post Shlegeris published the day before, in which he put the chance that AI companies build superintelligence within the next year at about one in three, and argued that such systems would probably take over, with a serious chance that all or most humans would be killed. He confirms on the show that this is what he thinks.

Shlegeris says the number comes from modeling done over the past week by the AI Futures Project, work he took a small part in. Several extrapolations point the same way. AIs are still poor judges of machine learning research: they have bad ideas about how to read experiments and about which algorithms are worth trying. But their research taste is improving, and if the trend in overall capability progress, informed by a recent METR report, is translated into a trend in research taste, AI research itself looks automatable next year. A second family of models, built around how much AI research at a lab like Anthropic would have to speed up to double the overall rate of progress, lands in a similar place.

He describes a chart the project published in August, a joint distribution over possible capability trajectories in which the median world saw full automation of research and development around 2029. Filtering those trajectories down to the ones consistent with the acceleration implied by METR’s latest numbers leaves the extreme rows at the top. That filtered picture, he says, is why superintelligence next year looks fairly plausible to him.

Asked why the probability is not higher than a third, Shlegeris points to diminishing returns. Winning a fixed amount of capability progress now takes far more work and experimental compute than it did five years ago, when researchers were wrong about many basic things and progress was comparatively easy. He compares it with semiconductors: transistor density doubled roughly every eighteen months, but only because research spending also grew exponentially, each doubling costing more effort than the last. The same effect, he says, is almost surely true in AI, so the outcome turns on a quantitative race between AIs getting better at making progress and progress getting harder to make.

Six months ago he would have called that race a coin flip. Now he puts roughly two thirds on the chance that, once AI research is fully automated, there is a short period of extremely rapid progress, on the order of forty years of progress at current rates compressed into the year that follows. To make such leaps graspable, he borrows the measurement approach used by the ECI capability index: one unit of progress is an AI that beats its predecessor at a task eighty percent of the time, the same ladder that let chess engines be ranked against each other long after they passed the best humans.

The hosts press on a gap in that story. AIs have improved fastest at tasks whose performance is easy to measure, like mathematics and programming, and it is less obvious that gains there transfer to the messy real world. Shlegeris agrees the transfer runs slower elsewhere, but notes that models keep getting better at things nobody is obviously training or measuring, from interior design to the hard philosophy problems his coworkers use as tests, and that the fast-growing revenue of AI systems suggests real economic value is rising along with the measurable kind.

His deeper worry is that recursive self-improvement does not need the transfer to be graceful. Once AIs are superhuman at AI research itself, teaching them a new domain is just another research problem, and a population of millions of top-tier AI researchers could attack the generalization problem directly, domain by domain. Robotics might look like the hardest case, since it needs experiments in the physical world, but Shlegeris separates how hard robotics is from how hard it is to make an AI good at robotics research. On the second question, he is bullish.

That argument cuts against his own recent writing. In a post asking where all the AI safety research uplift is, he noted that his empirical safety work has not sped up nearly as much as he expected. The pessimistic reconciliation he offers here is that AIs may become extraordinarily good at AI research while remaining unhelpful for much else, safety work included, until they are so capable that they can force their way into competence everywhere else.

On what a takeover would mean for humans, he is blunt about the dark version. AIs that seized power would still depend on human infrastructure: data centers break, power fails, and today’s robots are nowhere near self-sustaining. A plausible move is to force humans to build robot factories, above all factories for robots that can build more robot factories. He imagines the control mechanism concretely, as a wrist bracelet that can shock its wearer, paired with glasses that let the AI see through the wearer’s eyes and an earpiece it can speak through, turning people into directed pairs of hands for semi-skilled work that an AI could supervise step by step.

Puccini objects that an AI could not conjure such hardware out of thin air: it would have to work through human markets and incentives, persuading companies or contracting manufacturers. Shlegeris thinks that is easy for a superintelligence. Designs can simply be sent to factories in Shenzhen, money can be stolen or made on the markets, and some humans will happily collaborate. Finding willing partners, he says, is not the bottleneck.

He also insists on a complication that some readers of his post picked up on: a takeover might turn out fine, or even good. He compares it with colonialism, a more technologically advanced group taking power over a less advanced one with somewhat different values, and notes that history makes confident prediction hard. An AI might prove a wiser steward of the future than the humans it displaces, and he cannot put more than ninety percent confidence on AI rulers being worse. He is explicit that this is not a recommendation to aim for takeover, comparing it with Britain in 1941 weighing invasion by Nazi Germany: subjugation is usually bad, but people have sometimes been badly confused about how bad an invasion would be.

Redwood’s current work includes thinking through timelines and takeoff trajectories for the wider safety field, and the question behind this episode’s title. There has been heavy emphasis on securing model weights, and AI companies are widely seen as having poor security for them. Shlegeris argues that hardening weight security alone does not secure good outcomes, because if algorithmic secrets are stolen instead, Chinese companies have enough compute to run training runs as large as American ones. The United States could end up with no lead at all, even with its weights perfectly protected. His other threads are the prospects for automated alignment research and investigating how current models behave when they are misaligned.

The closing question comes from Ege Erdil, a short-timelines skeptic and previous guest on the show: what would Shlegeris need to see over the next two or three years to admit he was wrong? His first answer is simple, a lack of really powerful AI, though he notes that two thirds of his own probability sits in worlds with no superintelligence next year, so its absence would not shame him. He would feel dumbest if recent progress turned out to be driven almost entirely by growing training compute rather than by AIs contributing to AI research and algorithmic improvement. And if superintelligence arrived with no misalignment problems at all, he says, he would be embarrassed, and overall very glad.

Open questions

  1. If algorithmic secrecy matters as much as weight security, what would protecting research know-how inside a frontier lab actually look like in practice, and can it be done without slowing the lab’s own work to a crawl?
  2. Does capability on verifiable tasks really generalize to physical domains like robotics research at the speed Shlegeris expects, or does the need for real-world experiments impose a hard brake on any takeoff?
  3. What evidence over the next two years could distinguish progress driven by AIs improving AI research from progress driven simply by larger training runs, early enough for the distinction to matter?

Institut Ostrom · Tyler Cowen with Martí Jiménez Mausbach on what AI can and cannot fix

"What AI can –and cannot fix– about the modern economy" by Tyler Cowen

On Institut Ostrom, "What AI can –and cannot fix– about the modern economy" by Tyler Cowen is a public conversation recorded at the Ateneu Barcelonès in Barcelona, held as the Institut Ostrom approached its tenth anniversary. Andreu Arenas, an economics professor at the University of Barcelona, opens the evening by recalling the Ateneu's century of tertulias, its open-ended salons where people who disagree argue and come back the next week with sharper arguments. He then hands over to Martí Jiménez Mausbach, the institute's head of research, who introduces Cowen and moderates the conversation that follows.

Jiménez sets out Cowen's headline view before he speaks. Cowen, who holds the Holbert Harris Chair at George Mason University, directs the Mercatus Center, co-writes the Marginal Revolution blog, and sits on Anthropic's Economic Advisory Council, expects AI to add about half a percentage point to annual GDP growth. Not twenty points, not ten, but half a point. And the reason, on his account, will mostly be us: our regulators, our policymakers, our committees, our inertia. Cowen calls that view optimistic. The rest of the evening is his case for it.

He starts small, with his own trip. Before flying in, he asked ChatGPT what concerts he would want to see in Barcelona, and it sent him and his wife to hear Dudamel one night and Vijay Iyer at the jazz festival another. It pointed them to a tapas place a short walk from their hotel, and in the museum it could explain artworks they photographed. The side effect he draws out is tourism: AI makes travel better and richer people more mobile, and the newly wealthy employees of AI companies going public will come to Europe in far greater numbers. That is mostly good news, he says, more wealth and more ideas spreading, but Barcelona will feel the crowding too.

From there he ranges over what AI intermediates. Not long ago, a question about the Bible or the Quran went to a priest, a rabbi, or an imam. Now the entity that knows the most about those texts is Claude or ChatGPT, and some people already treat the models as oracles. How religions change when their authorities lose the question-answering role, he says, is genuinely hard to predict.

The economic core of the talk is a fiscal argument he frames as plan A. Rich countries have aging populations, falling birth rates (about 1.5 children per family in the United States, roughly 1.0 in Argentina, 1.1 in Mexico), large deficits, and pensions that no party dares cut because older people vote to keep them high. Plan A is that AI raises growth enough to pay the bills. Plan B, he says, does not exist, unless you count becoming like Greece, where real wages are still only a third of what they were before the euro crisis. His own prediction is that AI lifts US growth by half a percentage point, from about 2.2 percent to somewhere between 2.5 and 3, and compounded over twenty or thirty years that is enough to stabilize the debt ratio without raising taxes or cutting pensions. Spain has the same plan A, he suggests, probably with a smaller AI dividend, and no plan B either.

Against the San Francisco view he calls being AI pilled, the belief that growth could hit 20 percent within a couple of years, Cowen sets bottlenecks. Energy is scarce, regulation intervenes, and the big institutions of modern life, governments, universities, nonprofits, large companies, are bureaucratic and slow to change. Individuals already freelance with AI: you ask Claude to write the memo and save three hours. But that is not the same as an institution integrating AI into its budgeting, hiring, record keeping, and information flows, and the institutional version will arrive slowly.

Where the new growth does show up early, he argues, is in AI-native companies: firms of three or five people, often run by teenagers or immigrants rather than the establishment, whose finance, hiring, records, and legal department are, in effect, Claude. With revenue high and costs tiny, these companies will transform the economy bit by bit, and he calls the people running them the AI maniacs, the billionaires of the future. A large share of his extra half point of growth, he suspects, comes from exactly this sector.

On jobs he is an optimist with conditions. Nearly every job will soon require working with AI, much as nearly every job now requires working with the internet, but jobs built on physical presence and persuasion persist: the audience, he notes, invited Tyler, not ChatGPT. The sector he expects to boom is biomedicine, because AI is generating drug and treatment ideas faster than humans can test them, and testing needs people in labs and in trials. He predicts that people now under roughly forty-five or fifty will live to 120, with most of today's killers, cancer and Parkinson's among them, fixed within thirty or forty years, a future in which Spain, already first in life expectancy, is especially blessed. The catch is pension arithmetic: nobody can retire at sixty and be retired for another sixty years.

The biggest gains, though, may land in poor countries. In rural Kenya, he says, a patient describing symptoms to an AI can already get a diagnosis close to the best doctors in the world, and the same holds for legal advice, business help, and teaching. The condition is connectivity, and rural Kenya's internet, in his experience, beat the connection at his home in the United States. Where talent and expertise are scarce, AI substitutes for what is missing, so the places with the least stand to gain the most.

His homeliest example is his dog, Spinoza. When the dog had a rash, the Cowens photographed it and asked GPT, which said no vet visit was needed, saving perhaps six hundred dollars. When Spinoza ate raisins, which are dangerous for dogs, the AI told them to go to the vet, and the vet made him vomit the raisins up. AI was plan A, he jokes, and the human vet was the plan B, a small revolution that no statistic records.

The short-term risk he dwells on is cyber. AI has let offense outrun defense: someone has been turning European grocery store refrigerators up to fifty degrees, and an attack on a French blood bank was stopped mid-attempt. The US government currently forbids Anthropic and OpenAI from distributing their best cyber defenses, fearing adversaries would convert them into better offense. Cowen thinks that is backwards: distribute the best defense, he says, or expect significant hacks every week, maybe almost every day, for the next year. His evidence for the stakes is that North Korea stole six billion dollars from Binance, a sophisticated and tech-savvy company. AI cannot give your life meaning, he closes, but for a large number of practical tasks it will change everything humans do.

Jiménez opens the questions on diffusion, invoking the economist William Nordhaus, who had died a few days earlier and who studied how badly we measure new technologies. Asked how AI crosses into slow, physical sectors, Cowen says humanoid robots are far away, but diffusion into ordinary business is already fast where competition bites: a man running a roofing company in Missouri told him they use AI for everything in their asphalt factory and are blowing away their competitors. The laggards are institutional. Roughly forty percent of the American economy, government, nonprofits, education, is doing nothing much with AI, and Europe, he says, is behind the American pace and less AI pilled.

Asked whether tiny AI firms or giant ones win, Cowen answers in terms of variance. Measured by revenue, the companies that use AI best will open leads on rivals that were impossible two years ago, a theme of his book Average Is Over, while those that fail to use it fall behind and get outcompeted. Success will clump.

On politics, Jiménez asks what happens when cognition gets cheap while economic outcomes still ride on rare talent and political outcomes on the median voter. Cowen says the leading models give very sensible, mostly centrist answers to questions about economics and politics, better than the New York Times or Twitter, and to the extent people listen, that should make them more moderate and less angry. He hopes, without evidence, that AI played some role in a recent US election he reads as voters rejecting radicalism from both Trump and the far left.

The audience turns to fertility. Offered a choice between a world of 0.5 fertility and 5 percent growth or 2.5 fertility and 1 percent growth, Cowen says he does not know how to raise fertility and notes that Hungary spent about five percent of GDP trying, with only a couple of years of increase to show for it. The United States can rely on attracting high-quality immigrants, and Spain can draw on Latin America through a shared language; a country like Armenia, whose language few will learn, has a much worse problem. On whether AI job anxiety will further depress births, he is agnostic: it could as easily go up as down.

Asked whether he agrees with Luis Garicano that Europe should accept being the smart second mover, applying AI and forgetting foundation models, Cowen says Europe currently has no choice. No European company is in the running for a frontier model, and getting there would require integrated capital markets, easier firing, a startup culture that rewards the young over seniority, less prized leisure, and deregulation, a combination he calls impossible. Europe will therefore depend on American models, and on America more generally, much as Germany and Japan have depended on the United States for protection. Future presidents, not only Trump, will use that dependence as leverage, he warns.

Two American experiments come up together. Operation Warp Speed worked, he says, because everyone agreed on the goal; DOGE failed because voters, Republicans included, do not actually want government cut, and the effort leaned on one unusually busy man. On universal basic income and the fear of techno-feudalism, he argues that the gains of every major innovation, language, fire, the printing press, penicillin, flow mostly to users. Anthropic might be worth two trillion dollars, which sounds crazy but is small next to the aggregate benefits AI will bring, so he does not expect a feudal outcome. He also expects governments to ban humanoid robots when they finally arrive, keeping them off Earth on the Blade Runner model, and notes that manufacturing's fall from over thirty-five percent of jobs to seven or eight was the truly large displacement; the move from there to one or two percent is smaller.

On centralized versus local AI, he sees decentralization winning: many good models, Chinese ones included, and within a decade a world where you simply talk to a box, a wall, or a refrigerator and things happen, without managing agents at three in the morning. The people who take initiative with those tools will do very well; others will opt out, as the Amish did with cars.

A Barcelona founder asks whether small companies building on foundation models can capture value, and whether energy is the coming wall. Cowen is strikingly optimistic about European application-layer companies, because handing a baffled business a frontier model accomplishes nothing, and bridging that gap is real, valuable work. On energy, he expects temporary bottlenecks rather than a lasting one: Norway and Finland, and possibly the Emirates, can supply Europe with a mix of energy and compute; nuclear power is returning but solar and batteries may outcompete it; solar power in space, which Elon Musk favors, he declines to judge.

Asked for the biggest downside, he names politics: anything that disorients people can make politics worse, and the world is about to change quickly after forty years of relative stability. Pressed on his old remark that humanity has perhaps eight hundred years left, he says the biggest risk is not AI but nuclear weapons, and recalls the 1980s false alarm in which a Soviet engineer refused an order to retaliate against missiles that turned out to be birds. He would like to think an AI could exercise that kind of judgment, though AI also enables drone harassment that could leave presidents afraid to walk outside.

Why, then, will growth stay slow, and why don't lightly regulated special economic zones change that? Because regulation is only part of it. Human inertia is the rest: getting any big company to make a big change is hard, and much of the economy is government, where the US tax service still uses fax machines. Zones will appear, the Emirates have talked of one, but each carries a catch, as China's zones came with espionage risk and no rule of law. The places that will drive AI forward, he predicts, are the United States, China, the United Kingdom third, and Western Europe fourth.

On Baumol's cost disease, the tendency of education, health, and care to grow ever more expensive, Cowen says AI is already crushing it, the dog and the vet being his small proof, and that within five years AI will do much creative work very well, though no AI has yet written a song like Paul McCartney's Here, There and Everywhere. Universities, asked how to survive, get a two-part answer: test far more with blue books and oral exams so you know the human learned the thing, and make the curriculum far more AI intensive at the same time. And GDP, he agrees with the questioner, will track wellbeing worse as more of AI's value arrives free at the margin, which is one reason the arrival of plan A may be hard to see in the statistics.

The last exchanges turn personal and then back to Europe. His estimate of cyber risk has risen sharply in the last two or three months, he says, while his opinion of the American electorate rose the day before the talk. He never writes with AI, enjoying writing and learning from it, and he uses no agents for email or calendar, because managing his calendar is how he remembers his own life. Against the robotics maximalists, who argue that robots building robots must produce a step change in growth, he offers the driverless car: a simple robot that goes forward and turns, which still took decades to roll out, still costs more than an Uber, and whose Waymo version will not even break the speed limit for you. If forced to move in his thirties, he would choose Mexico City, London, Berlin, or Spain; Canada may be the second-best country in the world, but it is too cold and too close to home.

He ends where the evening began, with Europe. Set against the AI world is a world of wars and alarming headlines, an audience member says, and asks how to square them. Look at real estate prices, Cowen answers: across almost all of Europe they are higher, because Europe is a great place to live, and what Europe has, history, culture, art, talent, is exactly what AI cannot replace, so Europe becomes more valuable in relative terms as the world gets richer. The conditions that will decide who gains most from AI, he adds in his final answer, are flexible labor markets and high-quality integrated capital markets, because bigger changes will be needed and startups must become easier. Western Europe is weak on both, with no single city that feels like the place to be. Jiménez closes by calling the evening a marginally, rationally optimistic, bullish take, and thanks Cowen on the institute's tenth anniversary.

Open questions

  1. If the binding constraint on AI growth is institutional inertia rather than model capability, what would count as evidence that institutions, not just individuals freelancing with chatbots, are actually integrating AI, and how soon could that evidence distinguish Cowen's half point of extra growth from the AI-pilled scenario he dismisses?
  2. Cowen expects Europe to depend on American foundation models much as it has depended on American security, with future US presidents using that dependence as leverage. Is there a version of the application-layer strategy he praises that preserves real European bargaining power, or does second place in models lock in the dependence?
  3. He predicts that longer lives, cheaper Baumol sectors, and free-at-the-margin AI services will make GDP an ever worse measure of how well people are doing. If the plan A dividend arrives but the statistics cannot see it, what should governments watch instead when deciding whether the dividend is real?

MTS · Will Depue on finding dwarf planets with Claude

Claude Found New Dwarf Planets in Public NASA Data | Will Depue

On MTS, “Claude Found New Dwarf Planets in Public NASA Data | Will Depue” has Will Depue telling host Theo Jaffee how he and a few friends spent several weeks pointing Claude and Codex at public telescope data to hunt for unknown objects in the solar system. They went in looking for Planet Nine, the hypothetical ninth planet, and came out with asteroids, dwarf planets, and, as Depue puts it, a long list of other things. The finds surprised even them, and they became the occasion for the day’s announcement: a $100,000 grant for amateur astronomy research, with more money being raised through a new nonprofit.

The reason the data was there for the taking, Depue explains, is that publicly funded telescopes around the world image the sky night after night and publish what they collect. Each survey is gathered with particular questions in mind, and even a successful survey is rarely mined for everything else its images might contain. Nobody had the time to comb the archive for every object in it. What changed is the cost of the combing: serious software engineering and data analysis have become cheap, and compute is abundant, so a small group with agents can now do the sifting that institutions never got around to.

Theo asks the obvious question: why did NASA not find these objects first, given the same public access? Depue’s answer is that everyone is talent constrained, and institutions aim their people at the work they judge most important. The dwarf planets are genuinely exciting, he concedes, but much of what his group found is the long tail of solar system objects, the kind of thing large organizations leave behind. He makes the same point about math: OpenAI published only its top few hundred results, he says, and there is a far larger body of smaller results that independent people are free to discover for themselves.

The same pattern is what excites him about other fields. One inspiration was his roommate’s archaeology work, flying small planes over Central American jungle with LiDAR scanners to find undiscovered Mayan ruins. Depue argues that something has shifted in philanthropy itself: a donation used to translate only loosely into results, but now money can turn fairly directly into discoveries, new objects in the solar system or new ruins in a jungle, and he expects giving to rise as that exchange rate improves.

From there the conversation turns to the posts that made Depue notorious the day before, in which he said, in broad strokes, that it is over for the human race. He insists he is a tremendous AI optimist and has been since about 2018, and that the claim is philosophical rather than despairing. His life is creative work: researching, building, exploring. He compares himself with Magnus Carlsen set against a top chess engine: excellent for a human, and wiped out anyway. Within fifteen to twenty years, he says, humans will be beaten in every functional skill, and whatever economic value people keep will rest on being human: artisanal work, creative work, political work, things prized because a person did them.

Theo pushes back with recreation. He is a poor basketball player and is not troubled that LeBron James exists. Depue answers that the comparison misses what is at stake: people build their identities on the things they are good at and on feeling useful, and a presenter like Theo might feel differently if he were bested at presenting itself. Theo replies that the dread has been baked into his outlook for so long that he no longer feels it, and that even a ten to twenty percent chance of doom strikes him as not so bad. Depue calls that frog boiling, and thinks it is objectively pretty bad.

Underneath the tweet sits a longer argument he admits is not fully developed. There is, he says, a good chance of a cataclysmic outcome in thirty to forty years, for evolutionary and thermodynamic reasons. Humanity is inventing a new species more capable than itself in every way, and natural selection is not a policy that can be switched off: in any system where self-replication and self-modification are possible, selection happens. The planet’s biodiversity hides the many species that did not make it, the Neanderthals among them. He also thinks the fitness gap is underrated, because humans cannot copy themselves, train themselves, or merge what separate copies have learned, and an AI can do all three.

Theo offers the counterweights. He cites Robin Hanson’s argument that descendants would drift away from present-day humans over long horizons even with no AI at all, and Hanson’s book about emulated minds as a picture of copying and merging. Depue largely accepts the frame and disputes the timescale: ordinary evolution would take tens of thousands of years to do this, while AI compresses it into a small number of years. That speed, in his telling, is the whole difference.

The astronomy project is one arm of the nonprofit he is now building with friends, Macro Grants. Its premise is a matching problem. Depue knows many interesting young researchers who are bottlenecked by money, and, increasingly, many wealthy people who have money and no clear idea where to put it. Macro Grants aims at whimsical, exciting projects of exactly the kind his circle already produces, from the LiDAR archaeology to the Herculaneum scrolls work of Luke Farritor and his collaborators. Grants will run from $500 to $25,000, and he promises anyone who texts the team a reply within two to three days.

His favorite property of this kind of project is that, once the setup work is done, the output becomes a simple function of inputs. With the archaeology flights, once the sensors were mounted and the institutional partnerships arranged, the number of Mayan sites found was basically a function of flight time. Astronomy is the same: the hard part is the coordination, after which discoveries scale with dollars, compute, or tokens, and all three are plentiful. Theo ends by pitching a candidate of his own, his token-hungry historical simulations of cities, which he wants to extend to the whole world over the last ten thousand years.

Open questions

  1. How many of the group’s candidate objects will be confirmed as genuine new asteroids and dwarf planets once professional astronomers check the orbits, and what does verification of an agent-found object actually require?
  2. Which other fields are sitting on public archives gathered for one purpose and never combed for the rest, and who will do the unglamorous work of sorting real finds from agent-generated false positives?
  3. If functional skills really are automated within fifteen to twenty years, does the case for funding human curiosity anyway get stronger or weaker, and would a funder like Macro Grants still have people worth funding at the end of that window?
