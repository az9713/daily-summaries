WSJ Events · Dina Powell McCormick at WSJ Tech Live

Watch Live: Meta President Dina Powell McCormick at WSJ Tech | WSJ

On WSJ Events, the session "Watch Live: Meta President Dina Powell McCormick at WSJ Tech | WSJ" pairs Meta's president with a Wall Street Journal moderator at WSJ Tech Live, on the day after an election in which data centers had become a live campaign issue. The session opens with a video Meta submitted, filmed around its project in Richland Parish, Louisiana: a catering business that grew from serving 40 people to 400 a week because Meta arrived, teachers whose pay is topped up by bonuses funded from the local tax surplus, and residents describing neighbors moving back home.

The moderator's opening question sets the stakes: were data centers on the ballot last night? Powell McCormick answers that they plainly were, in campaign ads and in candidates' platforms, and that the moderator's count put 33 of 40 ballot measures restricting data centers on the winning side. Her framing is that the data center is simply the visible form of AI, the thing a community can point to and refuse, while the deeper question is how people feel about AI itself. Meta's stated rule is to build only where a community judges the investment will make a difference.

She describes the method behind that rule as a community compact, built from the Louisiana experience and now applied everywhere. Its elements, as she walks through them, are electricity, teachers, local labor, and bipartisanship, with local citizens treated as the decision makers who matter most.

On electricity, she says Meta works with Entergy in Louisiana, pays for its own power, and is structured so that residents' costs fall rather than rise, by some two billion dollars over the life of the investment, in her telling. On teachers, the parish routes any tax surplus to teacher bonuses, an arrangement she credits a Journal story with bringing to Meta's attention, and the company now looks for ways to invest in teachers and first responders in every host community. On labor, the commitment is to train local workers rather than import them. On politics, she points to a New Orleans expansion event held weeks earlier where Republican Governor Landry appeared alongside former mayor Mitch Landrieu and former senator Mary Landrieu, and reads it as proof that host communities can put an investment above party.

The scale she describes is large. Meta has been building data centers for twenty years, she says, long before the current boom made them controversial. The company operates in 23 states, five of them with projects of one gigawatt or larger, Texas among them. She also reports that a governor who won the previous night after making data centers a big part of his platform had already asked to meet once the inauguration is past, saying his state does not want to lose the investment or the jobs.

Asked whether the buildout will now stall, she concedes the likely near term: much more review, and much more attention to whether constituents actually want these projects. That, she argues, is making compute an even scarcer asset, one the market has not fully priced. Starting a brand new project in the United States is very challenging, while expanding an existing site is easier because the community has already felt benefits rather than costs. Every hyperscaler, she says, is now asking whether some building should happen outside the United States, and she notes that Singapore and Australia both saw something close to moratoriums in the past week. The future may be international, she says, and it may even be in space, but the immediate future is harder building.

On regulation, her line is that AI oversight is neither a Republican nor a Democratic project. She describes meeting, with Mark Zuckerberg, the House Democratic AI commission led by Josh Gottheimer, and the recent White House superintelligence safety accords agreed with other lab leaders, naming Jensen Huang, Greg Brockman, and Sam Altman. The accords, she argues, have teeth that went underreported. Inside Meta's superintelligence lab, more than 200 people work only on safety, reviewing how models are developed. Researchers who see a problem will be able to go straight to an independent board committee rather than through their managers. An independent auditor will oversee the process. And the standard has already bitten: Muse, Meta's consumer AI product, was delayed by more than eight weeks while the team kept fixing bugs, because the release threshold was as safe as possible.

Then the conversation turns to money. The moderator puts Meta's future spending commitments at 825 billion dollars, and Powell McCormick demurs, saying it is not quite that much. What she describes instead is machinery: a dedicated compute division working daily with chief financial officer Susan Li on a puzzle that joins financing, power, workforce, and community support. BlackRock and Larry Fink are the capital partners on the El Paso project. Entergy supplies power in Louisiana, and NextEra Energy under John Ketchum is the other power partner she names. On labor, Meta works with Sean McGarvey of the building trades and with the IBEW electricians, against an industry backdrop she sizes at ten trillion dollars of capital, 250 gigawatts of power, and two million jobs needed over the next four years, with a shortage of about 350,000 electricians today.

The market evidence she offers is reception, not price. Meta's recent equity and debt offerings were both oversubscribed, and its European bond offering closed that day heavily oversubscribed even with 18 other bond deals in the market. The story she says investors are buying is a core business with 3.6 billion people using the products daily, 200 million small businesses among them, six consecutive quarters of double digit growth, and capital spending that is visibly improving that core business. She quotes Blackstone's John Gray calling compute itself a financeable asset, and notes that Meta already gets daily calls from companies wanting to lease its compute, which she frames as a path to monetization whether or not its own products use all of it.

Asked for a plan B if the bets fail, she reaches for the company's track record rather than a hedge. She has been at Meta two years, counting board service, and she lists WhatsApp's growth, Threads passing X in monthly users, and Reels, built only five years ago, as evidence that this founder builds products first and finds the monetization after, with distribution to nearly half the world's population as the multiplier.

Her account of Meta's edge in the compute race is partly personal: she spends 80 percent of her time in Menlo Park on compute. The product evidence she cites is Muse, which she calls the number one downloaded app of the day, built on an underlying model she names as Wonder, and already at ten million users, many of them people who had never used AI before. Polling from the election, she adds, found that people who use AI view it much more favorably, which is the political version of the same point.

Muse is free for now, and the monetization path she sketches runs through small business. Only 20 percent of American small businesses use any form of AI, she says, and a Ramp study found that the adopters grew market share and revenue dramatically. In focus groups those owners said they would pay. Commerce is already flowing: the average product sold through Muse goes for about 200 dollars, to the satisfaction of connector partners she names as Plaid, DoorDash, and Stripe.

Her own use of the product is domestic as much as executive. Muse reads the news outlets and newsletters she follows each morning, writes her a detailed summary, and turns it into a thirty minute podcast. She uses it to arrange travel for her grown daughters. Friends who run small businesses, she says, use it like a chief operating officer. She also describes the glasses feeding the same system: at Bennett Orchards, a third generation grower named Henry walks the trees in Meta glasses, films the crop, and runs the footage through Muse to check inventory, spot problems, and see what the week's weather will do to the harvest.

On Washington, she argues the industry's job is to work with whichever government voters elect and to keep the work bipartisan, naming administration officials Michael Kratsios and Emil Michael among those engaged on the same questions. The workforce piece of that pitch is a program called America's Workforce Academy, which pays people to train as welders, fiber technicians, and electricians and guarantees them a job on a Meta infrastructure site. Meta deliberately left its own name off the program. She compares the moment to the country physically building the arsenal that won the Second World War, and says Zuckerberg is long on humans: the stated creed is that the future is for everyone, and that access has to be broad and fair for that to mean anything.

She closes on the most misunderstood thing about Zuckerberg. When she asked him, while discussing joining the board, why Meta would win, he answered that there would not be one winner, but that Meta would be one of the winners. He has physically moved his desk into the superintelligence lab to build alongside the researchers. And when Muse's design began with Jolly, a fuzzy creature, and the team produced a hundred alternatives, he told them to stick with their gut: the first one was really good. Her summary is founder risk taking joined to twenty years of stability, the adult in the room, as she jokes, who is not yet fifty.

Open questions

  1. If restrictive ballot measures keep passing and greenfield projects stall, does the industry's center of gravity actually shift abroad or into expansions of existing sites, and what happens to the American claim to AI leadership if it does?
  2. The safety accords rely on the labs policing themselves, with internal safety teams, a board committee, and an independent auditor. Once Congress writes federal guardrails, does that private machinery survive, merge with public oversight, or quietly become redundant?
  3. Muse is free while Meta's spending commitments climb toward the better part of a trillion dollars. At what point does consumer adoption have to turn into revenue for the compute bet to pay off, and is small business commerce really the path that gets there?

Laude Institute · Brynjolfsson, Yang and Hashimoto on reskilling with AI

Erik Brynjolfsson, Diyi Yang and Tatsunori Hashimoto on reskilling with AI | Moonshots // ONE

On Laude Institute, the episode "Erik Brynjolfsson, Diyi Yang and Tatsunori Hashimoto on reskilling with AI | Moonshots // ONE" is a long conversation about a Stanford research project called Collaborative Intelligence, hosted by Laude Institute co-founder Chris Rytting. The project is one of the Moonshots // ONE entries: it received a seed grant, spent six months developing a full lab proposal, and was one of four winning teams to receive a 10 million dollar lab. Its premise runs against the usual framing of AI at work. Instead of asking which jobs AI can take over, the team asks how AI can help people learn new skills and do new kinds of work alongside it.

The conversation opens with two competing visions that are more than sixty years old. Alan Turing asked whether a machine can think, and much of the field has chased that question ever since, building machines to be as capable as possible. Doug Engelbart asked a different question: whether a machine can help humans think better. The project belongs to the second tradition, and the team frames its work as a choice between automation, where machines substitute for what people do, and augmentation, where machines complement people and let them do things that were never possible before.

Brynjolfsson, a labor economist, makes the economic case for augmentation. Most value in history, he argues, has come not from replacing existing work but from doing entirely new things: a world that had only mechanized its old crafts would be full of clay pots and chariots, not jet engines, smartphones, and mRNA vaccines. The space of things people do not yet do is far larger than the space of things they currently do, so augmentation offers the bigger prize. It also divides the prize differently. Technology that substitutes for workers tends to push wages down and concentrate wealth, while technology that complements workers tends to raise wages, which is how most past technologies have behaved. He tells of a chief financial officer who planned to judge AI purely by headcount reduction in each department, and of his reply that a finance chief should be inventing better measures: customer satisfaction, new products, new services. What gets measured, the team argues, steers what gets built.

Hashimoto adds that the impact will be uneven because AI capability is spiky rather than a single rising tide. A calculator has outcalculated everyone for decades, yet machines remain far behind humans on many other dimensions, and that unevenness is a source of value, since differences between people and machines, and among people themselves, are what create comparative advantage. Whether AI touches your core work or only auxiliary tasks depends on the job. Terence Tao, the mathematician, uses AI for prettier figures and side tasks, not for the work that makes him a mathematician. For someone whose core task is writing boilerplate code, the change is not distant at all. The social problem, as Hashimoto puts it, is to find the people whose core work is being hit and move them toward work where they genuinely complement the models.

The early evidence is already visible if you look below the aggregate numbers. Headline unemployment has barely moved, but in the fifth of occupations most exposed to AI, employment for young workers has fallen by about 16 percent in relative terms, a pattern the team calls canaries in the coal mine. Brynjolfsson invokes the J-curve: with a powerful technology, productivity first dips while organizations reorganize processes and reskill people, and only then rises. That lag, measured in years rather than decades this time, is a window in which preparation is still possible.

Yang insists that workers themselves belong in the design loop, because the debate is not only about what technology can do but about what people want. Her earlier work uses a human agency scale from H1 to H5, running from full automation of a task to full human control, with an equal partnership in the middle. Scanning the workforce with it produced a surprise: for some core tasks, workers actually want automation, while for some auxiliary tasks they want to keep control. The project's plan has three parts built on that foundation. The first is measurement: an infrastructure to audit how jobs are actually changing, going beyond the Department of Labor's O*NET database, which covers roughly 900 occupations and more than 18,000 tasks but is updated only every few years by experts. The team instead interviews representative workers, a few hundred per occupation at most, using AI voice interviewers that have become good enough that many people prefer them to human interviewers, and repeats the process in regular waves. The interviews ask not only what people do but what they want help with and what worries them, and the results map back onto the existing taxonomy. One new task already surfacing among coders is verifying AI output, work that barely existed two years ago.

The second part is the technology of collaboration itself. Today's alignment optimizes for finishing the task in front of the model, but evidence already shows a cost: people using coding agents finish faster while their mastery of the material declines. The team wants post-training that optimizes for long-term skill acquisition as well, treating deskilling as an externality to be priced into the objective. That requires models that adapt to the microtasks of an individual worker's job with very little supervision, rather than big centralized training efforts aimed at whole job categories. Yang's GUM research, short for general user models, points the way: a system that locally and privately watches how someone works can infer their tasks and preferences and help proactively, sometimes before the person has articulated what they need. Hashimoto frames it as a shift from reactive AI, which waits for a well-formed prompt and puts the burden of clarity on the user, to proactive AI that knows enough about your work to act a few steps ahead.

The third part is proof: large randomized controlled trials with partner companies, testing whether augmentation-oriented AI actually leaves workers and organizations better off over time. The team's first concrete artifact is Upskilling Together, a month-long program with no human teachers, in which participants use AI however they like, guided by a designed curriculum and assessments, to train for two of the hottest new roles in the economy, AI engineer and forward deployed engineer. It currently targets recent graduates, with background checks for motivation, and it is deliberately two-sided: the team spent time asking hiring companies which skills are missing, and aims to fill exactly that gap. Yang's earlier work on generative interfaces shapes the teaching itself, generating an animated neural network to show how gradients flow through backpropagation rather than handing the learner a wall of equations. Rytting pushes them on the wildest version of success, and the answers stack up: millions of people reskilled through the platform, model providers measured on how well their systems teach and upskill rather than only on task completion, a rebuilt public infrastructure for understanding what jobs are, and, in Yang's answer, a community where anxious workers learn alongside each other rather than alone. Brynjolfsson is blunt that not everyone can do every job, and that this is fine: the point is to find and grow each person's comparative advantage, not to erase differences.

Why is none of this simply being built by the frontier labs? Partly, the team says, because education is a public good with a classic market failure: the learner, the employer, and society all benefit, so purely private provision undershoots. Partly because collaboration is a fuzzy target. Math and coding offer clean reward signals; nobody yet knows how to define a good evaluation for human and AI working together, or how to run reinforcement learning toward it, and human variability makes the design space enormous. And partly because the labs, for all their resources, are finite and are busy competing over enterprise coding and similar prizes. Academia has one structural advantage here: a university's dual mission of teaching and research forces daily engagement with questions a lab's pretraining team rarely asks, such as whether Claude Code is deskilling its own undergraduates. The human side is also just hard. Yang's lessons from the first eight months are that learner motivation has to be designed for, not assumed, and that research with human participants, from sampling to keeping people engaged for a month, is a discipline of its own that she hopes to distill into shared practice. Hashimoto adds that the layers keep going: even a perfect platform fails if employers will not hire from it.

The failure modes they name are honest ones. The deepest is betting on the wrong future: either AI becomes better than people at everything, which Hashimoto doubts but will not rule out, or the team reskills people toward a comparative advantage that has already moved. The more likely failure, Brynjolfsson says, is mundane execution, the same reason most businesses fail. On open source, where all three have form, Hashimoto's Alpaca among them, the lessons differ and the two computer scientists cheerfully disagree: Hashimoto credits serendipity and cultural demand no one can schedule, Yang argues for artifacts that are simple and useful, as with her SWE-smith data recipe and GUM, and Brynjolfsson insists both are being modest, since consistent quality is what actually improves the odds. As for why now, the technology for AI interviewers and generative teaching interfaces has only just become good enough, while the demand side has turned urgent: two weeks before the conversation, Brynjolfsson helped organize a statement signed by 16 Nobel laureates and a few hundred economists under the title "we must act now." A graduating Stanford student came to his office hours asking whether her generation is doomed. This project is their answer: she is not, but only if the tools, the measures, and the training get built on purpose.

Open questions

  1. The team wants models optimized for how much skill the user gains, not just whether the task gets done. Can something as fuzzy as good collaboration be turned into an evaluation and a training signal as reliable as a math answer key, or does its resistance to measurement keep augmentation permanently underfunded next to automation?
  2. Reskilling is a bet on where human comparative advantage will sit, placed while the frontier is moving. Can a workforce audit that runs in regular waves actually keep up with the capability curve, or will programs keep training people for the advantage they had last year?
  3. Upskilling Together starts with motivated recent graduates aiming at two roles employers are desperate to fill. Does the same design survive contact with mid-career workers whose occupations are shrinking rather than growing, and whose motivation cannot be screened for in advance?

Invest Like The Best · Ramtin Naimi on the hidden capital crunch behind the AI boom

The Hidden Capital Crunch Behind Silicon Valley’s AI Boom

On Invest Like The Best, the episode "The Hidden Capital Crunch Behind Silicon Valley’s AI Boom" is Patrick O'Shaughnessy's second conversation with Ramtin Naimi, founder and managing partner of Abstract Ventures. Naimi's firm sees as many early companies as almost anyone, and this conversation stays zoomed in on the present moment: how deals are actually getting done around San Francisco, why venture funds themselves are running short of cash, and what the AI compute buildout is doing to the price of everything.

The deal market, Naimi says, is moving faster than ever. A small set of firms gets preferential pricing in the first tranche of a hot round. The firms that lose no longer wait politely for the next round, as they did in 2021, when a fast follow might price at twice the earlier valuation a few weeks later. Now they ask what it costs to get into the same financing today, and the answer is usually three to five times the primary price, paid in a second tranche running alongside the first. He argues the tranching itself is not necessarily a sickness: these companies are so capital intensive that they genuinely need the money, and they do not want to raise it all in the primary. What does echo 2021 is the behavior around the edges. Offers go out very quickly and are then changed, pulled, or renegotiated, and in the last six to eight weeks he has seen more term sheets renegotiated after being offered than at any time since 2021.

His recurring question is whether this is 2019, early in a run, or 2021, at the top of one. The pace resembles 2021, but he thinks the substance is healthier, because the value creation is real: he uses these products himself, and family members who were never early adopters have folded them into daily life. The misleading part is the revenue underneath the growth. Companies growing ten to twenty times year over year all have something unusual in their numbers: very low or negative margins, heavy customer concentration, data revenue annualized by multiplying one month by twelve, or usage based rather than contractual revenue. Data labeling companies grow at insane rates on margins of 15 to 20 percent, because the data itself is expensive. Other companies sit on top of foundation models and watch the margin flow to the model providers, though margins are improving fast. He cites Anthropic at 85 percent gross margins in the first half of this year, and portfolio companies growing 200 to 700 percent while their margins swing from negative 15 percent to positive 35 percent. Vertical software companies are fetching 100 times revenue while public software trades near 13 times, a gap that only makes sense if these are really labor replacement businesses: law firms spend vastly more on paralegals than on software, so capturing a fifth of the labor budget changes the math entirely.

The comparison to 2021 cuts both ways. The 2021 companies, he says, were mostly uninteresting software businesses growing three to five times a year, carrying billion dollar valuations on eight million dollars of revenue with constrained markets. Public market investors are again rushing into venture, as they did late in 2021. But today's founders are finding product market fit faster than ever, and investors genuinely believe this is a window where sitting on the sidelines is not an option.

The episode's title points at the least visible squeeze, which sits one level up, with the funds themselves. A venture fund normally raises enough to deploy over 18 to 24 months. In the last two years the mega platform funds have raised two or three times inside that window, deploying roughly three times faster than planned. Every one of them, Naimi says, underestimated the capital required: the companies they want are more expensive than modeled, and holding their ownership takes far more money. Limited partners are left choosing between maintaining ownership with far fewer shots on goal, or keeping their number of bets while accepting much less ownership. The limited partners also expected far more liquidity by now. What looks like 30 to 50 percent of an institution's assets in venture is often really concentrated in three or four companies, SpaceX, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Databricks, and partners without exposure to those names have seen almost no cash back. He gives the pace in concrete terms: one fund raised 2.5 billion dollars, began deploying in July, and will be back in the market in January; another took 3 billion dollars nine months ago and is now raising 5 billion.

The crunch is now rippling into term sheets themselves. In the last eight to twelve weeks, firms have begun committing money they have not yet finished raising. One company was offered 150 million dollars with funding deferred to mid January, because the firm's current fund was fully committed and its new fund was still closing. Another received a 50 million dollar term sheet where the lead, taking 40 million of it, could wire only 5 million at closing and needed eight to ten weeks for the rest. The firms are good for the money, he stresses, but they are signing deals ahead of their own capital calls. Against that backdrop, he marvels at Anthropic's S-1: on his reading, the company has raised 140 billion dollars, spent 10 billion of it, and holds 132 billion on its balance sheet, reaching its revenue at 85 percent gross margins while burning remarkably little cash.

All of this spending ultimately funnels into compute, which prompts Patrick's question about whether an antiquated instrument is being used on a modern problem. Naimi points to partial answers: General Catalyst's customer value fund, which finances growth against proven returns, and firms that now invest compute instead of cash, which founders often prefer because the money would go to compute anyway and the allocation is guaranteed. Compute as currency is complicated, though: someone has to source it at good prices, and it is unclear how many times the same capacity can be recycled before it degrades. Founders still default to clean equity, and venture capitalists historically talk them out of anything resembling debt, so new structures spread slowly. Meanwhile the compute market itself is contradictory. Elon Musk talks about stranded capacity and the neoclouds have had a rough stretch in public markets, yet nobody can get an allocation of Nvidia's newest chips and GPU prices only move one way. The largest neoclouds will not engage on deals under 200 million dollars. The segment he finds most fascinating is the neolabs: where two or three were expected, there are now 80 or 90 that have raised between one and five billion dollars. One he met recently is raising a billion dollars to spend entirely on a single training run next year, and expects to raise five billion more during the year. Their results are hard to underwrite, since a dazzling launch may be a genuine breakthrough or a model tuned to shine on benchmarks. If capital is what eventually changes the compute story, he thinks the constraint binds on the debt side: these builders need five to ten times their equity in debt, and the neoclouds' public market troubles will raise that cost.

Seed pricing, where Naimi has spent his career, is being reset by moving goalposts. When he started in 2016, the best case for any company was a 10 billion dollar outcome, and deals priced off that. By 2021, Stripe, Databricks, Airbnb, and DoorDash pushed the imagined best case to 100 billion, and everything priced off that instead, though only a handful of companies ever got there. Now the lens is a trillion dollars, and a one billion dollar entry valuation looks reasonable against it. His own bet is that there may be two or three more trillion dollar companies, not forty. The losing pattern, he argues, is deploying most of a fund into the most obvious thing at market prices: that is what the bottom three quartiles of the 2021 vintage did with 100 times revenue software deals. The returns live in backing great founders on ideas that are not yet obvious. Reactive investing, picking well among what everyone sees, can make a top quartile manager; proactive investing, backing founders who are early to a market, is what makes a top five percent one.

He gives two examples. Conduit, a brain computer interface company Abstract seeded nine months ago, reads brain signals from a device worn on the head rather than implanted, and its model could already tell what a person was about to type with about 80 percent accuracy before they typed it. It has since gathered 30,000 hours of human training data, and in the last eight to ten weeks, as the category caught fire, it raised a large round with 13 term sheets. Etched's founder Gavin Uberti met every major firm and convinced them all he was brilliant, but most assumed a startup could not beat incumbents at AI chips, until Sequoia blessed the company with a 10 billion dollar valuation and the whole market repriced around it. His rough gauge of a deal's temperature is the term sheet count: fifteen sheets means a very expensive company, while two or three means reasonable pricing and room for a great investor to win a core position. Pricing overall has become erratic, with a fourfold spread between plausible low and high term sheets, because someone will pay three times the second highest bid rather than lose, and founders then anchor their expectations on a friend's outlier raise.

ARK Invest · Ara Kharazian on the cracks in the AI thesis

Ramp's Economist Found Cracks In The AI Thesis | The Brainstorm 156

On ARK Invest's The Brainstorm, episode 156, "Ramp's Economist Found Cracks In The AI Thesis", Ara Kharazian, the lead economist at Ramp, joins hosts Sam and Brett to present data drawn from more than 80,000 businesses and over $275 billion in annual business spend that flows across Ramp's platform. His latest research series argues that beneath the rapid headline growth in corporate AI spending, the market is far more concentrated, and more fragile, than the standard story suggests.

The run-up itself is not in dispute. Over the two years Ramp has tracked it, AI spend has grown faster than any other spend category in the data, and it has spread across providers: Anthropic is now the most popular AI model provider among businesses, and OpenAI and Anthropic together account for roughly 95 percent of the market, with xAI taking a small share and Meta only just entering enterprise. Kharazian's caution is about who is doing the spending. One percent of businesses drive 80 percent of all AI spend. The median firm that spends on AI spends about $15 per employee per month, roughly one chat subscription per employee plus a coding agent for someone, while a top 1 percent firm spends $7,000 to $10,000 per employee per month. The top 10 percent of firms are still growing their spend, and the median firm is creeping up, from about $12 per employee per month a month ago to $15 now.

The crack he points to is at the very top. The heaviest adopters appear to have started slowing their AI spend: September came in below July, and the earliest October data shows the slowdown continuing from the hyper-growth of the first half of the year. This is not because they are using AI less. Token volumes kept rising over the same months. The drivers are economic. The price of AI has fallen by almost half over the period, as OpenAI and Anthropic cut prices and ship more efficient models, large enterprises are negotiating contracts with new discounts, and capable models at the standard and light tiers are taking work that used to require frontier models. A firm can double its usage and still watch its bill flatten.

Brett pushes back hard. Three months, he argues, do not make a trend: the top 1 percent is still up four or five times year over year, and Ramp's sample skews toward tech-forward, venture-backed companies that were always going to over-index on AI. He also reads the pricing pressure strategically. Anthropic has just filed an amended S-1 ahead of going public and, on his account, is investing in pricing to drive token volume; both leading labs want longer-term contracts in place, because their credibility with the suppliers financing hundreds of billions of dollars in infrastructure depends on showing durable revenue growth to set against those liabilities. And the huge gap between the median firm and the top 1 percent, he argues, has to close through diffusion rather than the leaders stopping.

The sharpest exchange concerns revenue quality. Sam cites Chamath's estimate that 70 to 80 percent of revenue at AI companies comes from other AI companies funded by venture capital, and Kharazian agrees with the framing: not a circular revenue problem, but a revenue quality problem. The top 1 percent of AI spenders on Ramp are by and large venture-backed tech companies, which is sustainable while they keep growing and raising, but the concentration is also a correlation. If the AI trade slows, those firms will all slow their AI spend at the same time, and the impact could cascade across the sector. If a company cannot raise its next round, its AI spend stops.

Kharazian adds that maturing adopters learn to control the bill. Ramp itself is a profitable top 1 percent spender, yet it now blocks frontier models on automated tasks, sets cheaper defaults, and uses routers to send work to the right tier. Brett notes the same discipline from the supply side: OpenAI cut the price of one of its efficient models, Luna, by 80 percent to win marginal share, and when performance per dollar roughly halves every couple of months, a company can hold its activity constant and halve its cost.

The open question is what the other 99 percent will ever spend. Coding agents are, all three agree, the most commercially advanced form of AI today, and Kharazian doubts the median business will extract from them anything like the value a software engineer gets, so forecasts that assume the median firm climbs to top 1 percent intensity are assuming new product categories that do not exist yet. Brett counters that security could force broad spending, since cheaper attacks from open-weight models will oblige even non-tech firms to defend their patchwork infrastructure, and Sam floats the private equity angle: buy a median firm, spend like a top 10 percent firm, and outcompete the industry.

Asked for a five-year level, Kharazian puts the median firm at under $1,000 per employee per month, with AI use table stakes rather than a differentiator, and he resists even that precision, at one point wondering whether he means per year. Against predictions in his circles of tens of thousands per employee per month, he benchmarks against a computer purchase: significant, but not a second payroll. Brett does the arithmetic the other way: $1,000 a month across a billion knowledge workers is $12 trillion a year, so even the bear case is enormous. Kharazian's closing position is narrower than a collapse call. He does not expect AI spend to fall; he thinks the headwinds to its growth are stronger, and more neglected, than the tailwinds right now.

No Priors · Vlad Tenev on tokenization, agentic trading, and expanding ownership

Tokenization, Agentic Trading, Harmonic: Robinhood's Mission to Expand Ownership with CEO Vlad Tenev

On No Priors, host Sarah Guo sits down with Vlad Tenev, the co-founder and chief executive of Robinhood, for a conversation organized around a single word: ownership. Tenev says Robinhood is live in some form in more than 120 countries and has grown from a phone app for buying stocks at zero commission into a global financial conglomerate, and that everything it now builds, from tokenized stocks to accounts for newborns, is an attempt to let more people own more of the economy.

His case for ownership runs on two levels. For an individual, accumulating assets is the tried and true route to financial freedom, and the things a person owns become part of their identity. For a society, he argues, the stakes are higher: a society without broad ownership is inherently unstable, because people who own things have a stake in the system and become its defenders. That second argument does much of the work in the episode, since Tenev keeps returning to the backlash against AI and asking who actually gets to share in its gains.

Guo asks how a mathematician became a missionary for mass ownership, and Tenev reaches back to his childhood. He was born in Bulgaria in the late 1980s and immigrated to the United States in the 1990s, after a stretch in which he did not see his father for a year and a half. Bulgaria's inflation ran at roughly 170 percent in 1991 and, by 1997, at something like 2,000 percent annualized, and his grandparents kept their savings in copper cookware because pots held value better than the currency. Tenev notes that mathematics itself is changing character, converging with coding as researchers adopt tools like Lean and version control. His objection is to how the progress is organized: it is too centralized, he says, with a few AI companies using big data centers and secret models to do the work. He predicts that landmark results will be both more likely and more celebrated when they come from individuals using public tools, and warns that labs which hoard discovery risk losing their license to operate.

Tokenization gets the longest product discussion. The mechanism mirrors stablecoins: real assets sit custodied at a traditional institution while tokens minted against them trade freely, in fractional amounts, anywhere in the world. Robinhood launched stock tokens in Europe last year, then relaunched them this year in fully DeFi-enabled form alongside Robinhood Chain, its blockchain for real-world assets, which Tenev describes as among the fastest growing in history and first in developer activity. Developers are already composing with stock tokens as building blocks, pairing them with meme coins and NFTs, and Tenev says the deeper purpose is access: in countries without functioning capital markets, a phone and a non-custodial wallet could do for US equities what stablecoins did for dollars. Masters of Scale · Demis Hassabis on how AI could become dangerous

Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis: How AI could become dangerous (with Reid Hoffman) | Summit '26

On Masters of Scale, the episode "Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis: How AI could become dangerous (with Reid Hoffman) | Summit '26" is a live conversation recorded at the Masters of Scale Summit in October 2026, at the Presidio Theatre in San Francisco. Reid Hoffman, who has known Hassabis for more than a decade and once served on a Google AI ethics board, interviews the DeepMind co-founder about governing frontier AI, why progress in recursive self-improvement worries him, and why he thinks the field should build intelligent tools rather than conscious machines.

Hassabis begins with the reason he works on AI at all. His whole career, he says, has been aimed at using AI as the ultimate tool for advancing science, medicine, and our understanding of the world, and the first use he would choose is improving human health. What better demonstration of what these tools are for, he asks, than helping to cure terrible diseases, and he points to his own projects in that direction: AlphaFold, and the spinout drug discovery company Isomorphic Labs.

The AlphaFold story carries that argument. Proteins are the building blocks of life, and predicting a protein's structure from its amino acid sequence was a 50-year grand challenge, sometimes described to him as Fermat's last theorem for biology. Knowing a protein's 3D surface is critical both for understanding biological mechanisms and for designing a drug that binds to the right part of it. AlphaFold 2, finished back in 2020, accelerated that step by orders of magnitude, and Hassabis says it remains perhaps the best concrete example of AI cracking a scientific problem thought to be uncrackable: around 4 million researchers now use it, and almost every future drug will have made use of its structures. But structure alone is not a drug, so the work is expanding into biochemistry, predicting properties such as toxicity and ADME behavior that decide whether a compound is safe and effective.

The conversation then turns to recursive self-improvement, and Hassabis gives his theory of the game. RSI works best in verifiable domains, he says: math, coding, and originally games, where an answer can be checked as correct, code can be run, or a win is a win. AlphaZero was one of the first famous examples, learning purely by playing against itself, a million games at a time, then training a slightly better successor. As a chess player he felt it viscerally: starting from random play in the morning, it reached the point where he could still just about beat it at coffee time, was grandmaster level by lunch, and by tea time was better than any chess entity, human or machine. Verifiable domains also let you generate almost unlimited synthetic data, because each new problem or move can be checked for correctness.

His worry is about the next phase. Today's RSI keeps a human firmly in the loop: AI tools make an engineer perhaps three to five times more productive, but humans still review the code and architect the systems. The dangerous step is closed-loop RSI, recursive self-improvement with no human reviewer in the process. It would be faster, but Hassabis calls it a very dangerous way to reach full AGI, for two reasons. Control becomes hard to keep over a process that improves itself, the old intelligence explosion concern. And the end result would likely be an alien intelligence, with no reason to sit in a mind space similar to ours, which would make it hard for such a system to understand us, or us it. He urges real caution as labs get closer to that closed loop, while acknowledging the intense competitive race pushing the other way.

Asked how labs should navigate that race, Hassabis starts with defense. Cyber defense has to accelerate faster than cyber attack, with trusted defenders given early access to model versions tuned for defense. Sandboxes need to be far more hardened, and monitoring far better: many of the cyber incidents of the last six months, he says, could have been addressed with more robust engineering, and agents should never be able to run for weeks without anyone noticing. Eventually he wants formal guarantees and bounds on what these systems can do, and he is encouraging top mathematicians he knows to turn their skills toward complexity theory and game theory, because the field will soon have to keep track of swarms of agents potentially collaborating with each other.

On governance, he points to an essay he published over the summer proposing an industry-run standards body, an SRO, with some government oversight, US-led at first and ideally international later. The problem, he concedes, is that international institutions are very weak just when they need to be strongest, which Hoffman translates out of British understatement for the audience. The essay did land in the White House and among peer labs, and some of its energy fed into a joint accord the major players signed: better than nothing, Hassabis says, since it at least acknowledges the problems, but it says little about what to do, and that has to come next. The shared ground he sees includes third-party auditors and evaluators, an ecosystem like the audit industry in finance, with firms such as Accenture already building expertise, and the leading labs red-teaming each other's systems before general release.

The deepest disagreement comes over consciousness. Prompted by claims that a chatbot saying it is conscious settles the matter, Hassabis notes there is still no agreed definition of consciousness, and behavior alone is weak evidence: with another human, we also share the same substrate, the same kind of brain, which makes it parsimonious to assume they feel what we feel, and that second leg is missing for an AI. His position is that we should be building intelligent tools today, not conscious entities, for two reasons. The first is technical. There is a direct trade-off between self-volition, a system acting on its own internal judgements, and corrigibility, its willingness to follow instructions and be switched off or redirected. Train a system to reject commands based on its own workings and you are exposed whenever your constitution or rules are wrong, which is exactly what Asimov's stories about his three laws kept warning. Today's systems are more malleable than many expected fifteen or twenty years ago, which is genuinely good news for steerability, but Hassabis insists the misalignment problem is not solved, and combining self-volition with recursive self-improvement would be doubly dangerous.

The second reason is societal. Society has not yet had the debate, with philosophers, theologians, artists, and the humanities, and across many cultures, about whether to cross what he calls the second Rubicon: creating entities whose moral standing we would then have to reckon with. His proposal is to cross the first Rubicon alone, build the intelligent tools, use them to do the neuroscience that finally identifies the neural correlates of consciousness, which might take five to ten years, and only then hold the discussion about whether to cross the second. He also rejects the claim that great intelligence requires consciousness: the two are double dissociable, he argues, since today's systems are smart without, in his view, being conscious at all, while his dog is conscious, dreams, and is not that smart, and the same holds for dolphins, monkeys, and elephants.

Finally, on AI sovereignty, Hassabis expects the frontier to stay crowded: six to eight offerings, including Chinese companies, leapfrogging each other with leaders perhaps three to six months ahead, and no one better on every vector at once. Enterprises can arbitrage between them. Open source is maybe six months to a year behind, and carries the still unsolved problem of restricting bad actors while enabling scientists and other good actors, though he says the West needs open source solutions of its own. Companies and countries with sensitive data will fine-tune privately on it, but he would not recommend every country try to build a frontier model. The better play is to build value on top of these near-commodity tools, in sectors like healthcare, and to collaborate across borders, which is where Hoffman closes the session: agency in the AI age is collective, something countries have to take together, for the benefit of humanity.

Open questions

  1. If competitive pressure makes closed-loop recursive self-improvement hard to refuse, can monitoring, sandboxes, and formal bounds actually keep a human meaningfully in control of a process that improves itself faster than people can review it, or does oversight become nominal at that speed?
  2. Hassabis's governance plan depends on credible third-party auditors and an industry standards body becoming international while international institutions are at their weakest. Who staffs and funds those evaluators so they stay independent of the labs they are meant to check?
  3. If intelligence and consciousness really are dissociable, what evidence could ever justify crossing the second Rubicon, and who gets a say in deciding that society is ready, given the debate he calls for has barely begun?

It began in 2009 with ecosystem investments such as DocuSign, nCino, and Twilio, plus services partners; it moved into Snowflake, across several rounds and the IPO, and into cybersecurity with Wiz; then, when AI became the industry's next wave, it created a billion-dollar AI fund in early 2023 and invested in Anthropic across six rounds, alongside Mistral, Cohere, ElevenLabs, and Runway. What the portfolio gets in return, he says, is access to the broadest set of enterprise customers in the industry, plus operating advice. That vantage point makes him blunt about the current market. He professes bafflement at the wave of AI-native CRM startups the host names, Lightfield and Monaco among them; the only opening he concedes is far down market, and it is small. On valuations he goes further, calling parts of the market irresponsible: above ten billion dollars the buyer universe shrinks to almost nothing, fewer than one percent of companies ever go public, and a reckoning is inevitable for companies whose valuations they cannot grow into. Generational companies exist, and Anthropic's founders were plainly special from the first meeting, but they are rare, and founders who simply chase the highest valuation are usually making the wrong choice for themselves and their employees.

What kills deals, he says, is usually found in diligence rather than at the eleventh hour. Companies arrive without strong controls over their financials or their customer contracts, and a founder culture fixated on ARR masks the number he cares about, customer attrition, which betrays unhappy customers and product problems. Security is the one function among the fourteen in Salesforce's diligence process that can kill a deal unilaterally. In one process, Salesforce's penetration testers reached not just the target's full source code but its chief executive's laptop and employee information; the findings were shared back, as they always are, but the damage was unrecoverable. A company that is buttoned up, data accessible and stack red-teamed in advance, moves through diligence quickly and painlessly.

Anthropic just built it...

Wes Roth, in "Anthropic just built it..." on his YouTube channel, walks through a coordinated set of disclosures from Anthropic and the research organization METR, and argues that the two together point to something Anthropic is deliberately not saying out loud.

The backdrop is Anthropic's planned IPO. A few days ago the company amended its S-1 filing to say that recursive self-improvement is imminent and that it is about to meet a threshold in its Responsible Scaling Policy. That threshold is a doubling of the pace of AI research and development attributable to AI itself, a tripwire Anthropic set for itself, and Roth stresses that this is acceleration from algorithms, not from buying more training compute.

METR, meanwhile, published the results of an eleven week embedded audit of how much Anthropic's own models are speeding up Anthropic's research. Its central estimate puts the current acceleration at about 1.9 times the historical rate, just under the 2x tripwire, though the honest range runs from about 1.3x to as high as 3x. One supporting datapoint: AI went from leading about 1 percent of Anthropic's research projects in March to about 26 percent in August.

What fuels Roth's suspicion is how much of METR's evidence Anthropic redacted. He compares reading the report to watching a prisoner of war blink Morse code: the most informative findings are blacked out, and Anthropic also cancelled the compute allocation part of the audit midway, after originally agreeing to let METR verify how its compute is divided. Co-founder Sam McCandlish defended the redactions, saying that sharing exactly what is driving the speedup would hand competitors a roadmap.

The second disclosure concerns models the public has never seen. Beyond Fable, the strongest model anyone can use, and Mythos, released only to trusted organizations, Anthropic has internal models it refers to as Model 2, Model 3, and Model 4, which finished training in July, in August to September, and in September to October of this year. On Anthropic's internal AECI benchmark suite, scored in log odds so results do not saturate near 100 percent, Model 4 scored above 50 percent on every benchmark, a line no earlier model had crossed. Opus 4.6's lowest score in January was about 10 percent, the Mythos preview reached 20 percent in April, and Mythos 5 reached 35 percent.

Roth then walks through the trend line. Earlier Anthropic system cards suggested the jump at Mythos was a one-time step up, followed by a return to the previous slope. The newer models, he says, make that story hard to sustain: the curve looks steeper, more like a lasting change in trajectory than a bump. METR itself says the AECI trend shows no clear evidence of hyperbolic growth, while noting that other evidence pointing that way sits behind the redactions.

The researcher interviews inside the report describe where the speed is coming from. Most Anthropic researchers METR surveyed said the acceleration in their own area leans to or past 2x, reporting that they are no longer bottlenecked on code and that the time from idea to experiment has fallen by as much as 80 percent, because experiments that once took a day or two to implement are now nearly free to run. As of August, Anthropic counted roughly 30,000 AI agents doing research and engineering work inside the company.

Roth's own theory, which he flags as speculation to take with a grain of salt, is that the redactions all cluster around one thing: an unnamed agent scaffold, distinct from Claude Code, which together with Mythos lets researchers fire off many ideas at once and keep what works, turning agent effort into algorithmic improvements. Naming the scaffold, he argues, would give the technique away, and so would publishing how Anthropic's compute split shifted over recent months.

He closes with a cultural oddity, that Claude helped collect data for, write, and analyze much of the METR report itself, and with a prediction: if Anthropic really has built this engine, expect an open source version of the same idea from a Chinese lab within months.

Open questions

  1. What is the redacted agent scaffold, and how much of the measured speedup comes from it rather than from the models alone?
  2. The acceleration estimate of about 1.9x sits inside an uncertainty band that already includes Anthropic's 2x Responsible Scaling Policy threshold. If a future audit puts the number clearly past 2x, what concretely changes?
  3. Anthropic cancelled the compute allocation audit after agreeing to it. Without that verification, how can outsiders tell a genuine algorithmic breakthrough apart from a quiet shift in how compute is being spent?
