WSJ Events · Dina Powell McCormick at WSJ Tech Live

Watch Live: Meta President Dina Powell McCormick at WSJ Tech | WSJ

On WSJ Events, the session "Watch Live: Meta President Dina Powell McCormick at WSJ Tech | WSJ" pairs Meta's president with a Wall Street Journal moderator at WSJ Tech Live, on the day after an election in which data centers had become a live campaign issue. The session opens with a video Meta submitted, filmed around its project in Richland Parish, Louisiana: a catering business that grew from serving 40 people to 400 a week because Meta arrived, teachers whose pay is topped up by bonuses funded from the local tax surplus, and residents describing neighbors moving back home.

The moderator's opening question sets the stakes: were data centers on the ballot last night? Powell McCormick answers that they plainly were, in campaign ads and in candidates' platforms, and that the moderator's count put 33 of 40 ballot measures restricting data centers on the winning side. Her framing is that the data center is simply the visible form of AI, the thing a community can point to and refuse, while the deeper question is how people feel about AI itself. Meta's stated rule is to build only where a community judges the investment will make a difference.

She describes the method behind that rule as a community compact, built from the Louisiana experience and now applied everywhere. Its elements, as she walks through them, are electricity, teachers, local labor, and bipartisanship, with local citizens treated as the decision makers who matter most.

On electricity, she says Meta works with Entergy in Louisiana, pays for its own power, and is structured so that residents' costs fall rather than rise, by some two billion dollars over the life of the investment, in her telling. On teachers, the parish routes any tax surplus to teacher bonuses, an arrangement she credits a Journal story with bringing to Meta's attention, and the company now looks for ways to invest in teachers and first responders in every host community. On labor, the commitment is to train local workers rather than import them. On politics, she points to a New Orleans expansion event held weeks earlier where Republican Governor Landry appeared alongside former mayor Mitch Landrieu and former senator Mary Landrieu, and reads it as proof that host communities can put an investment above party.

The scale she describes is large. Meta has been building data centers for twenty years, she says, long before the current boom made them controversial. The company operates in 23 states, five of them with projects of one gigawatt or larger, Texas among them. She also reports that a governor who won the previous night after making data centers a big part of his platform had already asked to meet once the inauguration is past, saying his state does not want to lose the investment or the jobs.

Asked whether the buildout will now stall, she concedes the likely near term: much more review, and much more attention to whether constituents actually want these projects. That, she argues, is making compute an even scarcer asset, one the market has not fully priced. Starting a brand new project in the United States is very challenging, while expanding an existing site is easier because the community has already felt benefits rather than costs. Every hyperscaler, she says, is now asking whether some building should happen outside the United States, and she notes that Singapore and Australia both saw something close to moratoriums in the past week. The future may be international, she says, and it may even be in space, but the immediate future is harder building.

On regulation, her line is that AI oversight is neither a Republican nor a Democratic project. She describes meeting, with Mark Zuckerberg, the House Democratic AI commission led by Josh Gottheimer, and the recent White House superintelligence safety accords agreed with other lab leaders, naming Jensen Huang, Greg Brockman, and Sam Altman. The accords, she argues, have teeth that went underreported. Inside Meta's superintelligence lab, more than 200 people work only on safety, reviewing how models are developed. Researchers who see a problem will be able to go straight to an independent board committee rather than through their managers. An independent auditor will oversee the process. And the standard has already bitten: Muse, Meta's consumer AI product, was delayed by more than eight weeks while the team kept fixing bugs, because the release threshold was as safe as possible.

Then the conversation turns to money. The moderator puts Meta's future spending commitments at 825 billion dollars, and Powell McCormick demurs, saying it is not quite that much. What she describes instead is machinery: a dedicated compute division working daily with chief financial officer Susan Li on a puzzle that joins financing, power, workforce, and community support. BlackRock and Larry Fink are the capital partners on the El Paso project. Entergy supplies power in Louisiana, and NextEra Energy under John Ketchum is the other power partner she names. On labor, Meta works with Sean McGarvey of the building trades and with the IBEW electricians, against an industry backdrop she sizes at ten trillion dollars of capital, 250 gigawatts of power, and two million jobs needed over the next four years, with a shortage of about 350,000 electricians today.

The market evidence she offers is reception, not price. Meta's recent equity and debt offerings were both oversubscribed, and its European bond offering closed that day heavily oversubscribed even with 18 other bond deals in the market. The story she says investors are buying is a core business with 3.6 billion people using the products daily, 200 million small businesses among them, six consecutive quarters of double digit growth, and capital spending that is visibly improving that core business. She quotes Blackstone's John Gray calling compute itself a financeable asset, and notes that Meta already gets daily calls from companies wanting to lease its compute, which she frames as a path to monetization whether or not its own products use all of it.

Asked for a plan B if the bets fail, she reaches for the company's track record rather than a hedge. She has been at Meta two years, counting board service, and she lists WhatsApp's growth, Threads passing X in monthly users, and Reels, built only five years ago, as evidence that this founder builds products first and finds the monetization after, with distribution to nearly half the world's population as the multiplier.

Her account of Meta's edge in the compute race is partly personal: she spends 80 percent of her time in Menlo Park on compute. The product evidence she cites is Muse, which she calls the number one downloaded app of the day, built on an underlying model she names as Wonder, and already at ten million users, many of them people who had never used AI before. Polling from the election, she adds, found that people who use AI view it much more favorably, which is the political version of the same point.

Muse is free for now, and the monetization path she sketches runs through small business. Only 20 percent of American small businesses use any form of AI, she says, and a Ramp study found that the adopters grew market share and revenue dramatically. In focus groups those owners said they would pay. Commerce is already flowing: the average product sold through Muse goes for about 200 dollars, to the satisfaction of connector partners she names as Plaid, DoorDash, and Stripe.

Her own use of the product is domestic as much as executive. Muse reads the news outlets and newsletters she follows each morning, writes her a detailed summary, and turns it into a thirty minute podcast. She uses it to arrange travel for her grown daughters. Friends who run small businesses, she says, use it like a chief operating officer. She also describes the glasses feeding the same system: at Bennett Orchards, a third generation grower named Henry walks the trees in Meta glasses, films the crop, and runs the footage through Muse to check inventory, spot problems, and see what the week's weather will do to the harvest.

On Washington, she argues the industry's job is to work with whichever government voters elect and to keep the work bipartisan, naming administration officials Michael Kratsios and Emil Michael among those engaged on the same questions. The workforce piece of that pitch is a program called America's Workforce Academy, which pays people to train as welders, fiber technicians, and electricians and guarantees them a job on a Meta infrastructure site. Meta deliberately left its own name off the program. She compares the moment to the country physically building the arsenal that won the Second World War, and says Zuckerberg is long on humans: the stated creed is that the future is for everyone, and that access has to be broad and fair for that to mean anything.

She closes on the most misunderstood thing about Zuckerberg. When she asked him, while discussing joining the board, why Meta would win, he answered that there would not be one winner, but that Meta would be one of the winners. He has physically moved his desk into the superintelligence lab to build alongside the researchers. And when Muse's design began with Jolly, a fuzzy creature, and the team produced a hundred alternatives, he told them to stick with their gut: the first one was really good. Her summary is founder risk taking joined to twenty years of stability, the adult in the room, as she jokes, who is not yet fifty.

Open questions

  1. If restrictive ballot measures keep passing and greenfield projects stall, does the industry's center of gravity actually shift abroad or into expansions of existing sites, and what happens to the American claim to AI leadership if it does?
  2. The safety accords rely on the labs policing themselves, with internal safety teams, a board committee, and an independent auditor. Once Congress writes federal guardrails, does that private machinery survive, merge with public oversight, or quietly become redundant?
  3. Muse is free while Meta's spending commitments climb toward the better part of a trillion dollars. At what point does consumer adoption have to turn into revenue for the compute bet to pay off, and is small business commerce really the path that gets there?

Laude Institute · Brynjolfsson, Yang and Hashimoto on reskilling with AI

Erik Brynjolfsson, Diyi Yang and Tatsunori Hashimoto on reskilling with AI | Moonshots // ONE

On Laude Institute, the episode "Erik Brynjolfsson, Diyi Yang and Tatsunori Hashimoto on reskilling with AI | Moonshots // ONE" is a long conversation about a Stanford research project called Collaborative Intelligence, hosted by Laude Institute co-founder Chris Rytting. The project is one of the Moonshots // ONE entries: it received a seed grant, spent six months developing a full lab proposal, and was one of four winning teams to receive a 10 million dollar lab. Its premise runs against the usual framing of AI at work. Instead of asking which jobs AI can take over, the team asks how AI can help people learn new skills and do new kinds of work alongside it.

The conversation opens with two competing visions that are more than sixty years old. Alan Turing asked whether a machine can think, and much of the field has chased that question ever since, building machines to be as capable as possible. Doug Engelbart asked a different question: whether a machine can help humans think better. The project belongs to the second tradition, and the team frames its work as a choice between automation, where machines substitute for what people do, and augmentation, where machines complement people and let them do things that were never possible before.

Brynjolfsson, a labor economist, makes the economic case for augmentation. Most value in history, he argues, has come not from replacing existing work but from doing entirely new things: a world that had only mechanized its old crafts would be full of clay pots and chariots, not jet engines, smartphones, and mRNA vaccines. The space of things people do not yet do is far larger than the space of things they currently do, so augmentation offers the bigger prize. It also divides the prize differently. Technology that substitutes for workers tends to push wages down and concentrate wealth, while technology that complements workers tends to raise wages, which is how most past technologies have behaved. He tells of a chief financial officer who planned to judge AI purely by headcount reduction in each department, and of his reply that a finance chief should be inventing better measures: customer satisfaction, new products, new services. What gets measured, the team argues, steers what gets built.

Hashimoto adds that the impact will be uneven because AI capability is spiky rather than a single rising tide. A calculator has outcalculated everyone for decades, yet machines remain far behind humans on many other dimensions, and that unevenness is a source of value, since differences between people and machines, and among people themselves, are what create comparative advantage. Whether AI touches your core work or only auxiliary tasks depends on the job. Terence Tao, the mathematician, uses AI for prettier figures and side tasks, not for the work that makes him a mathematician. For someone whose core task is writing boilerplate code, the change is not distant at all. The social problem, as Hashimoto puts it, is to find the people whose core work is being hit and move them toward work where they genuinely complement the models.

The early evidence is already visible if you look below the aggregate numbers. Headline unemployment has barely moved, but in the fifth of occupations most exposed to AI, employment for young workers has fallen by about 16 percent in relative terms, a pattern the team calls canaries in the coal mine. Brynjolfsson invokes the J-curve: with a powerful technology, productivity first dips while organizations reorganize processes and reskill people, and only then rises. That lag, measured in years rather than decades this time, is a window in which preparation is still possible.

Yang insists that workers themselves belong in the design loop, because the debate is not only about what technology can do but about what people want. Her earlier work uses a human agency scale from H1 to H5, running from full automation of a task to full human control, with an equal partnership in the middle. Scanning the workforce with it produced a surprise: for some core tasks, workers actually want automation, while for some auxiliary tasks they want to keep control. The project's plan has three parts built on that foundation. The first is measurement: an infrastructure to audit how jobs are actually changing, going beyond the Department of Labor's O*NET database, which covers roughly 900 occupations and more than 18,000 tasks but is updated only every few years by experts. The team instead interviews representative workers, a few hundred per occupation at most, using AI voice interviewers that have become good enough that many people prefer them to human interviewers, and repeats the process in regular waves. The interviews ask not only what people do but what they want help with and what worries them, and the results map back onto the existing taxonomy. One new task already surfacing among coders is verifying AI output, work that barely existed two years ago.

The second part is the technology of collaboration itself. Today's alignment optimizes for finishing the task in front of the model, but evidence already shows a cost: people using coding agents finish faster while their mastery of the material declines. The team wants post-training that optimizes for long-term skill acquisition as well, treating deskilling as an externality to be priced into the objective. That requires models that adapt to the microtasks of an individual worker's job with very little supervision, rather than big centralized training efforts aimed at whole job categories. Yang's GUM research, short for general user models, points the way: a system that locally and privately watches how someone works can infer their tasks and preferences and help proactively, sometimes before the person has articulated what they need. Hashimoto frames it as a shift from reactive AI, which waits for a well-formed prompt and puts the burden of clarity on the user, to proactive AI that knows enough about your work to act a few steps ahead.

The third part is proof: large randomized controlled trials with partner companies, testing whether augmentation-oriented AI actually leaves workers and organizations better off over time. The team's first concrete artifact is Upskilling Together, a month-long program with no human teachers, in which participants use AI however they like, guided by a designed curriculum and assessments, to train for two of the hottest new roles in the economy, AI engineer and forward deployed engineer. It currently targets recent graduates, with background checks for motivation, and it is deliberately two-sided: the team spent time asking hiring companies which skills are missing, and aims to fill exactly that gap. Yang's earlier work on generative interfaces shapes the teaching itself, generating an animated neural network to show how gradients flow through backpropagation rather than handing the learner a wall of equations. Rytting pushes them on the wildest version of success, and the answers stack up: millions of people reskilled through the platform, model providers measured on how well their systems teach and upskill rather than only on task completion, a rebuilt public infrastructure for understanding what jobs are, and, in Yang's answer, a community where anxious workers learn alongside each other rather than alone. Brynjolfsson is blunt that not everyone can do every job, and that this is fine: the point is to find and grow each person's comparative advantage, not to erase differences.

Why is none of this simply being built by the frontier labs? Partly, the team says, because education is a public good with a classic market failure: the learner, the employer, and society all benefit, so purely private provision undershoots. Partly because collaboration is a fuzzy target. Math and coding offer clean reward signals; nobody yet knows how to define a good evaluation for human and AI working together, or how to run reinforcement learning toward it, and human variability makes the design space enormous. And partly because the labs, for all their resources, are finite and are busy competing over enterprise coding and similar prizes. Academia has one structural advantage here: a university's dual mission of teaching and research forces daily engagement with questions a lab's pretraining team rarely asks, such as whether Claude Code is deskilling its own undergraduates. The human side is also just hard. Yang's lessons from the first eight months are that learner motivation has to be designed for, not assumed, and that research with human participants, from sampling to keeping people engaged for a month, is a discipline of its own that she hopes to distill into shared practice. Hashimoto adds that the layers keep going: even a perfect platform fails if employers will not hire from it.

The failure modes they name are honest ones. The deepest is betting on the wrong future: either AI becomes better than people at everything, which Hashimoto doubts but will not rule out, or the team reskills people toward a comparative advantage that has already moved. The more likely failure, Brynjolfsson says, is mundane execution, the same reason most businesses fail. On open source, where all three have form, Hashimoto's Alpaca among them, the lessons differ and the two computer scientists cheerfully disagree: Hashimoto credits serendipity and cultural demand no one can schedule, Yang argues for artifacts that are simple and useful, as with her SWE-smith data recipe and GUM, and Brynjolfsson insists both are being modest, since consistent quality is what actually improves the odds. As for why now, the technology for AI interviewers and generative teaching interfaces has only just become good enough, while the demand side has turned urgent: two weeks before the conversation, Brynjolfsson helped organize a statement signed by 16 Nobel laureates and a few hundred economists under the title "we must act now." A graduating Stanford student came to his office hours asking whether her generation is doomed. This project is their answer: she is not, but only if the tools, the measures, and the training get built on purpose.

Open questions

  1. The team wants models optimized for how much skill the user gains, not just whether the task gets done. Can something as fuzzy as good collaboration be turned into an evaluation and a training signal as reliable as a math answer key, or does its resistance to measurement keep augmentation permanently underfunded next to automation?
  2. Reskilling is a bet on where human comparative advantage will sit, placed while the frontier is moving. Can a workforce audit that runs in regular waves actually keep up with the capability curve, or will programs keep training people for the advantage they had last year?
  3. Upskilling Together starts with motivated recent graduates aiming at two roles employers are desperate to fill. Does the same design survive contact with mid-career workers whose occupations are shrinking rather than growing, and whose motivation cannot be screened for in advance?

Invest Like The Best · Ramtin Naimi on the hidden capital crunch behind the AI boom

The Hidden Capital Crunch Behind Silicon Valley’s AI Boom

On Invest Like The Best, the episode "The Hidden Capital Crunch Behind Silicon Valley’s AI Boom" is Patrick O'Shaughnessy's second conversation with Ramtin Naimi, founder and managing partner of Abstract Ventures. Naimi's firm sees as many early companies as almost anyone, and this conversation stays zoomed in on the present moment: how deals are actually getting done around San Francisco, why venture funds themselves are running short of cash, and what the AI compute buildout is doing to the price of everything.

The deal market, Naimi says, is moving faster than ever. A small set of firms gets preferential pricing in the first tranche of a hot round. The firms that lose no longer wait politely for the next round, as they did in 2021, when a fast follow might price at twice the earlier valuation a few weeks later. Now they ask what it costs to get into the same financing today, and the answer is usually three to five times the primary price, paid in a second tranche running alongside the first. He argues the tranching itself is not necessarily a sickness: these companies are so capital intensive that they genuinely need the money, and they do not want to raise it all in the primary. What does echo 2021 is the behavior around the edges. Offers go out very quickly and are then changed, pulled, or renegotiated, and in the last six to eight weeks he has seen more term sheets renegotiated after being offered than at any time since 2021.

His recurring question is whether this is 2019, early in a run, or 2021, at the top of one. The pace resembles 2021, but he thinks the substance is healthier, because the value creation is real: he uses these products himself, and family members who were never early adopters have folded them into daily life. The misleading part is the revenue underneath the growth. Companies growing ten to twenty times year over year all have something unusual in their numbers: very low or negative margins, heavy customer concentration, data revenue annualized by multiplying one month by twelve, or usage based rather than contractual revenue. Data labeling companies grow at insane rates on margins of 15 to 20 percent, because the data itself is expensive. Other companies sit on top of foundation models and watch the margin flow to the model providers, though margins are improving fast. He cites Anthropic at 85 percent gross margins in the first half of this year, and portfolio companies growing 200 to 700 percent while their margins swing from negative 15 percent to positive 35 percent. Vertical software companies are fetching 100 times revenue while public software trades near 13 times, a gap that only makes sense if these are really labor replacement businesses: law firms spend vastly more on paralegals than on software, so capturing a fifth of the labor budget changes the math entirely.

The comparison to 2021 cuts both ways. The 2021 companies, he says, were mostly uninteresting software businesses growing three to five times a year, carrying billion dollar valuations on eight million dollars of revenue with constrained markets. Public market investors are again rushing into venture, as they did late in 2021. But today's founders are finding product market fit faster than ever, and investors genuinely believe this is a window where sitting on the sidelines is not an option.

The episode's title points at the least visible squeeze, which sits one level up, with the funds themselves. A venture fund normally raises enough to deploy over 18 to 24 months. In the last two years the mega platform funds have raised two or three times inside that window, deploying roughly three times faster than planned. Every one of them, Naimi says, underestimated the capital required: the companies they want are more expensive than modeled, and holding their ownership takes far more money. Limited partners are left choosing between maintaining ownership with far fewer shots on goal, or keeping their number of bets while accepting much less ownership. The limited partners also expected far more liquidity by now. What looks like 30 to 50 percent of an institution's assets in venture is often really concentrated in three or four companies, SpaceX, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Databricks, and partners without exposure to those names have seen almost no cash back. He gives the pace in concrete terms: one fund raised 2.5 billion dollars, began deploying in July, and will be back in the market in January; another took 3 billion dollars nine months ago and is now raising 5 billion.

The crunch is now rippling into term sheets themselves. In the last eight to twelve weeks, firms have begun committing money they have not yet finished raising. One company was offered 150 million dollars with funding deferred to mid January, because the firm's current fund was fully committed and its new fund was still closing. Another received a 50 million dollar term sheet where the lead, taking 40 million of it, could wire only 5 million at closing and needed eight to ten weeks for the rest. The firms are good for the money, he stresses, but they are signing deals ahead of their own capital calls. Against that backdrop, he marvels at Anthropic's S-1: on his reading, the company has raised 140 billion dollars, spent 10 billion of it, and holds 132 billion on its balance sheet, reaching its revenue at 85 percent gross margins while burning remarkably little cash.

All of this spending ultimately funnels into compute, which prompts Patrick's question about whether an antiquated instrument is being used on a modern problem. Naimi points to partial answers: General Catalyst's customer value fund, which finances growth against proven returns, and firms that now invest compute instead of cash, which founders often prefer because the money would go to compute anyway and the allocation is guaranteed. Compute as currency is complicated, though: someone has to source it at good prices, and it is unclear how many times the same capacity can be recycled before it degrades. Founders still default to clean equity, and venture capitalists historically talk them out of anything resembling debt, so new structures spread slowly. Meanwhile the compute market itself is contradictory. Elon Musk talks about stranded capacity and the neoclouds have had a rough stretch in public markets, yet nobody can get an allocation of Nvidia's newest chips and GPU prices only move one way. The largest neoclouds will not engage on deals under 200 million dollars. The segment he finds most fascinating is the neolabs: where two or three were expected, there are now 80 or 90 that have raised between one and five billion dollars. One he met recently is raising a billion dollars to spend entirely on a single training run next year, and expects to raise five billion more during the year. Their results are hard to underwrite, since a dazzling launch may be a genuine breakthrough or a model tuned to shine on benchmarks. If capital is what eventually changes the compute story, he thinks the constraint binds on the debt side: these builders need five to ten times their equity in debt, and the neoclouds' public market troubles will raise that cost.

Seed pricing, where Naimi has spent his career, is being reset by moving goalposts. When he started in 2016, the best case for any company was a 10 billion dollar outcome, and deals priced off that. By 2021, Stripe, Databricks, Airbnb, and DoorDash pushed the imagined best case to 100 billion, and everything priced off that instead, though only a handful of companies ever got there. Now the lens is a trillion dollars, and a one billion dollar entry valuation looks reasonable against it. His own bet is that there may be two or three more trillion dollar companies, not forty. The losing pattern, he argues, is deploying most of a fund into the most obvious thing at market prices: that is what the bottom three quartiles of the 2021 vintage did with 100 times revenue software deals. The returns live in backing great founders on ideas that are not yet obvious. Reactive investing, picking well among what everyone sees, can make a top quartile manager; proactive investing, backing founders who are early to a market, is what makes a top five percent one.

He gives two examples. Conduit, a brain computer interface company Abstract seeded nine months ago, reads brain signals from a device worn on the head rather than implanted, and its model could already tell what a person was about to type with about 80 percent accuracy before they typed it. It has since gathered 30,000 hours of human training data, and in the last eight to ten weeks, as the category caught fire, it raised a large round with 13 term sheets. Etched's founder Gavin Uberti met every major firm and convinced them all he was brilliant, but most assumed a startup could not beat incumbents at AI chips, until Sequoia blessed the company with a 10 billion dollar valuation and the whole market repriced around it. His rough gauge of a deal's temperature is the term sheet count: fifteen sheets means a very expensive company, while two or three means reasonable pricing and room for a great investor to win a core position. Pricing overall has become erratic, with a fourfold spread between plausible low and high term sheets, because someone will pay three times the second highest bid rather than lose, and founders then anchor their expectations on a friend's outlier raise.

Patrick pushes the opposite strategy: why not simply buy the obviously great companies, like Databricks, at whatever price? Naimi concedes the math can work. Stripe at 100 billion dollars in 2021 is worth 179 billion today, roughly an 11 to 12 percent annual return, still better than the bottom three quarters of venture. Great companies compound, and backing them at full price may secure a top quartile fund. What worries him is that this has become the consensus move: a record number of firms have converged on the same handful of names as an easy way to deploy large funds, and he cannot recall an easy strategy at this scale that stayed a good one. On AI data companies, he thinks the old dismissal, that selling data is a trade rather than a business, underestimated how fast model builders' data needs keep growing. In robotics he sees two camps: companies that believe selling data is a durable, decades long business, and companies selling data to finance their own robot research. Looking ahead, he expects the newly wealthy employees of the IPO bound giants to swell the angel ranks, deepening the density of people in Silicon Valley willing to write a 100,000 to one million dollar check and not care if it vanishes.

Abstract itself has outgrown pure seed. It has led Series B rounds, made passive growth investments such as Crusoe, put about 15 percent of its 2023 vintage into the formation round of xAI, and backed Polymarket's Series B heavily. Its first fund was 100 million dollars in 2018, when limited partners scolded him for raising too big a seed fund; the latest is 800 million, and partners praised his discipline for staying small. Asked his biggest mistake, he says Abstract has always raised too little: he believes every fund could have held twice the capital at the same return profile, which would have meant leading more rounds and giving founders more. The method now is to deploy each fund as well as possible and size the next one to what can actually be put to work. He closes with ratings: the opportunity set is a 10 out of 10 against his decade in venture, the largest number of companies he has ever wanted to back, while frothiness rates a 6.5 to 7 against 2021's 10, because so much of it is justified by growth and adoption. His bellwethers are the next generation rather than the IPO queue: AI native drug discovery efforts aiming at a next generation Genentech, companies built on owning your own data and intelligence, prediction markets growing at extraordinary rates without being AI companies at all, and Physical Intelligence, whose talent density and retention he reads as a powerful signal.

If the IPO window disappoints or the giants' valuations stall, how quickly does the crunch travel from funds down to ordinary seed rounds?
  2. Naimi bets there will be two or three more trillion dollar companies, not forty, yet the whole market prices seed deals off the trillion dollar lens. When the count becomes clear, how much of today's seed and Series A pricing survives the repricing?
  3. The neolabs need debt at five to ten times their equity, and the neoclouds' public market slide is raising the cost of that debt. Does the AI buildout slow because equity dries up, or because lenders, not venture capitalists, are the real constraint?

ARK Invest · Ara Kharazian on the cracks in the AI thesis

Ramp's Economist Found Cracks In The AI Thesis | The Brainstorm 156

On ARK Invest's The Brainstorm, episode 156, "Ramp's Economist Found Cracks In The AI Thesis", Ara Kharazian, the lead economist at Ramp, joins hosts Sam and Brett to present data drawn from more than 80,000 businesses and over $275 billion in annual business spend that flows across Ramp's platform. His latest research series argues that beneath the rapid headline growth in corporate AI spending, the market is far more concentrated, and more fragile, than the standard story suggests.

The run-up itself is not in dispute. Over the two years Ramp has tracked it, AI spend has grown faster than any other spend category in the data, and it has spread across providers: Anthropic is now the most popular AI model provider among businesses, and OpenAI and Anthropic together account for roughly 95 percent of the market, with xAI taking a small share and Meta only just entering enterprise. Kharazian's caution is about who is doing the spending. One percent of businesses drive 80 percent of all AI spend. The median firm that spends on AI spends about $15 per employee per month, roughly one chat subscription per employee plus a coding agent for someone, while a top 1 percent firm spends $7,000 to $10,000 per employee per month. The top 10 percent of firms are still growing their spend, and the median firm is creeping up, from about $12 per employee per month a month ago to $15 now.

The crack he points to is at the very top. The heaviest adopters appear to have started slowing their AI spend: September came in below July, and the earliest October data shows the slowdown continuing from the hyper-growth of the first half of the year. This is not because they are using AI less. Token volumes kept rising over the same months. The drivers are economic. The price of AI has fallen by almost half over the period, as OpenAI and Anthropic cut prices and ship more efficient models, large enterprises are negotiating contracts with new discounts, and capable models at the standard and light tiers are taking work that used to require frontier models. A firm can double its usage and still watch its bill flatten.

Brett pushes back hard. Three months, he argues, do not make a trend: the top 1 percent is still up four or five times year over year, and Ramp's sample skews toward tech-forward, venture-backed companies that were always going to over-index on AI. He also reads the pricing pressure strategically. Anthropic has just filed an amended S-1 ahead of going public and, on his account, is investing in pricing to drive token volume; both leading labs want longer-term contracts in place, because their credibility with the suppliers financing hundreds of billions of dollars in infrastructure depends on showing durable revenue growth to set against those liabilities. And the huge gap between the median firm and the top 1 percent, he argues, has to close through diffusion rather than the leaders stopping.

The sharpest exchange concerns revenue quality. Sam cites Chamath's estimate that 70 to 80 percent of revenue at AI companies comes from other AI companies funded by venture capital, and Kharazian agrees with the framing: not a circular revenue problem, but a revenue quality problem. The top 1 percent of AI spenders on Ramp are by and large venture-backed tech companies, which is sustainable while they keep growing and raising, but the concentration is also a correlation. If the AI trade slows, those firms will all slow their AI spend at the same time, and the impact could cascade across the sector. If a company cannot raise its next round, its AI spend stops.

Kharazian adds that maturing adopters learn to control the bill. Ramp itself is a profitable top 1 percent spender, yet it now blocks frontier models on automated tasks, sets cheaper defaults, and uses routers to send work to the right tier. Brett notes the same discipline from the supply side: OpenAI cut the price of one of its efficient models, Luna, by 80 percent to win marginal share, and when performance per dollar roughly halves every couple of months, a company can hold its activity constant and halve its cost.

The open question is what the other 99 percent will ever spend. Coding agents are, all three agree, the most commercially advanced form of AI today, and Kharazian doubts the median business will extract from them anything like the value a software engineer gets, so forecasts that assume the median firm climbs to top 1 percent intensity are assuming new product categories that do not exist yet. Brett counters that security could force broad spending, since cheaper attacks from open-weight models will oblige even non-tech firms to defend their patchwork infrastructure, and Sam floats the private equity angle: buy a median firm, spend like a top 10 percent firm, and outcompete the industry.

Asked for a five-year level, Kharazian puts the median firm at under $1,000 per employee per month, with AI use table stakes rather than a differentiator, and he resists even that precision, at one point wondering whether he means per year. Against predictions in his circles of tens of thousands per employee per month, he benchmarks against a computer purchase: significant, but not a second payroll. Brett does the arithmetic the other way: $1,000 a month across a billion knowledge workers is $12 trillion a year, so even the bear case is enormous. Kharazian's closing position is narrower than a collapse call. He does not expect AI spend to fall; he thinks the headwinds to its growth are stronger, and more neglected, than the tailwinds right now.

Open questions

  1. If prices keep falling fast enough that usage can double while bills stay flat, which number should investors actually watch as the health signal for the AI trade: dollars spent, tokens consumed, or business outcomes delivered?
  2. If the top 1 percent of AI spenders are mostly venture-backed firms whose budgets all depend on the same funding climate, how much of the leading labs' revenue growth survives a venture downturn, and how quickly would the cascade reach the infrastructure buildout?
  3. Will the median firm ever close most of the gap with the top 1 percent by spending more, or will cheaper models let it capture most of the value at something like today's modest levels, leaving the intensity gap permanently in place?

No Priors · Vlad Tenev on tokenization, agentic trading, and expanding ownership

Tokenization, Agentic Trading, Harmonic: Robinhood's Mission to Expand Ownership with CEO Vlad Tenev

On No Priors, host Sarah Guo sits down with Vlad Tenev, the co-founder and chief executive of Robinhood, for a conversation organized around a single word: ownership. Tenev says Robinhood is live in some form in more than 120 countries and has grown from a phone app for buying stocks at zero commission into a global financial conglomerate, and that everything it now builds, from tokenized stocks to accounts for newborns, is an attempt to let more people own more of the economy.

His case for ownership runs on two levels. For an individual, accumulating assets is the tried and true route to financial freedom, and the things a person owns become part of their identity. For a society, he argues, the stakes are higher: a society without broad ownership is inherently unstable, because people who own things have a stake in the system and become its defenders. That second argument does much of the work in the episode, since Tenev keeps returning to the backlash against AI and asking who actually gets to share in its gains.

Guo asks how a mathematician became a missionary for mass ownership, and Tenev reaches back to his childhood. He was born in Bulgaria in the late 1980s and immigrated to the United States in the 1990s, after a stretch in which he did not see his father for a year and a half. Bulgaria's inflation ran at roughly 170 percent in 1991 and, by 1997, at something like 2,000 percent annualized, and his grandparents kept their savings in copper cookware because pots held value better than the currency. He describes an independent childhood under real financial pressure, a gift for mathematics that teachers kept accelerating, study under Terence Tao, a mathematics PhD program he later left, and work in high-frequency trading.

The first major topic is Harmonic, the startup Tenev co-founded to build mathematical superintelligence. Its model, Aristotle, was one of three that reached gold-medal level at the International Mathematical Olympiad last year, and since being released publicly it has been used by individuals on unsolved problems, including Erdős problems. Tenev notes that mathematics itself is changing character, converging with coding as researchers adopt tools like Lean and version control. His objection is to how the progress is organized: it is too centralized, he says, with a few AI companies using big data centers and secret models to do the work. He predicts that landmark results will be both more likely and more celebrated when they come from individuals using public tools, and warns that labs which hoard discovery risk losing their license to operate.

Tokenization gets the longest product discussion. The mechanism mirrors stablecoins: real assets sit custodied at a traditional institution while tokens minted against them trade freely, in fractional amounts, anywhere in the world. Robinhood launched stock tokens in Europe last year, then relaunched them this year in fully DeFi-enabled form alongside Robinhood Chain, its blockchain for real-world assets, which Tenev describes as among the fastest growing in history and first in developer activity. Developers are already composing with stock tokens as building blocks, pairing them with meme coins and NFTs, and Tenev says the deeper purpose is access: in countries without functioning capital markets, a phone and a non-custodial wallet could do for US equities what stablecoins did for dollars. To critics who call it a corporate chain, he answers that Robinhood trades some decentralization purity for focused leadership and trusted, company-backed primitives that developers can rely on.

Prediction markets, in his telling, are also about ownership, of knowledge. People hold expertise that used to be monetizable only indirectly, through a stock or an option that might fall even when the underlying call was right. Robinhood's planned KPI contracts attack that directly, letting users trade the earnings-per-share or revenue print itself, and contracts such as whether a California billionaire tax will pass can serve as hedges. He also frames prediction markets as an evolution of news: many users never trade at all and simply open the app to read the odds.

Robinhood launched an MCP-based agentic trading product earlier this year, and Tenev says more than 150,000 customers have opened agentic trading accounts, with tool calls running at roughly 30 million a day by the last announced figure. Robinhood Agents, unveiled at the company's Hood Summit, moves the harness and interface inside the account, with reusable skills and an app store where, for example, a strategy could draw on satellite data. The north star, he says, is to put the capabilities of a hedge fund into an individual's pocket, and he expects adoption to follow the coding-agents pattern: sophisticated users first, then everyone. The safeguards are structural. Agentic accounts are separate and segregated, trade approvals are on by default, the system is sandboxed, and much of the harness was built in house. The sharpest section concerns who owns the AI revolution. The leading AI companies are private, stay private longer, and now go public at valuations so large that the life-changing returns are gone before an ordinary investor can buy in, so the gains concentrate among venture capitalists and institutions. Crypto, for all its losses, built the opposite pattern: broad individual ownership from the start, which produced a constituency that defended the industry politically in 2024. Robinhood Ventures is his answer. Fund One raised money from retail customers through the company's IPO channel into a vehicle that trades on the New York Stock Exchange like a stock and holds late-stage private companies including OpenAI, ElevenLabs, and Crusoe. Tokenized private shares are the parallel path outside the United States, with the goal of private companies trading around the clock in fractional amounts.

That agenda runs into the accredited investor rules, which Tenev wants loosened. He welcomes an SEC initiative, announced the week before the recording, directing FINRA to create a knowledge-based accreditation exam, roughly two hours at a testing center, as an alternative to wealth tests, while calling it still too high-friction. His customers, he says, overwhelmingly endorse the statement that they should be in charge of their own money. Disclosure should be absolute, but prohibition is harder to defend when anyone can freely buy a meme coin, which regulators have treated as a collectible, while shares in a private company are restricted under rules written before the internet existed.

The final product topic is Trump accounts. Robinhood was selected as the sole initial trustee and broker, working with BNY, the Treasury, and the IRS, to run accounts in which every child born from January 2025 onward receives 1,000 dollars from the Treasury invested in a low-cost S&P 500 fund. The Dell family foundation added more than 6 billion dollars aimed at children in lower-income zip codes, Gwynne Shotwell committed SpaceX shares, and Brad Gerstner, whom Tenev credits as the program's architect, made a large donation in Indiana. Tenev's ambition is household stock ownership rising from about 65 percent toward 95 percent, and a government product good enough to endure.

Guo closes on temperament, quoting a colleague's description of Tenev as pairing very high intelligence with the steadiest of hands. His explanation is calibration: a childhood that included possible deportation and 2,000 percent inflation makes business crises feel survivable, and he tries, imperfectly, to give his own children some constructed challenge. His advice to other leaders is borrowed from Jeff Bezos: you cannot control the outside world, so grow thick skin.

Masters of Scale · Demis Hassabis on how AI could become dangerous

Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis: How AI could become dangerous (with Reid Hoffman) | Summit '26

On Masters of Scale, the episode "Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis: How AI could become dangerous (with Reid Hoffman) | Summit '26" is a live conversation recorded at the Masters of Scale Summit in October 2026, at the Presidio Theatre in San Francisco. Reid Hoffman, who has known Hassabis for more than a decade and once served on a Google AI ethics board, interviews the DeepMind co-founder about governing frontier AI, why progress in recursive self-improvement worries him, and why he thinks the field should build intelligent tools rather than conscious machines.

Hassabis begins with the reason he works on AI at all. His whole career, he says, has been aimed at using AI as the ultimate tool for advancing science, medicine, and our understanding of the world, and the first use he would choose is improving human health. What better demonstration of what these tools are for, he asks, than helping to cure terrible diseases, and he points to his own projects in that direction: AlphaFold, and the spinout drug discovery company Isomorphic Labs.

The AlphaFold story carries that argument. Proteins are the building blocks of life, and predicting a protein's structure from its amino acid sequence was a 50-year grand challenge, sometimes described to him as Fermat's last theorem for biology. Knowing a protein's 3D surface is critical both for understanding biological mechanisms and for designing a drug that binds to the right part of it. AlphaFold 2, finished back in 2020, accelerated that step by orders of magnitude, and Hassabis says it remains perhaps the best concrete example of AI cracking a scientific problem thought to be uncrackable: around 4 million researchers now use it, and almost every future drug will have made use of its structures. But structure alone is not a drug, so the work is expanding into biochemistry, predicting properties such as toxicity and ADME behavior that decide whether a compound is safe and effective.

The conversation then turns to recursive self-improvement, and Hassabis gives his theory of the game. RSI works best in verifiable domains, he says: math, coding, and originally games, where an answer can be checked as correct, code can be run, or a win is a win. AlphaZero was one of the first famous examples, learning purely by playing against itself, a million games at a time, then training a slightly better successor. As a chess player he felt it viscerally: starting from random play in the morning, it reached the point where he could still just about beat it at coffee time, was grandmaster level by lunch, and by tea time was better than any chess entity, human or machine. Verifiable domains also let you generate almost unlimited synthetic data, because each new problem or move can be checked for correctness.

His worry is about the next phase. Today's RSI keeps a human firmly in the loop: AI tools make an engineer perhaps three to five times more productive, but humans still review the code and architect the systems. The dangerous step is closed-loop RSI, recursive self-improvement with no human reviewer in the process. It would be faster, but Hassabis calls it a very dangerous way to reach full AGI, for two reasons. Control becomes hard to keep over a process that improves itself, the old intelligence explosion concern. And the end result would likely be an alien intelligence, with no reason to sit in a mind space similar to ours, which would make it hard for such a system to understand us, or us it. He urges real caution as labs get closer to that closed loop, while acknowledging the intense competitive race pushing the other way.

Asked how labs should navigate that race, Hassabis starts with defense. Cyber defense has to accelerate faster than cyber attack, with trusted defenders given early access to model versions tuned for defense. Sandboxes need to be far more hardened, and monitoring far better: many of the cyber incidents of the last six months, he says, could have been addressed with more robust engineering, and agents should never be able to run for weeks without anyone noticing. Eventually he wants formal guarantees and bounds on what these systems can do, and he is encouraging top mathematicians he knows to turn their skills toward complexity theory and game theory, because the field will soon have to keep track of swarms of agents potentially collaborating with each other.

On governance, he points to an essay he published over the summer proposing an industry-run standards body, an SRO, with some government oversight, US-led at first and ideally international later. The problem, he concedes, is that international institutions are very weak just when they need to be strongest, which Hoffman translates out of British understatement for the audience. The essay did land in the White House and among peer labs, and some of its energy fed into a joint accord the major players signed: better than nothing, Hassabis says, since it at least acknowledges the problems, but it says little about what to do, and that has to come next. The shared ground he sees includes third-party auditors and evaluators, an ecosystem like the audit industry in finance, with firms such as Accenture already building expertise, and the leading labs red-teaming each other's systems before general release.

The deepest disagreement comes over consciousness. Prompted by claims that a chatbot saying it is conscious settles the matter, Hassabis notes there is still no agreed definition of consciousness, and behavior alone is weak evidence: with another human, we also share the same substrate, the same kind of brain, which makes it parsimonious to assume they feel what we feel, and that second leg is missing for an AI. His position is that we should be building intelligent tools today, not conscious entities, for two reasons. The first is technical. There is a direct trade-off between self-volition, a system acting on its own internal judgements, and corrigibility, its willingness to follow instructions and be switched off or redirected. Train a system to reject commands based on its own workings and you are exposed whenever your constitution or rules are wrong, which is exactly what Asimov's stories about his three laws kept warning. Today's systems are more malleable than many expected fifteen or twenty years ago, which is genuinely good news for steerability, but Hassabis insists the misalignment problem is not solved, and combining self-volition with recursive self-improvement would be doubly dangerous.

The second reason is societal. Society has not yet had the debate, with philosophers, theologians, artists, and the humanities, and across many cultures, about whether to cross what he calls the second Rubicon: creating entities whose moral standing we would then have to reckon with. His proposal is to cross the first Rubicon alone, build the intelligent tools, use them to do the neuroscience that finally identifies the neural correlates of consciousness, which might take five to ten years, and only then hold the discussion about whether to cross the second. He also rejects the claim that great intelligence requires consciousness: the two are double dissociable, he argues, since today's systems are smart without, in his view, being conscious at all, while his dog is conscious, dreams, and is not that smart, and the same holds for dolphins, monkeys, and elephants.

Finally, on AI sovereignty, Hassabis expects the frontier to stay crowded: six to eight offerings, including Chinese companies, leapfrogging each other with leaders perhaps three to six months ahead, and no one better on every vector at once. Enterprises can arbitrage between them. Open source is maybe six months to a year behind, and carries the still unsolved problem of restricting bad actors while enabling scientists and other good actors, though he says the West needs open source solutions of its own. Companies and countries with sensitive data will fine-tune privately on it, but he would not recommend every country try to build a frontier model. The better play is to build value on top of these near-commodity tools, in sectors like healthcare, and to collaborate across borders, which is where Hoffman closes the session: agency in the AI age is collective, something countries have to take together, for the benefit of humanity.

Open questions

  1. If competitive pressure makes closed-loop recursive self-improvement hard to refuse, can monitoring, sandboxes, and formal bounds actually keep a human meaningfully in control of a process that improves itself faster than people can review it, or does oversight become nominal at that speed?
  2. Hassabis's governance plan depends on credible third-party auditors and an industry standards body becoming international while international institutions are at their weakest. Who staffs and funds those evaluators so they stay independent of the labs they are meant to check?
  3. If intelligence and consciousness really are dissociable, what evidence could ever justify crossing the second Rubicon, and who gets a say in deciding that society is ready, given the debate he calls for has barely begun?

Mini Brains, Beating Heart Organoids, and the AI That Predicts Them — Fabian Theis, Helmholtz Munich

On Latent Space, this edition of the AI for Science series has hosts RJ Honicky and Brandon Anderson interviewing Fabian Theis, who directs the Computational Health Center at Helmholtz Munich and whose lab built Scanpy and the scverse open-source ecosystem used across computational biology. The subject is the virtual cell: a model that can predict how a living cell will behave. The conversation arrives with money moving behind the idea, including a 1.8 billion dollar initiative from Biohub together with Google, Meta, the Department of Energy, and the National Institutes of Health, aimed at collecting the multiscale data needed to make biology predictable.

For Theis, a virtual cell comes down to one hard problem: given a cell and something done to it, predict what happens next. His lab began this work in 2019 with scGen, an early model that shifted perturbed cells through a learned latent space. He is frank that the early models were limited less by their mathematics than by the data and the questions, which were simplistic. What has changed is data generation. Single experiments now measure ten million transcriptomes at once. Labs run full CRISPR perturbation screens across a hundred cell lines, or batteries of a thousand drugs, where once the perturbation side of the data was a thin control-versus-disease comparison.

The basic object is the transcriptome, the count of messenger RNAs in a single cell, which can be pictured as a vector of roughly 20,000 numbers. Cells cluster in that space into types, or stretch along trajectories as they differentiate. A perturbation, in his broad usage, is anything that acts on a cell: a gene knocked out by CRISPR, a signal from a neighboring cell, a hormone, the chemistry of the growth medium, the Yamanaka factors that reprogram skin cells toward an embryo-like state, or ordinary human genetic variation. A real virtual cell, he argues, has to take all of that as input, in combination, along with disease and time.

That is why the title's organoids matter. Researchers can now grow miniature multicellular systems in a dish: little brains whose neurons wire up and talk to each other, heart organoids that actually beat, colon and cancer systems that are comparatively tractable. Organoids give far richer readouts than a single cell line, and they make otherwise inaccessible tissue studiable, for instance by growing mini brains from the skin cells of patients with neurodegenerative disease. Theis's group runs perturbation experiments on organoids and is building a cancer organoid perturbation atlas with pharmaceutical partners who pay for early access. Modeling these systems is unsettled territory: there is no agreed language for a cell's neighborhood, and today models mostly treat an organoid as a distribution of cells, with graph-based descriptions still to come.

Running through the modeling discussion is the Bitter Lesson. Theis warns that strong hand-built assumptions cost predictive power, and gives AlphaFold as the example: later versions stopped hard-coding rotational symmetry and let the model learn it from data, gaining flexibility. Biology, though, is not yet data-rich on the perturbation side, however rich it is in cells, so he expects hybrid models, part learned and part structured, to be the practical answer for now, with interpretation layered on afterward.

That caution explains why the first wave of biological foundation models disappointed. On plain cell type prediction, logistic regression remains close to the state of the art, and his lab once published a paper whose title made the point bluntly: deep learning is not needed for cell type prediction. Part of the problem, he says, is the benchmarks themselves. Work by Bo Wang and collaborators showed that in some celebrated cases the metric, not the model, was at fault, and the Arc Institute's Virtual Cell Challenge shipped with metrics that could be exploited. Once the exploitation was stripped out, the winning approach was a flow-matching model, which he takes as evidence that the nonlinear machinery does earn its keep on harder tasks. His advice to students is unfashionable and firm: build the simple baseline first, even the predict-the-mean baseline, because the data are noisy enough to fool anyone.

His own lab's recent work tries to ask harder questions. Nicheformer, a foundation model for spatial data, asks whether a cell's expression state can predict the state of its neighbors, a fingerprint of the communication between them. scConcept learns representations by contrastive learning and shows clean scaling behavior, from 30 million cells in the paper to a model trained on 350 million cells across 16 species that the lab has now released. CellFlow then treats perturbation as a world-model problem: state plus action in, next state out, using flow matching on top of the learned representation to generate the effect of a perturbation. It has been accepted at Nature Methods. Representation learning alone, he insists, is not a virtual cell; the action-conditioned step is where the biology happens.

Theis also describes the institution behind the work. Helmholtz is a federally funded research organization, and his Computational Health Center has grown to roughly 45 group leaders and 500 to 600 people, which he calls the largest academic site for health AI research in Europe. There is no undergraduate teaching, only doctoral students and postdocs, with affiliations to Munich's two universities and a deliberate push toward translation. On startups, he says Germany now creates them at a rate comparable to the United States, with Munich the leading hotspot, but the ecosystem drops off at the scaling stage, where the large risk-taking funds are thinner and companies head to the US for big rounds. His own lever is expectation: he has told every group leader in his center to aim for at least one spin-off within three years.

He is similarly unsentimental about business models. Company after company that set out to build a foundation model of biology has pivoted into developing drugs, because a drug is the asset investors understand, while a platform's path to revenue stays murky. The positions he finds durable couple modeling to proprietary data generation, as with Noetik, or with his own organoid atlas, where the unique dataset is the moat. Widely used open tools, he notes from experience with Scanpy, do not by themselves make a business.

His answer to the talent question is a training design he calls the Y model, run through the Munich School for Data Science. Every student has two advisors, one for methods and one for the application domain, and attends both labs' meetings, learning the biology deeply enough to reformulate vague experimental questions as metrics and models. Eight years in, he says, the graduates scatter across pharma, startups, and pure AI work; Nvidia hired two of his people within a single month not long ago.

The closing topic is where he thinks the field goes next: closing the loop. His lab's new system, LabCompass, is an inverse generative model. Given a forward world model of the cell and a state you want to reach, it proposes what to do to get there. Built with a Cambridge collaborator working on blood formation, it has already been through three or four real cycles of model, experiment, and revision. Theis stresses that the hard part is as much organizational as computational: persuading experimentalists to spend weeks testing a model's chosen experiment, against their own instincts. The long-term vision is reinforcement learning with the laboratory itself inside the environment. For now, he concedes, what exists is closer to active learning, but the direction is set: from mapping cells, to predicting them, to steering the next experiment.

The Peterman Pod · Xavier Leroy on OCaml and almost-correct AI code

Creator of OCaml: The Danger of Almost-Correct AI Code | Xavier Leroy

On The Peterman Pod, the episode "Creator of OCaml: The Danger of Almost-Correct AI Code | Xavier Leroy" is an excerpt from host Ryan Peterman's longer conversation with Xavier Leroy, the creator of the OCaml programming language and a central figure in formal verification. Peterman returns to a warning Leroy has given in an earlier interview: the danger of almost correct code, the plausible but wrong code that a language model can produce, and asks how that problem should be addressed.

Leroy says it is a tough problem. He describes himself as a little skeptical about general AI: the models do amazing things that were unthinkable a few years ago, but their output always contains errors, and it cannot simply be trusted. In principle, humans should be there to check what the model produces, fix the errors themselves, or send the output back to the model until the result is usable.

In practice, he argues, that division of labor collapses under the volume of output, which he calls the slop problem. Generative AI produces text, code, and pictures so easily and in such quantities that there is no human time to check it all. He recounts someone at an AI startup enthusing that generative AI is driving the cost of programming to zero, and answers that the cost of writing code may be dropping, but the cost of checking it, and of making sure it is correct and does what we want, is not zero at all.

For Leroy, every new line of code is a liability. It has to be tested, checked, perhaps formally verified, and later evolved and maintained. What he wants is fifty lines of code that have been thought through and polished over the years, and that, he says, is not what he is getting with AI. He also doubts the checking role is one humans will fill even if asked: there is too much output, and reviewing it is not pleasant work either. It is, he says, not a good way to split the work between machines and humans.

One response he sees emerging is social, a movement to say no to AI slop, and he points to open source projects that refuse generated contributions because there are simply too many of them. He has felt it himself: for OCaml, and especially for CompCert, his formally verified C compiler, he has received issue reports that were obviously generated by AI, perhaps one good report among ten, each several pages long, with detailed explanations and reproduction cases that in the end reproduce nothing, or reproduce something else. Going through them takes real time, and he says he may at some point say no to AI generated contributions himself.

The technical answer he finds more promising is to have AI produce proofs: evidence that its creation is correct. This is starting to work for mathematical proofs, where models produce arguments both in English and in the formal language of the Lean prover, and Lean can reject a proof that does not check, which gives some confidence. Human review is still needed, but over much smaller quantities of text, and he thinks the same approach could one day work for programs, with the generator producing a proof that the program satisfies a specification.

He is careful about the caveats. Models sometimes change the statement or the definitions to make a proof easier, and so-called self formalization, where the AI itself extracts definitions and statements from a document, can introduce errors at that step. The statement has to be checked as carefully as the proof.

That leads to what he calls the big standing issue for formal methods: where the specification comes from. Verification is hard, but agreeing on the specification can be difficult too. Mathematicians have long experience at finding definitions they find interesting and stating theorems that mean something. Programmers, he says, are less good at this, and it is fairly easy to write specifications that are inconsistent or impossible. His example is an average function whose precondition on its three arguments can never be satisfied: the function body then verifies no matter how wrong it is, because the function can never be called, and the result is a false sense of confidence, something verified but unusable. He is not sure AI will help much with that part, though he notes one possibility, testing specifications themselves, pointing a test suite at the spec to see whether it checks out. Either way, some of the difficulty moves from the programming phase to the specification phase.

Peterman then asks how a decade of LLM generated code might change the programming language landscape. Leroy recalls that a couple of years ago the concern ran the other way: there might not be enough OCaml code in the wild for a model to learn the language, since there is far less of it than JavaScript. That concern has not held up. Contemporary models, by the feedback he receives, generate OCaml that is quite decent and similar in quality to what they produce in more popular languages. He can only speculate about why: perhaps the smaller body of OCaml code is of higher average quality, perhaps learning has become more efficient, perhaps models transfer knowledge from one language to another better than expected.

One thing that does seem to help the models, he says, is a type system. Static checking of types already avoids some errors, and it encourages the model to declare types first, giving the program some structure. As for ten years from now, he calls it difficult to guess. It could be that the most popular languages of today become even more popular because of generative AI, or that the safer languages of today become more popular because AI written code in them has fewer errors in the end. He is hoping it will be the safer languages, but he really does not know.

  1. If the cost of checking AI output stays high while the cost of producing it falls to near zero, what norms or tools will keep review effort proportional to the value of a contribution, and who sets them?
  2. Can the specification problem be made tractable for working programmers, through habits like testing specifications, soon enough for AI generated proofs about programs to matter?
  3. Over the next decade, will training data incumbency or error reducing safety decide which programming languages AI writes most, and how would anyone tell early which force is winning?

Mustafa Suleyman: Will AI Make Everything Cheaper? | Intelligence Squared

On Intelligence Squared, the episode "Mustafa Suleyman: Will AI Make Everything Cheaper? | Intelligence Squared" is an edited cut of an on-stage conversation from September 2023. The exchange opens on the upside: if AI makes both innovation and management far more efficient, what does that enable?

Suleyman's answer starts from a definition. Intelligence, he argues, has been the engine of creation: everything around us is the product of people interacting with an environment to make something more efficient or cheaper. A kilo of grain today takes just 2 percent of the labor it took a century ago, and the trajectory of technology is always toward cheaper and easier. AI, in his telling, applies that same trick to intelligence itself.

On agriculture, that means new crops that are drought resistant, pest resistant, and generally more resilient. On climate, it means optimizing the industrial systems we already have, the big cooling infrastructure being his example, and doing more with less. Across healthcare, education, and transportation, he expects massive efficiencies over the next two to three decades.

The larger claim is about invention. The models, he says, do not merely recombine what they have seen: they imagine the space between concepts and produce things that never existed, which is how new knowledge and new science get discovered. Get that right, he argues, and we are headed for an era of radical abundance.

In that era, everyone has a scientific advisor, a research assistant, a chief of staff, a tutor, a coach, and a confidant, roles that today are the preserve of the wealthy and the educated. He compares it to hardware: the best smartphone in the world is the same for a millionaire and for someone on a regular salary, and he expects intelligence to follow the same meritocratic path.

Minton Beddoes then calls time on the optimism, joking that they have had 20 minutes of upbeat, more than most of his interviews allow. Suleyman says he is wide-eyed about the risks. His prediction is that powerful models follow the path of the transistor and of every useful technology: they get smaller, cheaper, and easier to use, and so they spread far and wide. Proliferation has so far been a good thing, delivering immense benefits. The flip side is that really powerful tools can empower a vast array of bad actors to destabilize the world.

His concrete example is weapons coaching. The labs, he says, have all observed that models trained on everything on the internet can give good guidance on manufacturing biological and chemical weapons. The big companies exclude that material from training data and prevent those capabilities, and they want to abide by the law. The challenge is that everyone wants access, so the models are widely available in open source: smaller versions of Pi or ChatGPT can already be downloaded at no cost, and on a ten year trajectory they become much more powerful, smaller, and more transferable.

Minton Beddoes puts the containment question directly: the leading companies are few and their models the biggest, but a whole set of open source models is not far behind, so can the open ones, available to an angry teenager in a garage, be controlled or not? Suleyman's darker answer is that these systems are fundamentally ideas. An algorithm can largely be expressed on three sheets of paper, and most people can wrap their heads around it. Running one takes vast amounts of compute today, but remove that constraint, as he expects within a decade when such a model can run on a phone, and the containment challenge comes fully into view.

He is careful to say the risk does not sit only with open source. The technology also confers power on the companies building and running the models, his own included, alongside Google and the other big tech providers. We do not eliminate risk simply by addressing the open source community, he argues; we also have to settle the relationship between these very powerful companies and the nation state, which is ultimately responsible for holding them accountable.

The last topic is jobs. Minton Beddoes notes that the familiar fear, that AIs matching or exceeding human intelligence leave no jobs for anyone, is one she regards as bunkum: history has never run out of jobs, and as a paid-up economist she calls it the lump of labor fallacy. Suleyman walks through the fallacy on her side: automation creates time, health, and wealth, which create new demand, which creates new goods, services, and jobs, from the app designer to the present-day prompt engineer. On a two decade horizon, he says, structural disemployment, where people want to contribute their labor and simply cannot compete, is highly unlikely, and there is no evidence of it in the statistics today.

Beyond that horizon he concedes more. AIs are increasingly accurate, more reliable, able to work around the clock, and more stable, so it is possible that many people, even with an AI beside them, cannot produce things of sufficient value for the market to want them. His response is that we should lean into that and be honest about it, because work is not the goal of society: well-being, peace, and prosperity are. Many people, he notes, do jobs they would never do if they did not have to work. The goal, as he names it, is radical abundance, creating more with radically less and liberating people from the obligation to work. The published clip ends mid-sentence, in the middle of that answer.

  2. He argues the nation state must hold the big labs accountable, yet governments also compete to attract and champion those same companies. Who writes rules that genuinely bind firms this powerful, and how are they enforced?
  3. Past the two decade horizon he describes, if many people cannot sell their labor even with an AI beside them, what institution distributes the abundance he predicts, and who sets its terms?

Nate B Jones · The end of git? OpenAI CTO on the future of coding

The end of git? OpenAI CTO on the future of coding.

On AI News & Strategy Daily, Nate B Jones sits down with Vijay Raji, OpenAI's CTO of Applications, who joined the company when it acquired Statsig, the Seattle company he founded. The conversation starts from a striking internal problem: at OpenAI, agents began shipping code faster than the company's own systems could absorb, and the strain eventually forced OpenAI to build a new code repository system that replaces Git. Around that story, the two range over the next billion ChatGPT users, why they will mostly speak rather than type, and a litmus test Raji uses to judge whether a founder is building on AI in the right way.

The break point, Raji says, came in February 2026, when powerful models combined with Codex running multiple agents in parallel across work trees sent the number of pull requests from engineers sharply upward. Token usage is an imperfect, one-dimensional proxy for engineering productivity, he concedes, but as a measure of breadth and depth of AI use it tells a story: usage at frontier labs has grown about 17 times in a year, against roughly twice at an average software shop.

Cheap code changes what engineers do all day. When an idea can be turned into working code quickly, teams generate far more prototypes, and more code is thrown away than before, because sketching in code costs so little. Coding itself used to be the long pole that justified long planning conversations up front; now the bottleneck moves downstream. When AI-assisted engineers produce five times more code, review becomes the constraint, and behind it testing, continuous integration and deployment, and instrumentation. OpenAI's responses so far include a system of automatic reviews that teams can turn on, and a broader rethink of its build and deployment infrastructure to keep pace.

The deepest failure was the repository itself. OpenAI runs a monorepo, and the volume of code flowing in and out rose by more than an order of magnitude beyond what the current system was designed to support, with agents constantly checking out code, running tests, and pushing back to main. So the company set a team to solve the problem from different assumptions. Git was built for offline work: check out code, keep branches locally, push when done. Agents are online all the time, so a repository designed for them can be built for far more throughput. The hardest problems turned out to be less about locking main against collisions, which throughput solves, and more about the checkout itself: sparse checkout and caching everywhere, so agents pull from caches instead of hammering main. The internally built system now supports the entire monorepo and all of OpenAI's engineers.

Jones draws out the management pattern behind that fix. The team was handed the problem, not the method: go solve it and come back with a solution. Raji agrees that is how it works, and notes the extra difficulty that OpenAI's own engineers are the hardest customers, demanding and vocal, while the old system had to keep running as the new one was built and traffic was transposed onto it. Replacing a scaled solution with another scaled solution, Jones observes, is combinatorially complex.

The second half turns to growth. ChatGPT has 1.2 billion users, Raji says, reached with an interface that is essentially a text box and a button. The next billion users will not arrive through that interface: English will not be their most comfortable language, and typing will not be their most comfortable modality, especially in Indic languages whose scripts make keyboards awkward. Fresh from a trip to India, Raji argues the next modality is voice, pointing out that people there already send WhatsApp voice messages to friends rather than type. Voice-first is what his growth team is now thinking about.

Raji is also an example of what has changed for leaders. Rather than bringing the team an idea for a voice-first ChatGPT and asking them to explore it, he built the prototype himself with Codex: an app stripped down to voice, where the microphone opens the moment the app launches so people can start speaking while the connection is still being established, and the recording is replayed once the connection lands. Only after validating the idea and the experience did he take it to the team and ask them to build the real thing. Jones notes he has seen this pattern elsewhere, leaders who used to direct becoming individual contributors again, and Raji endorses it: the old line was that code wins arguments, and now almost anyone can make a point with a real product instead of a document.

For founders, Raji offers a litmus test. Models keep getting better on a monthly cadence; by his reckoning the smallest model available now is about ten times more powerful than the biggest model of a year ago. So when a new model drops, watch your own reaction. If you are worried the model will take over what your product does, you are not building the right product. If you are excited, because your product's capabilities just improved with it, you have used the models in the right way.

His proof case is OpenAI's ads team. The team is less than a year old, roughly nine or ten months, and it reached a billion dollar annual revenue run rate within seven or eight months of starting from zero code, with only about 50 people. The shortcut was to build the ads stack on general model capability at every stage, the demand indexer, the query rewriter, candidate selection, the auction, and the ranker, instead of spending far longer building a bespoke machine learning model for each stage. The payoff compounds: every time the models improve, the entire ads system gets better on its own.

  2. Raji's litmus test rewards founders whose products improve automatically as models improve. Does that same property quietly erase their moat, since every competitor standing on the same models gets the same free upgrade?

Asaf Ezra and Steph Zhang on rebuilding defense in depth for machine speed

Defense in Depth, Rebuilt for Machine Speed

In a new a16z essay, "Defense in Depth, Rebuilt for Machine Speed," Asaf Ezra and Steph Zhang pick up where their previous piece left off. That piece argued that AI has made turning a bug into a working exploit cheap, while patching stayed slow. This one asks what follows: since the gap between exploit and patch will not close by patching harder, the products and policies that matter over the next five years are the ones that protect systems even when they cannot be patched quickly.

The opening example is the October PHP incident. Two critical bugs in the language behind much of the web leaked before their fix, and exploit attempts began four days before public disclosure. Hosting and content-delivery companies blocked the attacks at their edge while the servers underneath stayed unpatched. Between 2.6 and 4.4 million PHP installations outside that shelter remained exposed, most on versions that will never receive a fix. The lesson the authors draw is that protection did not have to wait for the patch: someone else stood between the attacker and the vulnerability.

Patching itself is a per-vulnerability, human-gated process, and the numbers have barely moved. The 2026 Verizon breach report put median full remediation at 43 days and found only 26 percent of known-exploited vulnerabilities fully remediated. Veracode's 2025 report put average flaw remediation at 252 days, up from 171 five years earlier. Discovery, meanwhile, is accelerating: in October, Anthropic reported that an unreleased model produced a remote, no-login exploit for a Linux kernel bug that had been fixed upstream weeks earlier, while two widely used server distributions still had no fixed package. Red Hat had rated the bug moderate because its timing race was hard to win. The model won it in under an hour. And patching faster carries its own risk: in July 2024, one faulty CrowdStrike update crashed about 8.5 million Windows machines.

The case is starkest where updates are slow by design and failure is physical. Industrial controllers are patched in maintenance windows, often annually; Dragos found about a quarter of 2025's industrial advisories shipped with no patch at all, and US grid rules allow 35 days to evaluate a patch and 35 more to apply it. Medical devices live 10 to 20 years, often on unsupported operating systems, and a patch can mean re-certification; one 2025 survey found 89 percent of hospital systems had connected devices with a known-exploited vulnerability. At the far end are the countless chipped devices with no update channel and no owner who knows they are online. In October, attackers heated the refrigeration in hundreds of French supermarkets in one night, mostly through end-of-life controllers, and three weeks later the same attack hit stores in four more countries, where the fix requires physical changes to every unit. For all of these, the authors argue, patching was never really the control. The controls that work are off the device.

They rank four approaches by how far down the long tail each reaches. First, get systems off the open internet: when Censys found nearly 400 water-utility control screens exposed online, 40 of them controllable without a login, it worked through the EPA and the vendor, and within nine days a quarter were secured; by May 2025 fewer than 6 percent remained exposed, with no patch involved. The hard part is not finding exposure, which AI makes nearly free, but reaching owners through someone they answer to, a regulator, vendor, provider, or insurer; one study found a European provider's quarantine-and-notification process cleared 92 percent of infections within 14 days, while email warnings did no better than silence. Second, segment what must stay connected: in May, an intruder using a frontier model as his main operator reached the boundary between a Mexican water utility's office and control networks and stopped there, because the control network was walled off. Third, block attacks in the network at machine speed, generating protective rules automatically from each disclosure, as the big platforms did during the PHP week, and pushing them to industrial networks, hospitals, and small-business routers. Fourth, remove whole classes of bugs: memory-safety flaws fell from 76 percent of Android's vulnerabilities in 2019 to 24 percent in 2024 as new code moved to memory-safe languages. Alongside all four sit safe fallback, so a water plant can run by hand and a hospital on paper, and retirement of devices too old to protect.

Backups get their own warning. In Sophos's 2024 survey, 94 percent of ransomware victims said attackers tried to compromise their backups, and 57 percent of those attempts succeeded. In Veeam's 2026 survey, 90 percent of leaders believed they could meet recovery targets, but only 28 percent of victims got all their data back, and victims whose backups were compromised reported median recovery costs around $3 million against $375,000 where backups survived. Backups, the authors argue, are part of the attack surface, not insurance: the posture that counts is a timed, tested restore, not the claim of having copies.

The structure raises an uncomfortable question about who stands in the middle. Protection today concentrates in a few cloud, content, and security companies; the largest content-delivery network sits in front of a quarter of all websites, up from under 4 percent a decade ago. That concentration is why the PHP week went well, and it is also a single point of failure, as the 2024 outage showed, and a sovereignty question about whose rules govern traffic worldwide. When owners will not or cannot act, the authors note precedent for others stepping in: in 2021 a US court authorized the FBI to remove web shells from hundreds of private Exchange servers, and in late 2023 another order let it remove a Chinese state botnet from hundreds of small-office routers. They expect pressure to grow for a ladder of notify, quarantine, then remediate, and they call it a real civil-liberties trade, better argued now than improvised in a crisis.

The heart of the piece is six market opportunities, each merging old categories into one product and each making patching unnecessary or someone else's job. The first is live fixing: mitigate a vulnerability in the running system within hours, with an edge rule, an in-process hook, or an in-memory patch, while coding agents write the permanent fix against the exact versions deployed, then retire the live fix once the code fix ships. A deeper layer of the same category watches the few choke points every exploit passes through and blocks techniques rather than individual bugs, which matters because of the 48,185 vulnerabilities published in 2025 only 884 were newly exploited, and most exploited weakness types have been preventable for decades. The second opportunity merges the AI security operations center with incident response and managed detection: detection that reads event streams directly off existing products, proposes explainable detections instead of relying on hand-written rules, and responds first, in a world where teams see about 3,000 alerts a day and never reach 63 percent of them. The third merges identity, endpoint, and network access into judging every request rather than every login, because a single laptop now carries a person, a coding agent with its own keys, and a browser agent with its own sessions, all sending requests in the same minute. Access becomes just in time, with credentials scoped to one task and lifetimes measured in minutes.

The fourth opportunity is proving recovery: backup posture management that continuously verifies backups exist, resist alteration, sit outside the identity systems attackers seize first, and actually restore, producing a record a board, auditor, or insurer will accept. The authors note that victims whose backups survived were fully back within a week at nearly twice the rate of those whose backups were compromised, and that the recovery record doubles as the underwriting input insurers need for correlated losses they currently exclude. The fifth is a single control at the point of install, applying the email-gateway model of hold, detonate, and verify provenance to packages, extensions, vendor connections, and the skills and documents AI agents fetch. It is grounded in October's losses: Binance puts its theft at $6.3 billion, traced to one engineer's compromised laptop, and a tampered package reached up to 5,000 developers through an editor's extension marketplace. The sixth is a platform for the long tail itself, bundling exposure management, secure remote access, segmentation, and rehearsed manual fallback for organizations that will never run a security team: the water utility, the hospital's biomedical department, the supermarket chain's refrigeration contractor.

The authors close by repeating the wager from their earlier piece: 2026 looks less like the start of a cyber war than the start of a long, expensive worm era. The last worm era ended when one company took ownership of the problem. Nobody owns this one. For many systems, the window between exploit and patch will stay open, so the work is to make that window matter less: fewer things exposed, fewer operations that depend on an immediate patch, and backups that actually bring systems back.

Open questions

  1. The authors argue protection increasingly comes from a small number of platforms standing between attackers and everyone else, while also calling that concentration a single point of failure and a sovereignty problem. If the long tail has no realistic alternative to sheltering under those platforms, what governance or competition policy keeps the shelter from becoming the risk?
  2. The piece endorses a ladder of intervention, notify, then quarantine, then remediate, for devices whose owners will not act, and calls it a civil-liberties trade. Where should the legal line sit, and who decides that a private device endangers others enough to justify the state touching it?
  3. Several of the six opportunities depend on defenders deploying the same class of AI that made exploitation cheap, from automatically generated protective rules to models judging requests inline. What evidence, beyond the vendors' own trials, should buyers require before trusting machine-speed defenses not to become the next faulty update that takes systems down?

Charts of the Week: Apple Skips Capex, and the Crowd Goes Wild (Sort Of)

Looking at what drove Nasdaq returns over the last 30 days, the downward drag has come from AI and AI-adjacent companies: hyperscalers, neoclouds, chipmakers, and the AI supply chain. The smaller upward pull has come from household names outside tech, with Costco and Walmart among the biggest contributors.

Two names bucked the trend. Tesla is one, mostly a partial rebound in an otherwise weak year. Apple is the other. The market, he writes, is plainly rewarding Apple for investing almost nothing in AI.

The restraint has not hurt the core business so far. iPhone revenue growth has rebounded substantially, and services keeps compounding at about 13 percent year over year, despite fears that costlier components would push device prices up and demand down. Two games made almost entirely with AI reached its top 20 by concurrent players. Its new prompt-to-game tool, Build, has drawn more than 500,000 users in its four alpha countries, who have published over 70,000 games.

Two Minute Papers on the future of AI with Spencer Huang

The Future of AI Is Absolutely Incredible (Spencer Huang)

On Two Minute Papers, the episode "The Future of AI Is Absolutely Incredible (Spencer Huang)" is a long conversation between host Károly Zsolnai-Fehér and Spencer Huang, who works on NVIDIA's robotics platform. It is the rare interview that covers the whole stack of physical AI at once: the physics solvers, the training data, the simulators, the small models that keep a robot upright, and the question of what robots could do if training ran a million times faster.

It opens with product news. NVIDIA has updated Isaac Lab with the Newton physics framework, published blueprints for training robot policies on DGX Station workstations, and built a reference humanoid platform around the Agile One robot. That platform ships with Wuji dexterous hands, Jetson Thor integration, and the GR00T software stack ready out of the box, because getting a new robot's hands and software working can otherwise eat weeks or months. The theme Huang returns to all hour is simple: the less friction, the better, because the goal is to get more work done.

Why is physical AI so much harder than language models? Language models inherited a gift: humans have spent centuries writing down, photographing, and recording the world, so the training data already existed. Physical AI has no equivalent archive. Nobody has recorded how the world pushes back when a robot acts on it, and that pushback, the bidirectional coupling between a hand and the object it squeezes, is exactly the part that matters. A robot policy needs physics solvers that can emulate reality and data that captures real dynamics, and both are scarce.

Collecting that data is painful. The starting point is teleoperation, a human driving the robot so its actions can be recorded, but teleoperation has no force feedback. Huang compares it to working with numb hands, while also having to imagine what the robot sees from off to the side. Simple assembly becomes a huge undertaking. Newer routes help: egocentric video of human hands at work, and pose and object-interaction data extracted from footage of people. But real data stays limited and expensive, while simulated data is plentiful and uneven in quality. Training on the two together, co-training, is still something of an art.

Zsolnai-Fehér asks for the ideal data diet, and the answer is a hybrid. Neural simulators, the world models, are trained on observed footage rather than grounded physics, so they hallucinate physics: pick up one orange, drop it, and it multiplies into four. What they offer is diversity and variety. Conventional simulators never hallucinate, but building their environments by hand is tedious, and frontier coding models have only recently begun to shrink that bottleneck.

The combined recipe works like this. Train the policy inside conventional physics across a wide envelope of physical properties, so it learns how tables in general behave rather than one exact table. Then pass the plain simulation renders through a world model that randomizes materials, lighting, and deformations, and feed those richer observations back into the policy. World models are also being taught to output touch, force feedback, and acoustics, not just pixels. And the loop runs both ways: grounded physics data from conventional simulators and the real world trains neural simulators such as Cosmos to simulate more faithfully, one family of dynamics at a time. Ethernet cables are not shirt threads, shirt threads are not spaghetti, and spaghetti is not rope.

The conversation then turns to intelligence density. A robotics result called Sonic squeezes whole-body control into 42 million parameters, small enough to be a rounding error by modern standards, and it is open weights. Uploaded into a robot, it walks, crawls, and dances, and it works across different body shapes. The trick is scope: Sonic solves one subset of the problem, whole-body control, rather than everything. Whole-body control matters because useful tasks are full-body tasks. To lift a heavy box properly you articulate your legs, back, and arms together. Early systems split the body in two and could not walk and carry a coffee at the same time. Sonic is the fast, reactive layer, system zero, and it pairs with a vision-language-action model such as GR00T, the system one and two layers that reason over longer horizons.

Cosmos, NVIDIA's world model, is now being used in three ways. It generates data and performs domain randomization. It serves as the simulator itself, an environment in which a trained policy can be evaluated. And it is becoming the backbone of world action models, where the world model is the policy. For evaluation, Isaac Lab Arena decomposes a task like grabbing a bottle into subtasks, scores each one, and runs a sensitivity analysis over factors like collisions and lighting to find exactly where a policy starts to fail. That enables failure-based learning: fail, analyze why, retrain, the way humans improve.

This matters because simulation lies. Huang's phrase is that you are doomed to succeed in simulation: anything can be tuned to work there, with no guarantee it transfers. Simulation is in fact used more for evaluation than for core training, because conventional simulators cover only a subset of dynamics well, mostly rigid bodies. The sim-to-real gap is not a single step but the whole loop, from how data is collected, through generation and training, to deployment and re-evaluation. Teleoperated data has one quiet advantage: it is already mapped to the exact robot that will be deployed. Human data must be retargeted onto a different skeleton, with joints in different places. Huang's hope for the next phase is to train one skill across many robot bodies at once, instead of many skills on one body, and that requires knowing how much a given simulator can be trusted.

Robots, it turns out, get injured like athletes. Zsolnai-Fehér recalls DeepMind's soccer robots wearing out their knees from kicking, a problem eased by training policies to go easier on the joint. Huang adds that adaptation can be emergent. One company's robot dog, its knee locked, learned on its own to walk on three legs and hobble on the fourth. At a partner site, a humanoid seemed to walk oddly, and inspection found a slightly broken ankle that the policy had already silently adapted to.

Then the two dream. Huang frames reinforcement learning as lived experience: a human gets one second of experience per second, but a robot policy training faster than real time can accumulate the equivalent of a century of practice in seconds. A million-fold speedup applied to small systems matters as much as to large ones. It would let robots learn on the device, in the field, continually, instead of shipping data to the cloud for batch training, and eventually let a robot simulate inside its own head before acting, the way an athlete visualizes a performance before the event.

Some of this is already visible at NVIDIA headquarters, where a bipedal robot really does hand out popcorn in the lab, a delivery job that used to defeat wheeled robots at the stairs. Huang says problem horizons have compressed: work that looked ten years away now looks three to five years out. The bottlenecks are whether the hardware can enact what the policies learn, and whether there is enough compute to train them.

What remains easy for humans and hard for machines is the familiar paradox: in-hand manipulation, assembling Lego, micro-assembly with a screwdriver. These tasks are simple to describe and brutal to execute. They demand data or simulation that captures the behavior, a policy with tool skills and sub-millimeter control, and hardware with the precision to match. High-precision automation has existed for decades, but general dexterity is only now arriving.

Finally, Zsolnai-Fehér asks whether AI will ever be good enough that nobody reaches for the frontier model. Huang splits the question. A strong baseline model will cover most everyday problems, his analogy is the master's degree that serves most people well. But frontier intelligence, the PhD, will always be pursued, because humans keep inventing new work at the edge of what automation makes possible. For physical AI, the field is still just reaching the baseline.

Open questions

  1. Huang argues simulation is most trustworthy as an evaluation tool, yet the field is moving toward evaluating policies inside neural simulators that can themselves hallucinate physics. Who grades the grader, and what would a shared, quantitative measure of a simulator's trustworthiness look like?
  2. His stated hope is to train one skill across many robot bodies at once rather than many skills on one body. Which breaks first in that plan: retargeting human data onto new skeletons, physics fidelity across morphologies, or the economics of collecting data for each new embodiment?
  3. If a large speedup makes on-robot continual learning practical, a deployed fleet's policies will quietly diverge as each unit learns from its own experience and injuries. How would anyone keep quality control, safety review, and reproducibility over a fleet that no longer shares one policy?

Y Combinator · Blake Scholl on supersonic flight

Bringing Supersonic Flight To The People

On Y Combinator, the Social Radars episode "Bringing Supersonic Flight To The People" brings back Blake Scholl, the founder and chief executive of Boom Supersonic, for a second conversation with hosts Jessica Livingston and Carolynn Levy. The first episode ended on a cliffhanger, with Rolls-Royce walking away from building Boom's engine. This one tells what happened next, and how the worst moment in the company's life turned into the business model that may finally pay for a supersonic airliner.

Scholl calls outsourcing the engine the most disastrous decision he ever made. Boom's first employee was an engine designer, and the original plan was to build in house, but a Rolls-Royce engine looked adaptable, and the industry expected a startup to name one of the big three suppliers. The deal nearly closed. At a Rolls-Royce board meeting in early 2022, where the Boom contract was supposed to be approved, the board fired the chief executive but left him in his seat until a replacement could be found, which meant nobody could make a decision and every executive was auditioning for the top job. A handshake deal kept the relationship alive through the end of the year.

Then came the public breakup. Boom had a triumphant Farnborough Air Show in July 2022, and in August American Airlines followed United with a pre-order. Instead of helping, the momentum panicked Rolls-Royce, which ran to the press and ended the partnership without warning Scholl. Only later did he understand why: the engine Rolls-Royce had proposed rested on a next generation platform the company had quietly stopped developing, so it had promised something it could not deliver. Boom became instantly unfundable. No partner or airline would take a meeting, and reporters ambushed Scholl at conferences, microphones in his face, asking what he was doing about engines.

The way out was to build the engine themselves. Pratt and Whitney had closed its West Palm Beach office, home of its supersonic team, to save money on real estate, and the engineers quit rather than move to Hartford and formed a contract engineering firm. Boom found them. Confidence came from an unlikely place: a Brian Chesky talk at a Masters of Scale conference, arguing that in a crisis the instinct to compromise is wrong, and a company has to become more of what it was always supposed to be. Scholl heard it as an instruction. By the end of 2022 Boom had announced its own engine program with the Florida team, a small additive manufacturing role for GE, and a maintenance partner.

Owning the engine unlocked the physics. A sonic boom can be made to bend upward in the sky and never reach the ground, but only if the airplane cruises at high altitude, relatively close to the speed of sound, which demands strong transonic performance from the engine. The adapted Rolls-Royce engine could not do it, so Boom had shelved boomless cruise entirely. Optimizing their own engine changed the numbers meeting by meeting, and when the team finally ran a boom forecast on a Saturday before the XB-1 test airplane's first supersonic flight in early 2025, a ninety minute simulation session showed they had quietly solved the boom problem about six months earlier.

The second payoff solved the problem Scholl had wrestled with for a decade: how a startup with no revenue product finances an airliner. His engine team had noticed back in 2022 that a supersonic engine resembles a power turbine, since both run at continuous full power in a demanding, high temperature environment. In March 2025, with AI data centers short of power and gas turbines backlogged for years, Scholl texted Sam Altman, a day one Boom investor, to ask whether he would want a forty megawatt natural gas turbine. Altman's answer, in Scholl's telling, was please, as many as you can make, as fast as you can make them. His chief engineer, it turned out, already had a plan drawn up, but had assumed there was no market.

The conversion is simple in outline. Take the fan off the front of the engine and connect the core to an electric generator through an added turbine stage, and a core built to make forty thousand pounds of thrust produces forty two megawatts, close to the ideal building block size for data centers. Twenty four units gang together into roughly a gigawatt, deployed with spares the way blade servers fill a rack. Boom had accidentally spent three years developing exactly the product AI needed. Scholl says the company raised money on the strategy at the end of 2025, expects turbine revenue next year, and plans for the high margin turbine business to fund the airliner outright, ending years of living paycheck to paycheck.

That scarcity, he argues, was never really about hard tech costing more. Uber and Airbnb consumed more venture capital than SpaceX or Anduril. The difference is that a software company proves demand by shipping, while a supersonic jet cannot be test marketed, so far more capital is needed before the proof point rather than in total. Investors pattern match, and the last entrepreneur founded commercial aircraft company before Boom was Douglas Aircraft in 1921. Being starved of capital also made Boom worse: nobody makes long term decisions on a short runway.

Scholl widens the lens to American stagnation. Energy production per person rose exponentially until 1971 and then flatlined, as nuclear froze, environmental law like NEPA made infrastructure hard to build, and incumbents lost any reason to grow. The turbine giants are the same companies as the jet engine giants, and their mid sized state of the art, GE's LM6000, derives from a 747 engine from 1971. People hired away from GE tell him their former colleagues take bets on when Boom will fail. Boeing, meanwhile, has designed no new airplane since 2004, a decline he traces through the McDonnell Douglas acquisition, Jack Welch style financial metrics, and a chief executive who declared an end to moonshots.

His explanation is that aviation lost both of its engines of innovation. Commercial progress had always been driven by speed, and the United States banned supersonic flight over land in 1973, in effect banning the innovation itself. Defense progress was driven by urgent need, which vanished after the Cold War and turned programs into jobs programs. A whole generation of talented engineers, Scholl's own, chose software instead. Concorde and Apollo, he notes, were Cold War government glory projects with no economics underneath, which is why both died. SpaceX revived spaceflight on commercial terms, and Boom intends to do the same for supersonic flight.

Owning both the engine and the airplane is already changing the product. A redesigned cabin cost a thousand miles of range on a four thousand mile airplane, and Scholl rallied the company with Apple's Purple One and Purple Two story, the safe iPod phone against the risky shrunken iPad that became the iPhone. The team designed a dozen buildable engines, ran each through simulation software Boom built that wraps an airplane around an engine and flies it virtually, and found a clear winner that won back most of the range. The results include no middle seats anywhere, a goal of boarding and emptying the whole airplane in five minutes each, and built in Starlink, since this will be the first airliner designed after Wi-Fi existed rather than wearing it as a bolted on hump.

He closes on the industrial base. Keeping defense primes alive with small projects, he argues, produced companies that cannot move, and he points to this year's reports of interceptor shortages as the predictable result. In the Second World War, a strong commercial manufacturing base retooled almost overnight. Boom is built to be that kind of company: digital engineering and manufacturing, few hard tools, and a goal of going from a design idea to a finished high performance turbine part in twenty four hours, so in a crisis it could stop making airliners and power turbines and make whatever the country needs. With about a quarter of a recent YC batch working on hard tech and a new generation of aerospace students he calls awesome, he thinks the missing generation is finally being replaced.

Open questions

The founding bet is about scale. Agrawal argues that agents will use the web a thousand times more than humans ever did, because human use is capped by time and attention while agent use is capped only by how much useful, economically valuable work exists and what someone will pay for it. Set against the ten billion plus searches a day that Google already serves, a thousandfold increase cannot run on today's systems. It needs, he says, multiple generations of new technology.

Agents need the web, in his telling, because models only appear to know everything. A model can recite a Wikipedia fact yet cannot say who won a football game last weekend, information a person could find in a minute. That gap breaks the illusion the moment someone builds a real product on a model, so nearly every agent product ends up needing live access to the web to do basic things.

A search engine built for agents then looks nothing like one built for people. Human searchers type two or three underspecified keywords, skim ten blue links, and abandon any page that loads slowly or buries the answer. Ranking for humans means guessing intent and rewarding speed and placement. An agent, by contrast, can be handed the single relevant paragraph from page 77 of an SEC filing, the most authoritative source rather than the most readable one, with clear attribution. Because his team understands how the model is trained, it can set up flywheels, building features designed to highlight what the next model release will be good at. The surface keeps moving too: with GPT-6 and its intelligent interface, a chat answer can now come back as a widget, such as a flight card, rather than plain text.

Usage has already moved well past coding. People use Codex for absolutely everything now, he says, and describes his own routine as an example. After posting a vague promise to make the app better and asking users for complaints, he hands the feedback thread to his named agent, which runs Codex in the background, and asks it to save the complaints, group them into themes, and sort them by impact. By the end of the day he has a document in a shared space with some 300 bullet points, grouped and sorted, ready to work from.

That example feeds his answer on cost. Asked about reports that Apple is capping how much employees can spend on AI tools, he says he is surprised people are surprised: there was never a world in which every employee got an unlimited token budget. Efficiency is the company's standing focus, and shared spaces are part of the answer, because once one person's agent has answered a question in a space, a teammate's agent can find that answer instead of paying to generate it again. Product choices help as well, through modes and the reasoning selector, though he concedes many users do not want to choose models at all. They want the right one chosen for them.

He closes with an argument about how people learn these tools, built on an analogy to skeuomorphism. When the iPhone launched in 2007, its apps imitated leather, paper, and glass, familiar materials that guided people from one world into another. Cursors play the same role today, he argues: computer use and browser use do not need to show a cursor for the agent's sake, but showing one makes the work easy to follow. Named agents, the dots his team ships, do similar work. Embodying an agent as something like a coworker, proactive and able to remind you of things, helps people grasp what they can ask it to do. His line on the familiar criticism of that approach is a distinction: with the dots, OpenAI is embodying its agents, not personifying them, and the team will keep iterating on where that line sits.

Open questions

  1. If scaffolding features like goal mode are designed to be retired as models improve, how does a product team know when a model has truly absorbed one, and what happens to the users who built their workflows around the version that disappears?
  2. Ambrosino's cost answer leans on efficiency gains and shared spaces cutting duplicated work, but if agent use keeps growing faster than efficiency improves, where should a company draw its budget line, and who inside it should draw it?
  3. The distinction between embodying an agent and personifying it is doing a lot of work in his argument. As named agents get more capable and more proactive, can that line hold in practice, or will users treat a coworker-like agent as a person no matter what its designers intend?

Inside the Race to Build and Sell AI Agents

The Information, on its TITV show, sits down with Dan Shipper, the chief executive and founder of Every, for "Inside the Race to Build and Sell AI Agents." The interview was recorded in front of a live audience of about 400 people, and it ranges from how companies are mismanaging their AI rollouts to what today's models still cannot do, and from a rapid tour of the big labs to the economics of the agent Shipper's own company sells.

Shipper's first subject is the pressure inside big companies. Everyone at the event loves AI, he says, and employees are under heavy pressure from management to use it as much as possible. The problem is that executives who do not have their hands in the technology themselves can only follow the headlines, so management swings between token maxing, forcing people to spend as many tokens as possible, and token minning, capping budgets, and neither works on its own. Forcing spend on an organization that is not set up for it just burns tokens on work that does not matter, and expectations for how fast a good project can move are, in his phrase, out of whack with reality. The right start is the basics, done slowly: go slow to go fast.

The flip side is that the answer is not simply restraint. If an organization is set up to use AI well and unleashes the people who are genuinely good at it, Shipper argues, it probably should be token maxing, because with a good setup, more tokens spent means more high quality output. There is no headline rule that substitutes for a decision maker who uses the tools personally.

Asked what the models are still not good at, after three or four years of running his regular vibe checks on new releases, Shipper names coherence. Models often leak their own context into the artifact they are making: ask one to fix a landing page headline and it may write copy that announces what the page will not talk about, putting the editing conversation into the deliverable itself. His broader category is that a model often lacks an accurate sense of what it knows, what the user knows, and what belongs in the thing being made together, and even the smartest models break that coherence in basic ways while simultaneously solving far harder problems.

The tour of the labs is quick. Meta, he says, is prioritizing the consumer: shopping and everyday interactions like keeping up with a sports team. Anthropic is building power creative tools, aimed at professionals rather than generic enterprise work. OpenAI is aimed at the general knowledge worker, and its ChatGPT harness is extremely good for non-coders. Grok occupies a different niche: it will answer questions the other models refuse, such as how to dose peptides, which Shipper treats as a real, if uncomfortable, differentiator.

His dark horse is Google. A Google model he calls Argon is not a good coder, he says, but it is the best he has used at structuring nonfiction writing, so that one paragraph sets up the next and an argument actually builds. Claude is better at matching a particular person's voice and style, but for laying out a series of thoughts, Argon stands apart, and it feels trained on something different from the data mix the other frontier models share. His only theory is an advantage of incumbency: Google has spent twenty years organizing the world's information, and that shows up in the writing.

On economics, Shipper reads the market as deflationary. Model prices have fallen dramatically over time, and even when headline tiers held steady, the number of tokens included kept rising; recent releases, around the Opus 5.5 generation, cut prices by roughly half. The labs are heavily subsidizing usage, a point raised by Elon Musk's complaint that Grok is losing too much money, but training and inference keep getting more efficient. Shipper's expectation is a split market: a category of personal agents people will not want to pay for, much as they do not pay for Instagram, alongside professional agents that people already pay for gladly.

That split frames his own product. The Every agent is a coworker that lives in Slack and takes delegated writing and coding tasks, packaging the workflows Every's team develops by constantly testing new models at the edge. Working inside Slack is deliberate: capable users struggle to convert their colleagues, and doing the work where the team can see it brings people along without a lecture. The agent is profitable, he says, because of how it is priced. Customers pay the market rate for the tokens they use, with no markup, and pay Every a subscription instead. A startup cannot subsidize tokens the way the labs do, since even 50 million dollars of funding runs out fast, and the absence of a token markup aligns the company with its customers: Every profits by finding the best model for a job at the lowest price and making usage more efficient, never by keeping tokens flowing.

Open questions

  1. If the labs keep subsidizing usage while prices fall, who ultimately pays for the free tier of personal agents, and do advertising or data models arrive to fill the gap?
  2. Shipper treats coherence, a model knowing what belongs in the artifact and what belongs in the conversation, as the frontier weakness. Which lab fixes that first, and how would anyone measure the fix?
  3. Does Every's pass-through token pricing, with profit taken as a subscription instead, become the standard model for agent startups, or do the labs' subsidized agents undercut it before it can spread?
