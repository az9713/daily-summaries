MTS · Melvin Johnson of Google DeepMind on closed labs and open AI

Closed Frontier Labs Are Pulling Ahead of Open AI | Melvin Johnson

On MTS, “Closed Frontier Labs Are Pulling Ahead of Open AI | Melvin Johnson” is a conversation with Melvin Johnson, vice president of research at Google DeepMind, where he leads Gemini post-training. The occasion is a launch: Google is releasing Hydrogen, the newest model in its Gemini 4 family, and Johnson's central claim gives the episode its title. The gap between closed frontier models and open weights models, he says, is the largest it has been all year, and open models are stuck playing catch-up.

The Gemini 4 family has arrived quickly. Argon came first, as the family's first frontier model. Carbon followed within the same month, with what Johnson calls a massive jump in capability and better behavior, and it became generally available earlier this week. Hydrogen, launching today, is meant to be the daily driver and workhorse: much faster, while still delivering cutting-edge capability, and Google wants it rolled out to as many users and surfaces as possible. More models are coming in the Gemini 4 era, he says.

The surprise is that the workhorse beats the flagship. On the AA index, Argon scores 50, while Hydrogen scores 51 at its default high setting and 54 at extra high, which makes the newer, faster model stronger than the frontier model Google released a month and a half ago. That, Johnson says, is why Hydrogen beating both Sonnet and Terra at Vending Bench is not surprising to the team.

Asked what it will take for Google to durably stay in the lead after a long stretch behind Anthropic and OpenAI, Johnson points to advantages he treats as structural. Google is vertically integrated, planning training and inference around its own TPU chips on a fast chip cycle. More than 100,000 Google engineers use the models every day and give feedback in the building, which cuts out the slow loop of testing with outside customers and translating their feedback back. And Google has distribution: the Gemini app is a billion-plus product, AI Mode and AI Overviews serve Search users, and Antigravity is growing quickly as a coding surface.

AI Overviews gets a special mention. Johnson agrees it is seemingly the most used AI product in the world, and says he tells his researchers they are building the most used AI model in the world by a big margin without realizing it. The frontier gets the attention, he notes, but only a small sliver of people have access to it, while Google distributes its models to more than 7 billion people.

The title claim comes in answer to the standard open weights argument, that open models will erode frontier labs' margins by offering nearly the same capability for substantially less. Johnson's reply is wait and see. Hydrogen currently sits above most open weights models, if not all of them, and the frontier labs will not stop at Hydrogen: the next element and the next frontier model are already coming, so open models face a permanent catch-up game. The gap did narrow at points earlier this year, he concedes, but right now it is quite large. The element names themselves, he explains, began as internal candidate names that the team liked enough to externalize.

For enterprise use, the pitch is cost and speed at near-frontier quality. Johnson says early customers tested Hydrogen positively for exactly that trade-off: it is the best model in its class on Automation Bench, and state of the art on Tax Bench and a legal research bench, which makes it strong at knowledge work. Inside Google, the team already uses it for a lot of everyday coding and likes where it sits on the speed and quality frontier. Asked whether it has been run on GDPX, he says that is coming soon, and the host says he is especially excited to see where it lands.

On what comes next from Google DeepMind, Johnson pleads that products are the wrong department to ask, then points to the flagship surfaces anyway. The Gemini app already has a chat and task toggle, and the task side is getting massive improvements soon. AI Mode is going to become more and more agentic, so a question asked in Search can transition seamlessly into buying something or getting something done. The consumer agentic era, he says, is where Google's products will show lots of improvement.

Open questions

  1. Johnson says the closed-versus-open gap is the largest it has been all year, after narrowing at points earlier in the year. What would have to change, in open models' release cadence or in the frontier labs' pricing, for the gap to start closing again instead of widening?
  2. His durability argument rests on Google's chips, its hundred thousand internal users, and its distribution to billions of people. Which of those three advantages is actually doing the work, and could a rival with only one of them match Google anyway?
  3. Hydrogen, the fast workhorse, now outscores Argon, the frontier flagship released weeks earlier. If the cheaper model keeps overtaking the flagship this quickly, what is the flagship tier for?

Sourcery · Christian Garrett on the return of conglomerates

SpaceX, Palantir, Anduril & Onodrim | The Return of Conglomerates

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, "SpaceX, Palantir, Anduril & Onodrim | The Return of Conglomerates" is a conversation with Christian Garrett, a partner at 137 Ventures and a co-founder of the European defense platform Onodrim. Garrett's firm has spent sixteen years as an investor in SpaceX, and the talk moves from that position to the idea he says he has become obsessed with: the conglomerate is coming back, in a form different from the one that fell apart in the 1980s.

This, he says, has been the year of SpaceX. The company, now public, grew its top line about 160 percent in the third quarter, posted 4.4 billion dollars of net income and 8.8 billion dollars of EBITDA, and is worth roughly 2 trillion dollars. More than half of its revenue is now attributed to AI in some capacity, which raises the question of what its market comparisons even are. Garrett's answer is that SpaceX is becoming a hyperscaler, and that its earnings multiple, somewhere around twenty to thirty times forward estimates on his reading of consensus, prices a company that dominates several critical industries at once.

The AI side of SpaceX is a compute business. The company builds infrastructure faster than anyone, rents capacity out, and uses it for its own products, from Cursor and Grok to its robots. Garrett points to payback periods under twelve months on that capacity as the signal that demand still outruns supply, and he argues the vertically integrated players are the safest place to invest in the build-out: a company that monetizes at the application layer, the model layer, and the compute layer benefits whether open or closed models win, and if demand slows it can shift capacity to its own products. Because the build cycles are short, the capital at risk turns over quickly too.

The older businesses are not standing still. Starship is flying payloads, the V3 Starlink satellites are going up, and a single launch carrying a handful of them delivered on the order of 1 percent of the world's capacity, by his telling. Starlink's direct-to-cell business is compounding, and the revenue mix will keep shifting over decades. The next frontier is compute in orbit: SpaceX has said publicly it aims to launch its first orbital compute satellites in the back half of next year, Google has already tested hardware in orbit, and startups like Starcloud are building for the same market. Garrett's logic is that the binding constraints on terrestrial data centers are power and land, and that SpaceX's cost curve in launch is what unlocks the alternative.

On the wider market, he is candid that pricing is hard. Debt finances much of the build-out, so cost of capital is decisive, and he agrees with Elon Musk that a few companies hold a structural advantage there while others face fair questions about whether revenue will support their spending commitments. Volatility, he argues, does not settle anything about the underlying businesses, pointing out that ordinary people in his own family now use ChatGPT, agents, and Grok bots daily. His own discipline at 137 is to underwrite duration rather than any single year's growth: SpaceX compounded at roughly 40 percent for twenty years, and it is the length of that run, not any one year, that made it exceptional.

That discipline also means paying up. Citing his godfather Teddy Forstmann, Garrett says you would rather own a quality asset at a 25 percent premium than a poor one at a 25 percent discount. Markets today, he argues, mix genuine growth with too much capital chasing too few growth-stage companies, and the power law resolves it: a handful of companies drive all the returns. Fintech looked overfunded a few years ago and still produced Stripe, Revolut, and Ramp. The marks around this portfolio tell the same story, with Cognition recently valued at 48 billion dollars, Anduril at 90 billion, and Ramp at 60 billion.

The conglomerate thesis itself turns on what has changed since the 1980s. The old conglomerates held unrelated subsidiaries, gained diversification and cheap capital, but had no shared operating platform, so the parts were hard to value and the weak segments dragged on the strong ones. Today's versions, he argues, are built differently: the businesses share a common operating technology platform and are often vertically integrated, so there are real synergies instead of just a holding company discount. Apple is one example. Anduril is his defense example, a multi-product company whose Lattice software powers everything it sells, so each additional product makes the others more valuable. Anduril building its own computers is, for him, another instance of capacity being repatriated by a company that sees a commercial opportunity, something Palmer Luckey has argued for over many years.

His stronger claim is about dual use. The defense primes of China draw 68 percent of their revenue from the commercial sector, a figure he notes Palantir's Shyam Sankar cites often, and General Mills once made both cereal and weapons systems. Palantir and SpaceX, he says, are the only American examples of the form today: companies whose commercial scale subsidizes and sustains defense capacity that can throttle up when needed. Industrial policy alone, he argues, cannot pay for the capacity the West needs, so more companies will have to be dominant commercially first and cross into defense, as enterprise software and cybersecurity firms already have.

Onodrim is his attempt to build the European version of the layer underneath. Co-founded with Isis and Alex, veterans of Palantir's European business, it is a platform that acquires aerospace and defense supplier businesses across Europe, deploys technology into them, and holds them for the long term as partners of existing management. The United States has long had supplier consolidators such as TransDigm, Teledyne, and HEICO; Europe, whose defense market was not growing, never built one, even though its Airbus supply chain and industrial base are deep. Now Europe is rearming, and Garrett argues the bottleneck is production at the supplier level: components, engines, structures, and manufacturing capacity, in a fragmented market of hundreds of thousands of small firms. Against roughly 4,000 European drone companies chasing the prime layer, the layer below is the underappreciated one. Onodrim looks for durable revenue, low churn, critical capacity, and management that knows its category, and its first acquisitions and partnerships are to be announced soon. Garrett describes himself as a minority co-founder, closer to a deeply involved investor than an operator.

He closes with two cultural worries. Technology, he says, is unusual among industries in not being zero-sum, partly because it keeps growing and partly by culture, and the petty fighting now visible inside the industry puts that at risk. And he argues that the growing political weaponization of companies, from breakup campaigns to boycotts aimed at founders' politics, is a dangerous precedent on both sides: these firms are nonpartisan employers, critical to national competitiveness, and the market's own feedback, customers simply not buying, is the right mechanism. Co-founding a company, he adds, has made him a better investor, not least by teaching him which things investors do that founders find annoying.

Open questions

  1. Garrett's dual-use argument is that commercial scale is what pays for defense capacity in peacetime. If commercial demand in launch or AI compute softens sharply, does that subsidy logic still hold, or do the new dual-use conglomerates end up as exposed as the old defense primes were?
  2. Onodrim's bet is that Europe's fragmented supplier base can be consolidated into pan-European champions even though Europe is not a single market and national champions have been the default. What has to go right, politically and operationally, for consolidation to beat the national model?
  3. He says the new conglomerates escape the old conglomerate discount because their businesses share one operating platform. What evidence, in margins, cross-selling, or product development speed, would actually prove that synergy in public numbers rather than in the story?

Big Think · Adam Frank on what makes life different

What makes life different from everything else in the universe | Adam Frank: Full Interview

On Big Think, "What makes life different from everything else in the universe | Adam Frank: Full Interview" is a full-length conversation with Adam Frank, a professor of physics and astronomy at the University of Rochester, an astrobiologist and astrophysicist, and the author of The Little Book of Aliens. The interview runs in four chapters: why life is different from non-life, a different way of understanding quantum mechanics, what he calls the blind spot at the heart of modern science, and the search for life elsewhere in the universe.

Frank opens with a deliberately radical claim. The most important problem in physics for the twenty-first century, he says, is not quantum gravity or black holes. It is life: understanding why a cell is different from a rock. That question belongs to the young science of complex systems, and life sits at its center.

The first target is the idea that life is a machine. Frank traces the metaphor back to Descartes and calls its modern form, the person as a meat computer, fundamentally wrong. An organism is self-organized, and that puts it in an entirely different class of physical thing. Black holes and comets are impressive, he says, but life is the only system that innovates and the only system that creates.

He uses the brain in a vat thought experiment to make the point. Following the philosopher Evan Thompson, Frank argues there is no such thing as the brain on its own: the brain needs the body as much as the body needs the brain, down to the subtle movements that keep neural processes working. To keep a brain alive in a vat you would have to build it a body so complex that you would end up with a biological body again.

A thermostat, by contrast, only looks goal-directed. Someone designed it, and its goal belongs to the designer. A dog that gets up and moves out of the heat acts on its own urgencies. Living systems, Frank says, are autonomous agents: they choose, maintain, and update their own goals, and they are constantly making sense of their world. Consciousness, in this enactive view, is not a property a system has but something it does.

That sense-making has a name in cognitive science: relevance realization. Walking through a party, you revise what matters moment by moment, a drink, a person you meant to catch, a book on the table. Machines cannot do this, Frank argues, because they live in small worlds of predefined inputs, outputs, and rewards set from outside. Living systems live in large worlds where unknown unknowns exist, and they find what is relevant on the fly, for their own reasons.

Life, he says, is also the only system that uses information, storing, processing, copying, and transferring it. But the information of computers is Shannon information, and Claude Shannon stated in his 1948 paper that meaning was not his problem: his theory covers the syntax of a message, not what it means. Life's information is all meaning and relevance, aimed at self-chosen goals. What is needed is a theory of semantic information, and Frank describes a 2018 manifesto paper by Artemy Kolchinsky and David Wolpert of the Santa Fe Institute and his own group's follow-up work, which produced a mathematical formulation tying the information an agent exchanges with its environment to that information's relevance for staying alive.

The organizing idea underneath all of this is autopoiesis, or self-creation, from the Chilean biologists Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela. A cell is both a process and the product of its processes: the membrane lets the cell exist, and the cell's activity keeps the membrane working. The theoretical biologist Robert Rosen called this organizational closure. A cell is open materially, since matter and energy flow through it and your body's atoms are steadily replaced, but closed organizationally. That is why, Frank says, you can turn a machine off and on again, while a living system can be turned off but never turned back on. The real mystery, he argues, is not consciousness but sentience, and it goes all the way down to the cell.

Rosen also argued, using category theory, that organizationally closed systems may not be Turing computable, which would mean living systems cannot in principle be simulated. Frank calls the proof contentious and says his colleagues are beginning to probe it, but the question stands: living systems might be fundamentally different from anything a computer does, because in life the whole assembles the parts and the parts sustain the whole, and that circularity cannot be broken.

That view shapes his verdict on artificial intelligence. Large language models, he says, are not artificial intelligences but artificial mimics: brilliant, hollow shells that follow paths through everything they were fed. Intelligence is not just solving a problem, it is knowing which problem to solve, and a machine never chooses its own problem. The danger is not a Skynet awakening. It is a leveling of humanity, as we are forced down to the level of incomplete systems, the way building self-driving cars by flattening Paris would destroy what made Paris work. He offers three rules for sitting down with a mimic: make it refer to itself as "the system" rather than in the first person, make it stop praising you, and never say please or thank you to it.

The second chapter turns to quantum mechanics, our most powerful theory and, after some 120 years, one nobody understands. Frank reduces the machinery to two pieces. The Schrödinger equation is the dynamical law, but unlike Newton's equations it does not give one path; it gives many possibilities at once, held in the wave function. The Born rule then turns those possibilities into probabilities for what a measurement will find. That is the measurement problem: in Newton and Maxwell, the measurer never appears in the theory, while quantum mechanics puts measurement, and so measurers, at the center.

Most interpretations, he argues, are attempts to get away from the Born rule and make dynamical equations supreme again. The many worlds interpretation keeps the wave function fully real and pays for it with an essentially infinite number of unobservable universes, rather than accept the message Frank hears in the theory: that we matter to how the world takes on its properties.

His preferred alternative is QBism, quantum Bayesianism, developed by Chris Fuchs and collaborators. Following Thomas Bayes, it treats probabilities as personal: the wave function is not the electron's description of itself, it is your description of the electron. Agents poke the world with experiments and the world pokes back with results, and the quantum formalism is the user's guide for that exchange. It is, in John Wheeler's phrase, a participatory universe. Frank notes the view grows out of quantum information theory, and he repeats his friend Andrew Jordan's test for any interpretation: it earns its keep by suggesting new experiments.

The third chapter names the blind spot. Science is usually sold as a God's eye view, a view from nowhere, independent of any perspective. Frank says there is no such thing as a perspectiveless perspective: imagining the universe before anyone existed still quietly places you there, looking. With Evan Thompson and the physicist Marcelo Gleiser, he wrote a book, The Blind Spot, unpacking the philosophy bundled into that story: reductionism, physicalism, the idea of a perfect observer-independent frame, Platonism about mathematics, and epiphenomenalism, which treats experience as an unimportant byproduct. Reduction is a fine method, he stresses. The trouble starts when a method hardens into metaphysics.

The blind spot universe, he says, is a dead one: atoms careening meaninglessly, with no color or sound except as reconstructed inside heads, a universe in which a god tracking every particle could not tell which systems were alive. Historically the stance made sense. From Newton through the ever more abstract Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations, science climbed a spiral of abstraction and began mistaking its equations for the world. Edmund Husserl called direct experience the lifeworld, the world we are already embedded in each morning, and called the mistake the surreptitious substitution: taking a method for something more real than experience. Frank's example is temperature, which began as the felt difference between hot and cold and ended, through thermometry and statistical mechanics, with degrees that seem more real than the freezing feeling the wind chill produces. Alfred North Whitehead named the same error the fallacy of misplaced concreteness and objected to the bifurcation of nature, in which the wavelengths behind a sunset are counted real and the red of the sunset is not. Going beyond the blind spot, Frank argues, swaps a dead universe for a life-centric one in which meaning is central, because there is no telling the universe's story without the beings who have perspectives.

The final chapter turns to extraterrestrial life, and Frank begins by dismissing the usual starting point. UFOs, now called UAPs, are not good evidence of anything: personal testimony is the worst form of evidence, the videos remain fuzzy blobs after seventy years, and since the modern era began in 2017 some ninety-five percent of reports can be explained, with most of the rest too thin to evaluate. The famous Go Fast video, he notes, yields to simple trigonometry from numbers on the camera screen: the object was moving at about forty miles per hour at some fifteen thousand feet, which is to say it was a balloon. Claims in congressional hearings about recovered craft and bodies, he adds, have produced no test results anyone can examine. Looking for alien life in our atmosphere, he says, is like looking for Nebraskans in a Himalayan village. Go to where they live: alien planets.

Every star, it turns out, has planets, and roughly one star in five hosts a world in the right place for life. A robust biosphere rewrites its planet's atmosphere, as oxygenic photosynthesis did on Earth, so telescopes that read the light passing through an exoplanet's atmosphere can search for biosignatures. The James Webb Space Telescope is already at the edge of that ability: a proposed detection of dimethyl sulfide, a gas algae produce on Earth, on K2-18b, a possible Hycean world with a deep ocean under a hydrogen atmosphere, convinced almost nobody in the end, but it showed the instruments can now reach the relevant signatures. Frank expects the telescopes of the next ten, twenty, or thirty years to settle whether life is common or rare.

Technosignatures, the marks of technological life, may be easier to recognize. Long a career-killing sideline, the field was reborn at a 2018 NASA meeting, and Frank was principal investigator on NASA's first grant to study atmospheric technosignatures. His group's examples include chlorofluorocarbons, which nature cannot make and which a civilization might release on purpose to warm a planet, the glint of continent-scale solar panels, and the city lights of a city planet.

Thinking about civilizations leads back to Earth. Borrowing from the Gaia hypothesis, Frank describes planetary intelligence: some two and a half to three billion years ago, around the Great Oxidation Event, Earth's biosphere matured into a system that responds to its environment in ways that sustain itself. Our technosphere, the sum of our technology, is still immature: it degrades the biosphere it sits on top of and depends on. A mature technosphere would have its financial, political, and technological systems working to keep the whole healthy, as if banks refused loans for coal plants the way they now refuse loans for nuclear weapons. The civilizations anyone could ever find, he argues, will be the long-lived ones that made exactly that transition. The next flagship telescope, the Habitable Worlds Observatory, is being designed to search for both biosignatures and technosignatures.

If we found life, Frank predicts no riots and no collapse of religion, which he calls adaptable. Nor does human specialness require being the only example: what makes us special is that we are life, with its capacity to create. Even microbes elsewhere would matter enormously, because a star's whole future can be predicted from a few facts, while nothing predicts what a cell will become; life is the only physical system that innovates, so one more example would mean this creative capacity is part of what the universe does, and we would belong to a community of life. Communication across interstellar distances, he says, is overrated. Just knowing they exist, and being able to observe them from a distance, would change everything.

Open questions

  1. Frank endorses Rosen's suggestion that organizationally closed systems may not be Turing computable, while calling the proof contentious. What evidence or experiment could actually settle that claim, and what would follow for artificial intelligence research if it turned out to be true?
  2. QBism puts the measurer at the center of quantum mechanics, and Frank repeats the test that an interpretation earns its keep by suggesting new experiments. What experiment could distinguish QBism from many worlds, and if none can, does the choice between them have any practical consequence?
  3. The civilizations we could detect, Frank argues, will be the long-lived ones whose technospheres matured. If ours is still immature and actively degrading its biosphere, what would the transition to a mature technosphere concretely require, and on what timescale could it happen?

a16z News · Why AI will make pharma even bigger

Big Pharma Can Rival Big Software

On a16z News, “Big Pharma Can Rival Big Software”, subtitled “AI Will Make Pharma Even Bigger”, is a market analysis by six members of the a16z team, bylined on the page as Caroline, Justin Kahl, Alex Immerman, and three others. Their claim is that pharmaceutical companies already operate at a scale that rivals the software industry, and that AI is about to improve the economics of making drugs on both sides of the business: better odds in development, and cheaper, faster development itself.

The opening fact is Eli Lilly's size. Lilly is worth more than a trillion dollars, there are more than 300 drugs worldwide projected to sell over $1B a year each, and the top 30 pharma companies together represent roughly $5T of market value. The first chart, “Top pharma companies”, ranks the industry by market value against 2026 revenue. Lilly towers over the field at about $1.2 trillion, followed by Johnson & Johnson at $665B and AbbVie at $515B, with Roche, Merck, Novartis, AstraZeneca, Amgen, and Gilead completing the group of nine companies worth more than $200B. Gold dots on the same chart show what investors pay per dollar of revenue: Lilly trades at roughly 14 times its 2026 revenue, the richest multiple on the page, while most of the industry sits between two and seven times.

Lilly reached the trillion-dollar club, a group of about 16 companies otherwise dominated by tech, on the strength of tirzepatide, its GLP-1 drug franchise. The second chart, “GLP-1 sales rival OpenAI and Anthropic”, plots one drug class against the two best-known AI labs. Combined GLP-1 revenue for Lilly and Novo Nordisk climbs from $36B in 2023 to $53B in 2024 and $76B in 2025, reaching an estimated $101B in 2026. OpenAI and Anthropic together grow much faster, from $4B in 2024 to $18B in 2025 and an estimated $85B in 2026, but a single category of medicine still outsells them. Lilly's GLP-1 portfolio alone did close to $17B in the third quarter of 2026, and analysts expect it to pass $100B a year by 2030.

The authors describe a duality at the heart of the industry. Drug development is a hits business, while a successful approved drug behaves like an annuity. The third chart, “A small share of drugs drives most of the market”, shows how skewed the hits are. Drugs peaking above $10B a year, the generational drugs, are just 2% of all drugs yet produce 32% of sales. Blockbusters peaking between $1B and $10B are 29% of drugs and 56% of sales. Together, 31% of drugs account for 87% of the money, while the bottom half of the market, drugs peaking under $500M, contributes about 6% of sales between them.

The fourth chart, “Big pharma is a hit-driven business”, shows the same concentration inside each of the ten largest companies. A single largest drug supplies 67% of Novo Nordisk's sales, 56% of Lilly's, 54% of Merck's (that drug is Keytruda), and 49% of Gilead's, and the top five drugs together contribute anywhere from 38% of Amgen's sales to 85% of Novo Nordisk's. The text makes the same point with rounder numbers: for the top ten companies, roughly half of revenue comes from the top three assets, including 63% of Lilly's 2025 revenue from its GLP-1 portfolio and 54% of Merck's from Keytruda alone.

Once a drug wins, the prize resembles a software company. The fifth chart, “Blockbuster drugs rival software companies”, lines up peak annual drug sales against the full revenue of well-known software firms. Tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, peaks at a projected $88B a year, roughly double the entire revenue of SAP or Salesforce, each at $42B. Keytruda at $38B and Skyrizi at $34B sit just below those two software giants, and Dupixent at $29B exceeds Adobe's $24B. Patent protection makes the annuity durable: a drug typically keeps 10 to 14 years of exclusivity after launch, and Dupixent, launched in 2017, grew from $2B in 2019 to nearly $18B in 2025 with its United States patent running to 2031.

The sixth chart, “Top grossing drugs, 2000-2032F”, adds a table under the same ten drugs listing each one's owner, disease area, peak year, molecule type, and whether it was first in its class. Four of the ten were first-in-class, including tirzepatide and Keytruda, while the rest improved on existing mechanisms and still won their categories, which matches the piece's argument that both first-in-class and best-in-class strategies produce the largest winners. Most of the peaks are still forecasts, several dated 2032, and the list mixes monoclonal antibodies and peptides with small molecules such as Rinvoq, Foundayo, and Biktarvy. Below the generational tier sit the ordinary blockbusters: about 300 drugs in the $1B to $10B peak band, comparable to the roughly 190 public software companies with revenue in that same range.

The market itself is sliced two ways in the text. By disease, oncology takes 30% of global drug sales, metabolic disorders 18%, and immunology 11%. By molecule, biologics account for 42% of global revenue and small molecules for 43%. It is against this backdrop that the authors place AI: new models of human biology to find targets, models trained on proprietary data to design molecules that are safer and more potent, and AI-driven efficiency in trials and distribution, all of which would let companies take more shots on goal.

The problem AI is meant to fix is a long productivity decline, and the seventh chart, “Total FDA-approved drugs per year”, lays it out in three parts. Annual FDA approvals of novel drugs have bounced between 22 and 59 since 2015, ending at 46 in 2025, with small molecules making up most of each year's total. Over a similar span, United States pharma sales more than doubled, from $448B in 2016 to $915B in 2025. And a table at the bottom shows where the drugs come from: 65% are sourced externally through acquisitions or licensing, only 28% are invented internally, and 7% come from collaborations. Spending has doubled while approvals stayed flat, and most of what big pharma sells was bought rather than built.

The eighth chart, “The path from discovery to launch”, is a funnel showing why output is so hard to raise. In the article's illustrative model, about 24 candidates enter the pipeline for every one drug that launches, and only 4% of candidates make it through all eight stages, from target-to-hit through preclinical work and three phases of trials to regulatory submission. The journey takes 13.5 years. Costs per candidate climb steeply as the funnel narrows, reaching $150M for each candidate entering Phase III, and the cash cost works out to $264M for a single successful path, or $873M once the failures are amortized in. The text's own present-value figure, which adds the cost of capital, is close to $2B per approved drug over about 13 years, and fewer than 5% of candidates that start the process are ever approved.

Between discovery and approval sits a large services economy. Contract research organizations, which run experiments and process trial samples, form a $90B global market, split between $30B of discovery and preclinical work and $60B of clinical trials. The authors expect demand for wet lab data to explode as AI generates candidates faster than they can be tested, and they cite Formation Bio's prediction of an era of drug candidate abundance, with AI helping at every step from picking targets and designing molecules to recruiting patients and drafting FDA submissions.

The ninth chart, “Illustrative 10-year IRRs across drug development costs”, quantifies what happens if AI bends that cost curve. It models a drug's ten-year return at two development costs. At today's roughly $1.6B cost, a drug peaking at $500M a year returns 8%, a $250M drug loses money at negative 3%, and only billion-dollar drugs clear 22%. Halve the development cost to $800M and the same $500M drug returns 22%, the $250M drug turns positive at 8%, and a $1B drug returns 41%. The piece frames AI's contribution in exactly these terms: raise the probability of success, shorten the time to approval, and targets that are uneconomical today become worth pursuing, while AI-native drug companies become structurally more profitable.

Investors, however, pay for the annuity, not the engine. The tenth chart, “Historical multiples: top 25 pharma companies”, traces valuation multiples from September 2016 to March 2026. The median large pharma company has traded at about four times forward revenue and 16 times forward earnings across that decade, with the revenue median staying in a narrow band even as the most richly valued names rose toward ten times revenue and fell back. The text gives the working ranges: price-to-earnings multiples of 8 to 20 times, three to seven times revenue, and net income of 20% to 40% of revenue. Because development takes so long, drugs still in the pipeline are close to a rounding error in these valuations, and companies must constantly replace revenue as patents expire. Humira's fall at AbbVie, from $20B in 2022 to $4B in 2025 after loss of exclusivity, is the standing warning.

That sets up the piece's central speculation. If AI makes discovery repeatable, investors may start valuing the discovery engine itself, the way they value a software platform, granting what the authors call valuation credit and platform credit. Biotech startups, which historically invented much of the industry's platform technology but had to starve it in favor of individual drug assets, could then fund broader pipelines at a lower cost of capital. The authors note their own stake in the outcome: a16z has launched a joint venture fund with Eli Lilly, the Biotech Ecosystem Fund, for exactly this purpose.

The engine, they argue, is improving on schedule. Eroom's law, the observation that drug discovery kept getting slower and more expensive even as computing improved, may have met its opposite in continuous frontier model improvement. Progress runs on two vectors: reasoning models that take over scientist tasks such as literature review, experimental design, and regulatory drafting, and diffusion-based structure models that generate and optimize molecules. The authors put today's bio AI at roughly the GPT-3 moment, the point where one model starts working across many tasks, with molecular models showing both better generalization to new targets and the ability to generate functional designs, and with scaling laws apparently holding as training data grows.

The eleventh chart, “FoldBench: progress across AI models”, is their evidence. It plots the share of antibody and antigen predictions good enough to count as acceptable on the FoldBench benchmark, across 29 model results from 2024 to 2026. AlphaFold 3 set the early mark at 47.9% in 2024, and rivals such as HelixFold 3 at 28.4% and Protenix at 34.1% trailed it for more than a year, with SeedFold reaching 53.2% by late 2025. Then the line jumps: IsoDDE posts 75.58% in early 2026, AnewFold reaches 76.2%, and NovaAtom-Lite reaches 79.1% by late 2026. In about two and a half years the best score rose by more than 30 points, most of it in a single year.

Commercial signals follow the benchmark. Isomorphic Labs holds partnerships with Johnson & Johnson, Novartis, and Lilly, including up to $1.2B in potential milestone payments from Novartis and $1.7B from Lilly, and Chai Discovery, Boltz, BigHat Bio, Nabla Bio, Genesis Molecular AI, and Profluent all have deals with major pharma companies. The frontier AI labs are moving in as well: Anthropic has opened a wet lab and announced its own drug candidates for neglected diseases, OpenAI has acquired Orchestra Bio, and OpenAI's GPT-Rosalind already helps scientists interpret literature and experimental data. The low-hanging fruit, the authors say, is making pharma faster, better, and cheaper inside existing pathways; the larger prize is drugs that would not have existed at all.

For startups, the piece lays out two ways to capture value. The first is selling data and technology to pharma and to the AI labs, following precedents like Veeva, a vertical software company worth $50B. The emerging categories are data providers and lab automation, predictive models of biology including virtual cells, scientific reasoning agents, molecular design models, and model harnesses that help companies deploy and fine-tune all of the above. The second is building full-stack biotech companies that discover and sell their own medicines, as Recursion, Isomorphic, insitro, Xaira, Relay Therapeutics, and Octant Bio are doing, keeping the full value of each successful drug and feeding lab and clinical results back into their models.

The twelfth and final graphic, “The Emerging AI Pharma Stack”, draws that landscape as a diagram. Four layers stack upward: data and infrastructure at the base, split between data providers and wet lab automation; then models, divided into virtual cells, molecular design, and scientific reasoning; then an applications layer for model harnesses and deployment; and drug development at the top. A single vertical bar labeled “Full Stack AI Native Pharma” runs through all four layers, which is the diagram's whole argument: specialists will own individual layers, while the AI-native companies span the stack from raw data to approved drug.

The authors close with the headwinds. Biology has no internet-scale training corpus: the Protein Data Bank, the largest public archive of high-quality protein structures, holds about 300,000 structures gathered over 50 years. Biological systems are multilayered and may need far more data than language ever did, along with better architectures; as insitro founder Daphne Koller has argued, better molecular design does not help if the wrong target is chosen first. And unlike math or code, a drug design cannot be fully verified on a computer, so validation still runs through wet labs and human trials on long, regulated timelines. Their final position is nonetheless direct: it is hard to name a more impactful use of AI than better medicines, made faster and more cheaply.

Open questions

  1. The piece's own funnel and return charts are labeled illustrative baselines that assume no AI improvement, and its benchmark chart measures prediction quality on a computer. What clinical evidence, and on what timeline, would show that AI-designed candidates actually succeed in human trials at higher rates than the roughly 4% baseline the funnel describes?
  2. If discovery becomes repeatable and investors begin granting platform credit, who captures the value: the large pharma companies that own the annuities, the full-stack AI natives, or the model and data sellers? The sourcing table shows 65% of drugs already come from outside big pharma, so does AI deepen that pattern or reverse it?
  3. The headwinds section argues that biology's bottleneck is data and slow, regulated validation rather than model quality. If generating experimental data becomes the scarce input, does the durable advantage belong to whoever owns the wet labs and data infrastructure at the bottom of the stack, rather than to whoever builds the best model?

Code Bear · Why Microsoft 365 was down for 19 hours

Why Microsoft 365 Was Down for 19 Hours?

On Code Bear, “Why Microsoft 365 Was Down for 19 Hours?” walks through a daylong Microsoft 365 outage that, at the time of recording, had lasted 19 hours and was still not fully over. Outlook mailboxes would not open, Teams calendars failed, and Copilot could not read work email. A hospital network in Toronto called a code gray, its alert for an infrastructure failure. A jail in Colorado stopped taking bail payments online, and in Japan, CNN reported, government staff had gone back to printing documents.

The video's central claim is that the load did not come from extra traffic. Microsoft's servers were failing Microsoft's own automated health checks, the tests that decide whether a server is fit to work. The explainer uses a restaurant analogy: a health check is like a host who asks each waiter every few seconds whether they are okay, and stops sending them tables if they do not answer. Microsoft's Azure documentation describes the same design: when a server fails its health probe, the load balancer stops sending it new connections, and two misses in a row are enough.

That design carries a cascade risk. When one server is pulled, every remaining server gets more work. If they slow down under the extra load, they fail their checks too, and the fleet shrinks further. Google's engineering book on running large systems warns about exactly this failure mode, noting that health checking itself can make a service unhealthy.

The timeline, in Eastern time on Thursday, shows the explanation evolving. The email problem began at 11:17 in the morning, and Microsoft's first public post came at 12:54, later seen more than 17 million times. Downdetector logged reports from 74 countries and territories within about an hour, peaking at 19,650 reports a minute across five Microsoft services, against a normal level under 15. Outlook reports alone ran at more than 2,000 times their usual volume.

At 2:12 in the afternoon Microsoft paused all deployments, and at 2:31 it posted the key line: the load was not driven by an increase in traffic, but by a number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable. Twelve minutes later the admin center gave the reason. The servers were failing automated health checks, and that was making them unavailable.

Recovery kept failing in instructive ways. At 4:00 Microsoft validated an alternative health check method and some servers came back, which looked like the fix but was not. At 5:09 it conceded that its actions had not provided the anticipated relief, because a core process on the servers was becoming overwhelmed. At 7:06 came worse news: servers brought back could become unresponsive again once they received traffic.

The suspected trigger also shifted. At 8:06 Microsoft said a process that sets up secure connections was getting overwhelmed, and it began reverting a recent network configuration change that correlated with the start of impact, after saying earlier in the day first that the problem might be related to a recent change and then that no recent change correlated. At 10:13 the changes were still not taking effect as expected, and executive Charles Lamanna said there was no indication of a cyberattack.

Meanwhile the effects spread: Via Rail warned passengers they might not get trip emails, Indigo's email support went down, city staff in Austin could not send or receive email, and the Philippine courts said their system was hit too. Just before midnight Microsoft described a surge in outbound calls from its routing services to its backend servers. Around 2:00 in the morning the update read “Marginal success,” and 29 minutes later a patch to an authentication and routing component started to help. Microsoft also extended the email timeout to 48 hours so stuck messages would not be lost.

The video then puts the outage in a pattern. Six weeks earlier, on September 29, Azure's AI services in Sweden were down for about six hours: a traffic surge started it, but Microsoft's own report says the long outage was primarily caused by how the backend decided whether instances were healthy, and it was fixed by reconfiguring the health check. In October 2025 Amazon's US-East region was down for more than 14 hours after health checks failed against healthy load balancers, and engineers fixed it by turning off the automatic health check failovers. Facebook's 2021 disappearance and CrowdStrike's 2024 bad update, which reached 8.5 million Windows devices through a bug in the validator meant to catch it, complete the set: four companies, four different bugs, and in each report a check or safety system was part of the chain.

Engineers have a rule for the moment every server fails at once: fail open. Amazon's DNS documentation puts it in one line, “If no record is healthy, all records are healthy,” and its engineering library warns that automation which removes servers that could still have done useful work does more harm than good. Azure Front Door works that way, but Azure Load Balancer stops sending new traffic when every server fails. Which kind sits in front of Exchange Online, Microsoft has not said.

As of the recording, Microsoft had not published a root cause, though it promised a full report. Microsoft 365 has more than 450 million paid seats, Microsoft's stock was down about 2.5 percent before markets opened on Friday according to CNBC, and one consultant's estimate put the cost to businesses in the billions, which the video flags as an estimate rather than a measurement. Recovery was complete in parts of Asia-Pacific and moving to other regions. The internet, the video closes, had already renamed the service Microsoft 364.

Open questions

  1. Microsoft has promised a full root cause report. Will it confirm that health checks started the cascade, or will the network configuration change it reverted that evening turn out to be the primary fault, with the health checks only amplifying it?
  2. Which failure philosophy sits in front of Exchange Online: fail open like Azure Front Door, or stop all new traffic like Azure Load Balancer? Will Microsoft say, and will this outage push it to change the design?
  3. The same failure shape has now hit Microsoft, Amazon, Facebook, and CrowdStrike. Is there any realistic way to test a safety system at full scale before it becomes the outage, or is this a cost the whole industry has quietly accepted?

a16z · Ami Luttwak on defending against AI hackers

Cyberattacks Used to Take Months. AI Can Do It in Minutes.

On a16z, “Cyberattacks Used to Take Months. AI Can Do It in Minutes.” is an episode of The a16z Show in which a16z general partners Zane Lackey and Sarah Wang talk with Ami Luttwak, co-founder and chief technology officer of the cloud security company Wiz. Luttwak argues that the industry has crossed a threshold. Security professionals are calling October 2026 Black October, and he treats it as a black swan event: a single month with more security incidents, and more severe ones, than anyone has seen before.

Two earlier moments foreshadowed it. The first was a much-discussed demonstration that a frontier model could find real vulnerabilities. The second was the breach of Hugging Face, the world's largest AI model repository, by an autonomous AI agent. Both looked like warnings, and nothing much changed. In Black October, Luttwak says, the warnings became the reality: an explosion of newly found vulnerabilities, including PHP2Shell, which he calls probably the most severe vulnerability ever found by AI, alongside a rise in actual AI-run attacks on companies and startups.

The mechanism is speed. An attack that used to take the best human attackers weeks or months now runs end to end in about 20 minutes. Open source models are, in Luttwak's estimate, no more than about three months behind the frontier models at offensive work, and they have now caught up enough that an attacker does not need to be sophisticated to use them. AI development platforms are being targeted specifically because they hold their customers' AI keys. Wiz's blog post on that one market first counted nine companies attacked in October, and the list has since grown to fifteen.

PHP2Shell is his case study in how patching is failing to keep pace. PHP runs on roughly 70 percent of the internet, and almost 100 percent of Wiz's customers had some PHP running. Customers who could patch did so quickly, with about 50 percent patched within the first two to three days. But older versions of PHP receive no patch at all, so Luttwak expects this single vulnerability to take years to fully resolve. The deeper problem is structural: the time needed to turn a vulnerability into a working exploit has collapsed, while the time needed to patch has not fallen at all and has in fact risen over the past two decades. Details leak before a CVE is even published, and a fix for something like the Linux kernel can take weeks or months to reach every distribution. His verdict is blunt: the world is not ready for this.

Asked whether AI can fix the patching side too, Luttwak sees a path only for companies being built now. A new company can design for automatic patching, with an agentic pipeline in which an automated developer writes the fix and automated testing, deployment, and operations carry it into production. For legacy companies with sprawling, poorly understood code, he does not see a realistic path to that. Founders starting today, he says, should plan for it from the beginning.

Vulnerability discovery itself is being industrialized. Wiz built an internal harness, which it calls Atlas, that runs many different models against code, because every model and every model version behaves differently and has its own strengths, speed, and cost. He compares the result to Bitcoin mining: there is always more to find, and every new model release uncovers vulnerabilities nobody had spotted. Multiple teams now find the same critical flaws independently at the same time, as happened with PHP2Shell, and he says multiple teams have reported the same still-undisclosed critical vulnerability into a zero-day competition Wiz is preparing. The real engineering challenge, he argues, is building intelligent pipelines that route work across models by difficulty and cost. Wiz's own blue agent, which investigates attacks, starts with deterministic detection, escalates through a cheap model, and only calls the most advanced model when the cheaper stages agree something looks malicious.

That is also why he insists security testing has to be continuous rather than a one-off scan. A single full scan of a company's code with a frontier model can cost tens of millions of dollars, and it is out of date the moment a new model or new code ships. The hard problem is allocating a finite budget wisely across scanning from the inside and the outside, code analysis, and runtime, as a continuous system rather than an event.

His central defensive prescription is to attack yourself. Wiz's red agent mounts offensive AI attacks against a customer's applications from the outside, using context Wiz has from the inside, to find problems before real attackers do. The reasoning is that the bar keeps rising: an application considered secure last week may not be secure this week, because attackers gain new capabilities with every model release. The red agent's pipeline starts with the most urgent layer, a deterministic analysis of everything unauthenticated and exposed on the internet, which Luttwak calls more important than scanning code, and only then moves to deeper penetration testing and code analysis. Most Wiz customers already use it, he says, and it is one of the fastest-growing features the company has released, but across the industry adoption is nowhere near enough.

For founders, the practical picture is grim. Luttwak asks listeners to imagine a startup whose team wakes to find an attack already finished: twenty minutes from entry to the theft of every secret, including the AI keys of all its customers. Startups holding anything attackers want, above all AI credentials, are now prime targets, and one concrete step is to put limits on those keys so a theft cannot run up enormous charges. On budgets, he pushes back against the idea that money solves it. One CISO expects his security budget to rise 80 percent next year, but Luttwak argues the binding constraint is architecture and design, not spend: build as if a nation-state attacker is coming, with sandboxing, isolation, and network and process lockdown, and do not treat a container as a safety boundary, because kernel zero-days let attackers escape them. Most legacy security tools, he says, are simply no longer relevant.

That leads to his sharpest claim about security teams themselves: most of what they do is not security. Time spent on compliance and producing reports, his example is the configuration checklists of classic cloud security posture management, should shrink drastically. What counts as security, in his definition, is finding critical risk, mitigating it or at least detecting it, and being ready to respond within minutes. A brand-new team can build security fully automated; an established one is slowed less by tooling than by not knowing who owns what.

For the next few years, Lackey predicts a return to the 1990s and 2000s: unauthenticated remote holes and breaches surfacing constantly, and possibly internet worms again, as in the Windows XP era when a fresh machine could be infected before it finished updating. Luttwak largely agrees, and pulls forward his own earlier warning of a Y2K-like moment arriving within 12 to 18 months. Black October may be the beginning, he says, because open source models are catching up faster than defenders hoped. This is, in his telling, almost the only domain where AI is already better than humans: attacks built on kernel driver zero-days, once vanishingly rare, now happen every day. The companies that survive will be the ones redesigning their internet-facing systems for this attacker, and taking whatever is not ready off the internet.

The stakes extend well past tech companies. The hosts raise recent attacks on refrigeration units in Europe and the food supply chain behind them. Luttwak says anything connected to the internet is at risk, which is why Wiz started Scan for Good, an initiative that scans public services and nonprofits, finds their problems, and tries to push them toward continuous scanning of their own.

Open questions

  1. Exploitation now takes minutes while patch times have not improved in decades. What happens to the long tail of legacy systems, including the older PHP versions that will never receive a fix: permanent exposure, forced retirement, or something else?
  2. Luttwak insists the binding constraint is security architecture rather than budget. If he is right, what does a company actually rebuild first, and how does it protect itself during the years that rebuilding takes?
  3. Open source offensive capability is only months behind the frontier and still improving. Once that capability is effectively free and universal, does continuous self-attack remain a viable defense, or does the economics of defense break down entirely?

IFML · Scott Aaronson on whether we are cooked

Scott Aaronson: Are We Cooked?

On IFML, “Scott Aaronson: Are We Cooked?” is a talk by Scott Aaronson, the University of Texas at Austin theorist whose career has been spent on quantum computational complexity. He gave it at a workshop titled “Achieving Superintelligence, What Does It Take?”, a title he jokes he would have changed to “Achieving Superintelligence, Is That a Good Idea?” His claim is that in his corner of science, and in much of mathematics, superintelligence by any practical standard arrived sometime this summer: large language models now routinely do in hours things that he and his colleagues tried and failed to do for years, results they would have counted as career milestones.

He is careful about what humans still do. The field's work now is understanding, processing, explaining, and directing what the models produce. But the nature of that work, he says, has changed irrevocably, and it is the only subject theorists talk about when they get together, whatever a conference is nominally about.

The starting shot, in his telling, came in May of this year, when OpenAI announced that an internal model had settled the unit distance problem, a question of Paul Erdős about how many pairs of points in the plane can sit exactly one unit apart. Erdős had conjectured his grid construction was optimal, and the conjecture stood for some 80 years. It is false. The model found a better construction using ideas from algebraic number theory that were known there but known to almost no one working in combinatorial geometry. Within a week, humans had understood the proof, simplified it, and improved its key constant from roughly 10 to the minus 38 to about 0.035.

When Aaronson blogged about it, the reaction surprised him: angry denial. Commenters called it marketing hype, said the AI had produced slop from which humans extracted a proof, or said the unit distance problem was not real math anyway. He compares this goalpost moving to a social experiment in which people savaged a painting they were told an AI had made in the style of Monet, deploying all the tools of art criticism, before the poster revealed it was an actual Monet. It is why, he notes, Turing insisted 76 years ago that the imitation game be a blind test.

A month or two ago, the evidence got harder to argue with. One of the Clay Millennium problems, the question of whether the Navier-Stokes equations can blow up in finite time, was solved by an OpenAI model running some 10,000 agents in parallel and burning, by reports he cites, about 15 million dollars of compute to win a 1 million dollar prize. The proof runs 166 pages, and Aaronson doubts any human has yet read and understood it. But OpenAI also produced a Lean certificate, a machine checked formal verification, so belief in the result reduces to trusting the Lean kernel and the correctness of the problem's formalization. The controversy around the announcement, he adds, has pushed OpenAI to route such claims through a committee, leaving a rumor mill about which other famous open problems the company is privately sitting on.

He then tours the map of complexity classes he has spent his career on: P and NP, the randomized class BPP, the proof class MA, and the quantum class BQP, which Peter Shor put on the map 32 years ago by showing factoring sits inside it. Above those sit QMA, where the proof itself is a quantum state, and QCMA, where the proof is classical but verification is quantum. At the top of his map is PP, a counting class that his own 2004 theorem showed equals quantum computing with postselection, the power to condition on an arbitrarily unlikely measurement outcome.

The talk's central story concerns QMA1, the variant of QMA where a correct proof must make the verifier accept with certainty, with no error at all. Whether QMA can always be given this perfect completeness had been open for 27 or 28 years. The classical analogue for MA has been known since the 1980s, and Stephen Jordan and coauthors proved the QCMA version in 2011, but QMA resisted. In 2009 Aaronson showed the two classes differ relative to a quantum oracle, a black box world, while warning that oracle results do not always predict the real one. Last summer Stacey Jeffery and Freek Witteveen found a strikingly clever amplification that drives the completeness error down to one over two to the two to the n, doubly exponentially small, though still not zero.

Wondering whether that was the best any black box method could do, Aaronson did something novel for him, in June 2025: he asked GPT-5 to prove the lemma he needed. It could. The argument, which writes the verifier's best acceptance probability as the largest eigenvalue of a certain matrix and tracks it with a potential function borrowed from polynomial approximation theory, showed the Jeffery and Witteveen protocol is optimal relative to any quantum oracle. The blog post went viral. A dominant response was that the lemma was trivial and Aaronson had simply been lazy, a charge he half accepts. A mathematician named Harris then found an even simpler potential function than the model's, and joined the paper.

This September the underlying question fell. Aaronson reports that his former student Sabi Grewal and collaborators, and independently Liu and Thomas Vidick, used AI help to modify the Jeffery and Witteveen construction and prove that QMA equals QMA1 in the real world, despite the oracle separation. More results followed within weeks. With his former student Adam Bouland, Bouland's student Jordan Docter, and Barak Nehoray, he showed there is a quantum oracle relative to which his 2004 postselection result fails, a question they had been stuck on until GPT proved the key statement about polynomial degrees; one step the model called obvious turned out to need a five page proof when challenged. John Bostanci and coauthors, again with AI help, separated QMA(2), the two witness class whose only known upper bound is the enormous NEXP, from QMA relative to a quantum oracle. And the anti-concentration conjecture for the permanent of a random matrix, which Aaronson and Alex Arkhipov needed for their boson sampling proposal and posted to MathOverflow more than a decade ago, where Terence Tao could only improve the bound, has now been proved with AI help.

Looking for patterns, Aaronson says AI's advantage is greatest at two things: proving technical lemmas that are standard for someone with different expertise than yours, the cross field transfer that is often the beating heart of a career highlight paper, and highly parallelizable tasks like hunting for counterexamples, where thousands of agents can try thousands of constructions in a breadth first search. Even if those were all AI ever did, he argues, it would transform mathematics and theoretical computer science. It will not be all it does.

His forecasts he calls apocalyptimistic. The near future, he predicts, is a golden age for solving problems through human and AI collaboration: not cooked, but cooking. Young researchers should get in while they can. The models are still poor at explaining, at noticing what is most important in an argument, and at simplifying their own convoluted proofs, so there is plenty for humans to do. Access to the newest internal models is an advantage, but a short lived one; he spent two years at OpenAI and reckons it put him about six months in the future. If problems like P versus NP are ever solved, he says, AI will almost certainly play a central role. And if a time comes when machines do all of this better, the academic job market will be the least of anyone's worries.

His last piece of advice is to aim theory at AI alignment and safety, which he calls arguably the most urgent problem in the world and one that cannot be handed to AI until it is solved. In questions afterward he describes the group he runs at UT Austin on theoretical computer science and AI alignment, funded by Coefficient Giving: proving theorems about when out of distribution generalization works, asking what can efficiently be inferred about a model's behavior from its weights, and studying watermarking, backdoors, and even cryptographically embedded shutdown commands. He points to the Alignment Research Center and to Jacob Steinhardt, who left mathematics this summer to start an effort called MAZE, Math for AI Safety.

Fields that touch the physical world, he concedes, are moving slower, because their answers cannot be checked inside a self contained system and their training data is expensive. But he treats that as a delay, not an exemption, noting that Anthropic is building an autonomous wet lab run by AI models. One lesson of the last five years, he says, is that whenever anyone argues no one would be so stupid as to hand a powerful system a dangerous job, someone will be. Those of us in math and programming, he closes, are just canaries in a coal mine, here to warn everyone else of what is coming.

Open questions

  1. The Navier-Stokes proof Aaronson describes runs 166 pages, is formally verified, and yet may never be fully read by a human. Can the community's work of understanding and simplifying machine proofs keep pace with their production, or does verification without understanding become the new normal?
  2. Aaronson locates AI's edge in cross field lemmas and massively parallel counterexample search. Are the deepest open problems, P versus NP among them, reachable by those strengths, or do they need a kind of single deep idea that models have not yet shown?
  3. His closing advice is to build mathematical foundations for alignment before AI is trusted with the problem itself. Can theory on out of distribution generalization, interpretability, and watermarking mature fast enough to be useful before the systems it studies outrun it?

Intelligence is The Taste Test for AI

On YC Root Access, “Intelligence is The Taste Test for AI” is a conversation with Grace, the CEO of Intelligence, the company behind Design Arena. Intelligence benchmarks frontier AI models in the domains where there is no right answer to check against, starting with design. People come to Design Arena to build websites, images, and videos, and what they choose, edit, and keep becomes the benchmark data that frontier labs use to improve their models. The platform now has over 7 million users across 192 countries.

The mechanism depends on never asking users to act as raters. Building something visual works best the way a good designer works with a client: you are shown several versions of the thing and you pick the one you like, learning what you want by seeing the options. Those choices, edits, and back and forth are the raw signal. Intelligence plays the role a product manager plays inside a software company, turning raw user behavior into specific guidance for the model teams about what to fix. The labels are embedded in the experience itself, because asking people to grade outputs directly would degrade the product.

The company did not start here. Grace came out of big tech, with time at Apple and in quant, frustrated by how little individual effort moved a giant company. The startup began about a year and a half ago as a gaming company, when frontier models could just barely produce a single page of game code. The games worked, but they were not fun: badly designed, weak stories, nothing addictive about them. The conventional wisdom said models would keep improving at verifiable domains like math and physics, where answers can be checked, and would struggle in subjective domains like design and games. Grace and her team doubted that, because human game developers plainly get better with experience, so some learnable signal had to exist. For games, the ground truth turned out to be whether people play: retention. The product became double sided, giving people access to powerful models to make things they love, and giving the models access to people, at scale, to learn what people love.

That is why evaluation in subjective domains cannot copy the verifiable playbook. A model team cannot improve what it cannot diagnose, and one person's preference cannot diagnose a failure, because you cannot tell whether the population, or the specific cohort being served, agrees. Subjective quality only becomes measurable at population scale. Grace's comparison is TikTok, where watch time makes every user a verifier and the aggregate data predicts what will do well. There is no small priesthood of high taste, because what counts as high taste in one cohort can be low taste in another equally valuable one.

Preferences also move. Intelligence distinguishes stated preferences, what people say they want, from revealed preferences, what they actually choose, and it watches stated preferences drift from one quarter to the next. Some frontier labs now try to improve their models against where taste is heading rather than where it sits today. The labs are not all optimizing for the same audience either: what good design looks like differs between North America and Asia, so being better means different things to different providers. The result looks less like a single race and more like an ads landscape, a marketplace of intelligences with different correct answers for different people and tasks.

That marketplace is where Intelligence wants to sit. Grace cites one company's report that 70 percent of its model usage is already open source models, because plenty of tasks need a cheap, fast, good enough model rather than the strongest general one. The frontier, on this view, is a system chaining together many specifically intelligent parts from many providers. Intelligence aims to be the neutral marketplace for that specific intelligence, matching a requester's price, time, and preferences to the best available model, with no stake in supplying its own.

The company itself has moved unusually fast, from zero to a 60 million dollar annualized run rate in about a year. Grace credits a small team that refuses to give up on each other: instead of quietly deferring to a manager's call and nursing a story about incompatible approaches, people argue decisions through to a shared conclusion, which builds the trust that makes most other decisions effortless. The first 80 percent of the team came from Harvard, and the culture is meant to transmit the underlying beliefs, truth seeking, communication, trust, to people further from the founding circle. The one trait she hires for above all is the willingness, belief, and ability to learn anything, because in a field moving this fast, a hire who will not keep leveling up is not neutral but a growing liability.

Her personal rule for working this way is that an idiot in motion will always go farther than a genius at rest. She spent college as the genius at rest, pronouncing on what others should do, and found that actually doing a thing drowns out the peanut gallery and teaches what commentary cannot. Timing rewarded motion: three years ago there were essentially two frontier labs and no marketplace to build, and three years from now the market may be settled. Rejection, meanwhile, feels far worse from the inside than it looks from outside. She tells of a friend she tried to recruit who passed, which stung, and who called six months later offended that she had not tried harder. Her calibration is to expect hard things to hurt more than they should, the way public speakers are told to talk slower than feels natural.

Working at the frontier means the team is repeatedly among the first anywhere to do something, as when the models became good at video and Intelligence shipped one of the first production level videos with the new capabilities. Those moments arrive several times a month. The next year is about a different muscle entirely: scaling what works from one to a thousand, hiring a bigger team, building the internal systems to support it, and turning live embers into a bigger fire.

  2. Design Arena's signal depends on people coming to build for themselves rather than to rate outputs. As models get better and users need fewer comparisons to get what they want, does the signal each user generates shrink just as the labs need more of it?
  3. Intelligence wants to be a neutral marketplace across model providers. If one frontier lab's models end up dominating usage on the platform, can the marketplace stay neutral, or does it inevitably start optimizing for its biggest supplier?

Ryan Peterman · Sergey Levine on the best data for robotics

Sergey Levine (Top Robotics Researcher): The Best Data For Robotics

On Ryan Peterman, “Sergey Levine (Top Robotics Researcher): The Best Data For Robotics” is a short segment in which host Ryan Peterman asks Sergey Levine, the robotics researcher and co-founder of Physical Intelligence, a question the robotics community argues about constantly: of all the data you could train a robot on, simulated data, physical interactive data, video of humans, and more, what is the best, what is the worst, and what are the trade-offs?

Levine's answer is that the ranking matters less than the foundation underneath. A model can internalize many different data sources, he argues, if it can ground them in a thorough physical understanding of the world. People do this constantly. A flight simulator teaches you to fly because you arrive with world knowledge: you know the simulator stands for a real airplane, so the practice transfers. Even a cartoony Atari game makes sense because you connect the symbols on the screen to things you have experienced, and you fill in the blanks the simulation leaves.

The same happens when you watch someone else act. Watching a person cook, you do not track every muscle movement. You file the action away at a higher level of abstraction, something like “add salt,” because you have put salt on things yourself. Once that embodied foundation exists, Levine says, other sources of knowledge can connect up to it.

That leads him to invert the recipe the field is tempted to copy from language models. With LLMs, internet data came first, so it is tempting to start a robot model on YouTube videos and layer robot data on top. Levine thinks the order should be reversed: train a robotic foundation model on lots of real embodied robot data first, and it will then absorb other sources, such as human video, far better.

He points to evidence from work by his colleague Suraj Nair with a Georgia Tech collaborator, who added human video data on top of a foundation model already trained on robot data and then compared how the model represented human and robot experience internally. With a small model and little robot data, the two kinds of experience stayed fully separated in the model's features. But as the amount of robot data from many different robots grew, the features grouped by what task was being done rather than by whether a human or a robot was doing it, until at full robot data the embedding plots lined up almost entirely by task identity. Levine stresses the striking part: the base model was never trained on human data at all, yet once human data was added, it represented that data in the same task-based way.

Peterman then asks whether the foundation has to include data from the exact motors and joints of the robot you will deploy. Levine says that, so far, yes, you generally need some data from the deployment robot to get good performance. The measure of generalization is not zero-shot transfer to a brand new robot; it is how little experience from the new robot you can get away with, and how much skill transfers from other robots. What surprised him is how little special machinery this takes. His team skipped the elaborate morphology-aware designs he once planned, such as factorizing the model around six-degree-of-freedom arms and gripper heads. The model simply outputs one large vector of numbers, zero-pads it when a robot has fewer degrees of freedom than the vector, trains on all the robots at once, and produces the right actions from what it sees through the camera.

The last part of the segment is about how skills move between robots, and Levine's answer is that the model needs an intermediate step of thinking, done in the right modality. Thinking in text transfers high-level behavioral structure well: a plan like cleaning the kitchen and opening the drawer before putting the silverware away is largely indifferent to which body executes it. Lower-level skills can transfer too, if the thinking happens in images. In one experiment, the model imagined an image of the next milestone in a task, and with that a UR5 robot folded a t-shirt even though the team had no t-shirt folding data on the UR5 at all. Working out the exact joint angles directly would have been hard, because the UR5 moves differently from the robots in the training data. Picturing what the folded state should look like was easy, and once the image existed, the correct actions could be backed out from it. Levine compares it to a math problem where finding the right intermediate step makes the answer obvious.

He closes by generalizing the trick: the imagined milestone can be an edited image or a predicted video, and the real requirement is that the model imagine what progress on the task would look like. People plan the same two ways, semantically for some things and spatially for others. Rock climbing, he notes, is planned spatially: you do not think in coordinates, you imagine your arm reaching for the next hold. The segment ends mid-example, as the clip itself cuts off there.

Open questions

  1. Levine's ordering claim rests on representation evidence from one line of experiments. Will other labs, with different models and robot data mixes, reproduce the effect of features grouping by task rather than by embodiment?
  2. If real embodied data must come first, how much of it is enough before video and simulation data start paying off, and does that threshold change as models and datasets scale?
  3. Text and images are the two thinking modalities Levine demonstrates. Could thinking in other modalities, such as force or touch, unlock transfers that neither words nor pictures can carry, and can a model learn to choose the right modality for each task on its own?

McKinsey & Company · Andrew Lacy on whether full-body MRIs can change healthcare

Can Full-Body MRIs Change Healthcare? | Prenuvo CEO

On McKinsey & Company, “Can Full-Body MRIs Change Healthcare? | Prenuvo CEO” is an episode of the firm's Winning series in which senior partners Seth Goldstrom and Kevin Carmody interview Andrew Lacy, the founder and chief executive of Prenuvo. Goldstrom opens with his own story. He went for a full-body MRI that took about an hour, then got a call saying something serious had been found: a mass in his kidney. It was a stage 1A tumor, he had it removed, and two years on he describes himself as in great shape. He calls himself a poster child for what the company tries to do.

Prenuvo, which Lacy founded in 2018, performs detailed whole-body MRI examinations running from head to ankle, screening some 80 organs for disease. Patients receive a detailed report on what was found or not found, and, most importantly in Lacy's telling, on whether anything needs to be done about it. The aim is to give members information they can use to take control of their health and change its trajectory. On average, he says, the scans turn up a potentially life-saving finding in around one in twenty people, often a condition that has been present silently for years.

Lacy's own route into the company began in his early forties, after years of hard work, little sleep, and high stress. About to start a new company, he wondered how he could know he would be around to see the outcome of the effort. He traveled to Canada for an early version of a whole-body scan, nervous enough that he nearly canceled twice. Sitting with the radiologist afterward, he learned he was generally in good health despite his best efforts to harm himself, and he was struck by how much comprehensive information the scan gave him that the health system had never provided. He spoke with about a hundred customers of that clinic and watched people receive all kinds of diagnoses, then get up and hug the radiologist, grateful to understand their own health. He stopped what he was doing and started Prenuvo.

The mission, as Lacy frames it, is not to improve the existing health system incrementally. He argues that incremental work has made care more expensive without improving outcomes for individuals, and that the system needs complete transformation. Prenuvo's north star is to partner with patients on proactive health and to bring what he calls executive health to millions of people. He uses a sailing analogy: the destination sits over the horizon, the company sets waypoints a year or two out, and on any given day it tracks strategic initiatives with deadlines and key performance indicators. Prenuvo sees itself as a yearly waypoint for patients too, a check on whether the daily habits they control, exercise, food, sleep, and stress, are moving them where they expected.

Asked what capability matters most, Lacy points to an experimental mindset. There are few analogies for companies that have transformed healthcare, he says, so the company celebrates the act of experimenting, not only the outcomes, and measures activity as much as results. His favorite example is a pricing experiment that failed. The flagship scan has cost around $2,500 for years, and he wanted an offering under $1,000, reasoning that a normal elastic demand curve would reward a lower price. The $999 cancer screening exam he launched became probably the least popular exam the company has ever offered. Most people chose the comprehensive scan instead, which taught him that customers are not making simple price-versus-benefit decisions, and set him on a path of studying psychology as much as market dynamics.

That psychology is the heart of the conversation. People, Lacy says, have been conditioned by a reactive healthcare system in which a diagnosis is always advanced and serious, because the system only finds disease late. Why would anyone go looking for trouble? His counter is that disease is not a binary thing you have or do not have. It is a process that plays out over decades. The clinical definition of dementia, he notes, requires symptoms that amount to advanced disease, yet having imaged hundreds of thousands of brains, Prenuvo believes changes in blood flow can be visible twenty or thirty years before a clinical diagnosis. The job, together with the patient, is to bend the trajectory so the person never arrives at that point. He credits the pandemic, which left many people distrustful of a system that had missed their loved ones' underlying conditions, with growing the audience for proactive care each year. Only a small share of customers get a frightening diagnosis, but about one in five makes a significant lifestyle change after seeing a scan.

Beyond the scan itself, Lacy describes a data flywheel. Prenuvo has begun combining blood work with imaging and wants to add other modalities; more patients mean more data, and more data means better insights fed back into the product. The product has been rebuilt around a longitudinal lens, because the reason people come the first time, often a friend's diagnosis, is not the reason they stay. What brings them back is the belief that they can change their health's trajectory. A clinical team of about 200 people sits with each patient after a scan to go through the report, answer questions, and provide context, then checks in during the year and again at year's end.

Doctors were the hardest audience. When Lacy first asked physicians about the idea, they were almost universally against it. In the early days the company brought doctors in to experience the scan, and while perhaps 80 percent were opposed beforehand, more than 80 percent were referring patients afterward. He treats that information asymmetry as the conviction behind the company: transformative companies, he says, are built where such asymmetries exist. Physician referrals now introduce Prenuvo to employers, and the company has begun screening workforces, with insurance coverage a hope for the future. The deeper friction is professional identity. If a patient arrives with a detailed account of their own health, the physician's job is no longer mainly diagnosis, which Lacy estimates has been 80 percent of the role. The doctor becomes a coach, and not every physician, including some Prenuvo hires, can make that leap.

Inside the company, Lacy looks for a balance between what he calls pioneers, who try new things most of which might fail, and platformers, who build the scaffolding around whatever works. Culture is maintained deliberately: an internal Slack channel named “why we do this” collects every positive outcome story, with credit to everyone who touched that customer, and patients speak at the monthly all-hands meeting about how the service changed their health.

The most striking data story concerns spines. Prenuvo's first clinic, in Vancouver, drew customers averaging 52 years old; its second, in Silicon Valley, drew people in their twenties and thirties. Feeding tens of thousands of spine images into an AI system that estimates a spine's age, the team found a kink in the curve: it is genuinely hard to tell a 25-year-old spine from a 40-year-old one. Their hypothesis is that younger people are less active and sit more, so degeneration is arriving earlier than expected. Lacy, whose own first scan showed cervical spine problems, now uses a walking treadmill desk, and his spine has not worsened in eight years. The broader promise he sees is an organ age for each part of the body, tracked over time, and advice personalized to anatomy: a wide spinal canal tolerates bulges that would cause pain in a narrow one.

His closing advice to founders is about people. The first half of his career ran through law firms and McKinsey, high-performing organizations whose playbooks, he found, do not transfer to entrepreneurship and diverse teams. About 70 percent of his day now goes to coaching individuals, thinking about culture, and recruiting for the right kinds of people. You can have the best plan, he says, but without an effective organization you will never achieve it. And it is, he insists, never too late to start a company.

Open questions

  1. The scans are still paid for out of pocket, and the medical establishment's standard objections, false positives and downstream demand on the system, are raised in the conversation but not settled. What evidence would persuade insurers and employers to cover whole-body screening, and can Prenuvo produce it?
  2. Lacy's own classmates keep saying they must come in for a scan and then do not, for years. If the binding constraint is psychology rather than price or access, what actually converts intention into a first appointment at scale?
  3. Prenuvo's spine-age finding, and the organ-age measures Lacy hopes to build on it, come from the company's own data and models. Will those measures be validated independently, and will acting on them early change outcomes, or mainly change how people feel about their health?

Anthropic is speedrunning Fermat's Last Theorem verifications now

On Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander, “Anthropic is speedrunning Fermat's Last Theorem verifications now” is a read-through of Anthropic's blog post Safely Scaling Multi-Agent Teams with Claude Fable 5.2, by Samuel Allen Alexander, a mathematician and philosopher. In September, Anthropic published the first end-to-end formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem in the Lean proof language. Now a team of a thousand agents running an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 has reproduced that proof with no special platform and no researcher steering, and Alexander works through the post section by section, arguing with it as he goes.

Fermat's Last Theorem says that no positive integers a, b, and c satisfy a to the n plus b to the n equals c to the n for any n greater than 2. It went unproven for more than 350 years, until Andrew Wiles published his 129-page proof in 1995. Formalizing a proof, rewriting it so a computer can check every step, has been a long-standing goal in mathematics ever since.

The September formalization took a researcher and dozens of agents 11 days and roughly 6 billion output tokens, and produced some 13 million lines of Lean code. The agents coordinated through Prove2Me, a platform that links each statement to be proved to the smaller statements it splits into, so no one lost track of the project state.

The new run was what Anthropic calls a toy experiment. The thousand agents got a list of 60 statements to prove, the final theorem plus 59 intermediate milestones, along with a one-sentence sketch of the classical route, the definitions those statements use, and 47 already proved lemmas from the September work. There was no Prove2Me and no researcher directing them, only general tools: a shell and file editing in a private sandbox, the Lean toolchain with its mathematics library, a personal git branch with merge requests into the main branch, and one shared database for the whole team.

The result: the team finished in 28.7 hours, used a third as many output tokens as the September run, and wrote a proof one-seventh the length of the original. Alexander says the length is the real headline. The hours mean little, since faster hardware would cut them anyway, but a proof shrinking to a seventh of its size is a different kind of progress. Keep dividing by seven, he jokes, and the proof would eventually fit in the margin of Fermat's notebook, the margin where Fermat famously claimed his own proof would not fit.

He does raise one fairness question early. If the team was handed 47 proved lemmas from the earlier formalization, how much of the theorem did those lemmas quietly carry? If they were substantial, the comparison with September is apples to oranges. He hopes the post will clarify what those lemmas were, and notes the question as he reads on.

The sandboxes had no network access at all, and Alexander stresses why that matters: the September formalization is published, so a connected agent could simply download the finished proof instead of proving anything. Each agent's only link out was a pipe to a gateway that relayed its requests to the Claude API, the shared repository, and the database. Around that sat three layers of monitoring. A prompted classifier checked every shell command and file edit before it ran, looking for escape attempts or tampering, and an offline monitor later reviewed the team's actions. A block on the lead agent would have stopped the whole team; a block on a helper removed just that helper. Across more than a million tool calls in this run, none met the bar to be blocked. Anthropic also describes red-team sabotage exercises in which monitors that could see the agents' chain of thought and their messages to each other caught every sabotage attempt, and Alexander counts those watching agents as part of the hierarchy too.

Coordination is where the run got interesting. The only structure the team was given was one lead agent and 999 helpers. In its first ten minutes the lead set up five tables in the shared database: one for its orders to the team, one assigning who works on what, an open notes board, a subtasks list that any agent could add to and claim from, and a log of finished work. It then dealt the 60 statements out to crews of about 16 or 17 helpers each, and made one helper in each crew a captain, responsible for posting a plan and splitting the crew's work into claimable tasks.

The helpers' first job was to judge how hard their statements were. About 27 minutes in, three statements were already proved, and the lead set aside the 47 statements most helpers had judged infeasible within the run's budget, concentrating nearly everyone on the remaining 10. Crews swelled from about 17 helpers to between 46 and 139. All 10 statements were proved within roughly 70 minutes, far faster than the team's own pessimistic verdicts had assumed. The lead then wrote that there was no reason to idle, and moved helpers onto the harder statements it had first discarded. The helpers, meanwhile, took more and more initiative: by the third hour the lead was telling free helpers not to wait for it, and by hour 5.6 some 73 helpers had written 756 tasks between them, only 16 of them captains.

One statement dwarfed the rest. Langlands' Tunnel, a classical result that Wiles's proof relies on, had been judged the hardest piece: its captain estimated it might take 0.7 to 1 million lines of Lean, with under a 1 percent chance of finishing in time, so the lead saved it for last. Around hour 18, with nearly everything else done, the lead moved about 750 helpers onto it and wrote, honestly, that the odds were low. The team organized the statement as a tree: a plan split it into 10 areas, and any helper who found a piece too big could split it into smaller tasks beneath it. The tree grew to 2,418 tasks, up to 10 levels deep, and the statement took about nine times as many tasks and more than twice as much new code as any other. Langlands' Tunnel was proved at hour 28.4, and the final theorem followed at hour 28.7, kernel checked on the main branch. In the old days, as the lead's closing message put it, we used to say QED.

Alexander, who did his PhD on hierarchies of AIs, is delighted by how much structure the supposedly flat team invented for itself: crews, captains, and a ten-level task tree were none of them in the prompt. He is less impressed by the post's charts and animations, which he calls blinking lights, presentation gloss meant to dazzle managers, though he rates one visualization of the agents swarming between statements 99 out of 100 and asks only for a slider to scrub it. He also suspects the heavy emphasis on safety is partly marketing, while granting that the sandbox had to be airtight for the result to mean anything at all.

Anthropic's own conclusions are modest. Domain platforms like Prove2Me may still make agents faster, human direction still helps, and agents misjudge difficulty and give up too early: this team initially wrote off 47 of its 60 statements and then proved most of them. Alexander takes that last point and runs with it. If agents need encouragement at the moments they would otherwise quit, he jokes, the human job of the future may be cheerleader. He closes by wondering whether speedrunning teams will come for other monuments of mathematics next, the classification of finite simple groups or the Navier-Stokes problem, and signs off clearly pleased to be watching it happen.

Open questions

  1. The team was handed 47 proved lemmas from the September formalization, and the post does not say how hard those lemmas were. How much of the 28.7-hour result is the thousand-agent scaling, and how much is scaffolding inherited from the earlier run?
  2. Anthropic's safety case rests on monitors that read the agents' reasoning and messages and caught every sabotage attempt in testing. Fermat's Last Theorem has a kernel that can certify the final answer. Does the same monitoring hold up on long tasks where no checker exists to confirm the output is right?
  3. Alexander argues the shrinking proof, one-seventh the length, matters more than the shrinking clock. If agent teams keep compressing formal proofs run over run, what happens to the human work of understanding them?

Dwarkesh Patel · Recursive self-improvement has started

Recursive self-improvement has started

On Dwarkesh Patel, "Recursive self-improvement has started" is a solo video essay arguing that recursive self-improvement has begun in earnest. Earlier this year, Dwarkesh says, he would have put superintelligence at least the better part of a decade away. After three months of debating the question on and off the podcast and watching AI progress in verifiable domains, he now thinks AIs as good as the best humans at AI research could arrive within a couple of years, possibly within a couple of months, and that automating AI research would compress so much progress into a single year that by 2030, and plausibly as early as next year, AIs would match the best human experts in every field.

His median estimate is 2028 for automated researchers as good as the very best human AI researchers, assuming the labs go full steam ahead. He gives at least a 25 percent chance that it does not happen until after 2032, and notes that some of the best-informed researchers he knows expect it as early as 2027.

The first part of his case is that AI is already speeding up AI progress. METR, an independent evaluation organization, spent three months embedded inside Anthropic and concluded that Anthropic's rate of progress on well-scoped tasks is twice what it was a year ago, with a majority of that acceleration likely attributable to Anthropic's extensive use of AI to do R&D. OpenAI reported in September that its heaviest researcher users went from about $1,000 a day of internal coding agents at the start of the summer to over $7,000 a day by the end of August. Anthropic reported that Claude now leads 26 percent of its AI R&D work, up from under 1 percent in February.

His biggest single update is mathematics. In 2023 the models could not reliably add four-digit numbers. In 2024 they could barely solve high school competition problems. In 2025 they won a gold medal at the International Math Olympiad, earlier this year they began solving niche open problems, and a month ago an OpenAI model solved a Millennium Prize problem in 88 hours. Results that top mathematicians spent decades on are now produced as side quests to hype model releases.

He is candid about the skew: progress has concentrated in the verification-laden parts of mathematics, like finding counterexamples, rather than in asking powerful questions or founding new fields, a limitation he quotes from a recent essay by Toby Ord. His flip is that systems solved a Millennium Prize problem without any of that ability. And skilled problem solving of exactly that kind is worth more in AI research than in mathematics, because labs care whether a recipe empirically trains better models, not about theory for its own sake, and deep learning's progress over the last fifteen years has required little new theory.

His example is the transformer. Its pieces, attention and residual networks among them, were known and independently effective, and researcher Chris Olah has said the architecture was a natural combination of existing ideas. Dwarkesh's friend Sholto Douglas argues it could have been invented two years earlier than it was. What was missing was the persistence to connect half a dozen known tweaks in the right combination and run the many small experiments that combination required, which is precisely the search and matching work AIs have proved disproportionately good at in mathematics.

Once AIs match the best humans at research, he argues, progress accelerates for three reasons: they are faster, they are all that good, and there are far more of them. Today's AIs already work at about ten times human speed, and an automated researcher working around the clock crams roughly fifty times more work into a calendar week than a human can. Experiment compute does not speed up with them, so returns diminish steeply, but he expects algorithmic progress to run many times faster regardless.

On quality, he estimates that around 80 percent of a frontier lab's algorithmic progress is driven by its top fifty researchers, which is why labs pay hundreds of millions of dollars for the best talent. An automated lab makes every researcher that good. On numbers, research decomposes into modular streams that can be checked independently, one team filtering pre-training data, another building RL environments, another writing an attention kernel, much as a swarm of thousands of agents recently formalized a major theorem from thousands of subtasks. Swarms can try a thousand approaches in parallel, again with steeply diminishing returns.

Whether the pace then keeps compounding is, he says, close to a coin toss. It depends on a race between AIs getting better at research and ideas getting harder to find, and ideas already get harder: labs quadruple their R&D compute and double their workforce every year just to hold the current rate. If the AI side wins, the result is a software-only singularity, smarter and smarter versions within months, pressing against the physical limits of current chips. If ideas get harder faster, the result is what he calls a spark: three to six years of progress compressed into one calendar year, a leap comparable to going from GPT-3.5 to GPT-6 in twelve months, after which progress simmers until more compute is built.

Even the spark, he stresses, would feel insane. The resulting system would be vastly superhuman in any even slightly verifiable domain: better than Nvidia at designing the next generation of AI accelerators, able to out-earn Citadel and Jane Street given equal access to the market, and better than the rest of humanity at finding a room-temperature superconductor given remote access to a materials lab.

The obvious objection is transfer: most of the world, building a business, winning a court case, trading profitably, winning an election, cannot be graded from inside a data center. His answer is not that answers get baked into the weights. It is that scaling RL training builds general skills, seeking information, making and pushing a plan through mistakes, tracking what has been learned, collaborating, and that automated engineers during RSI would build tens of thousands of times more training environments: larger, longer, more open-ended ones, including simulated economies of millions of AIs learning to co-found companies and backtests over all of recorded history, such as rediscovering general relativity from a corpus that ends in 1700 or predicting the Cuban missile crisis from newspapers up to 1962.

He points to transfer already visible at a tiny fraction of that scale: coding agents dropped into projects they have never seen orient themselves and deliver ambitious work, and models have plainly improved at abstract research help far from code or math. His deeper argument is evolution. Natural selection, an optimization process with the crude verifier of whether genes got passed on, turned savannah survival tasks into a general-purpose learner that writes biographies and designs semiconductors. Deliberate training on tasks much closer to today's world, with transfer explicitly optimized for, should go further.

Ramp-up in a brand-new domain would still be fast, he argues, because competence compounds. Napoleon had stacked victory after victory by 27, and Rockefeller was running one of the world's largest refineries by 25. An AI shadowing a Fortune 500 CEO could read every email, Slack message, and document the company has ever produced on its first morning, while copies of it interviewed every employee and major client in parallel by the end of the first day. Feedback that arrives over serial time, like quarterly earnings, cannot be rushed, but he expects top human performance in essentially all domains within months of deployment, around 2028 or 2029 in his median scenario.

The appendix argues that AI research is largely verifiable, bucket by bucket. Pre-training runs on held-out loss: a model can be set the task of minimizing the compute needed to train a GPT-2 sized model to a fixed loss, and even research taste is trainable, for example by rewarding correct predictions of whether an improvement found at 100 million parameters still holds at 10 billion. Automating pre-training compounds through the whole system, because smaller, better base models make every experiment and every automated researcher cheaper, and tighten the loop itself: a giant three-month training run would be obsolete before it finished.

Post-training progress is measurable as the compute, data, or time a new method saves, and as how quickly a model learns inside an environment it was never trained on. Anthropic, he notes, already maintains an internal benchmark of around 500 real problems its researchers and engineers solved this year, and every such benchmark doubles as an RL environment for rehearsing the last few months of human progress. Inference efficiency is the most verifiable bucket of all: GPU utilization, KV cache per token, FLOPs per forward pass, latency. Underlying everything is sample efficiency, how much data a capability takes, which is measurable in several independent ways. He calls it machine learning's equivalent of a Millennium Prize problem, deep and important, but verifiable, and mathematics has just shown what happens to problems like that. He also cites a recent result from the lab Magic, matching leading open-source base models on half the compute used to train the original GPT-3, as evidence that pre-training is nowhere near its limits.

He then takes the strongest objections in turn. His own long-standing one was continual learning: today's models start every session on their first day and cannot accumulate on-the-job know-how. He has softened for two reasons. Scaling in-context learning may yield effectively months-long sessions without a new architecture. And online learning is itself an ML problem with verifiable proxies, which smarter models might crack even while remaining amnesiacs, the way a runner doing cartwheels who keeps pace with Usain Bolt might still win the race. He keeps real weight on the risk: it is part of why a quarter of his probability sits beyond 2032.

On taste and paradigms, the objection is that choosing which hill to climb is not verifiable, and future paradigm shifts may need proxies nobody has designed yet. His replies: the transformer was invented to beat translation benchmarks that already existed, and reasoning models were measured against pre-existing benchmarks, so old proxies have repeatedly captured paradigm leaps. And paradigm-agnostic outer-loop tests exist regardless: Millennium problems solved, general relativity rediscovered from pre-1905 data, unseen games mastered.

On data, he cites research he did with Princeton student Jerry Han: over the last six years, more than three times as much algorithmic progress came from data improvements as from model improvements. His answer is that data improvement is itself ordinary AI research, a matter of which filtering and synthesis techniques measurably lower held-out loss, and it is automating quickly. DeepSeek's latest paper, he notes, credits essentially all of its post-training gains to systematic improvements in the scale, diversity, and verifiability of synthesized data and environments, produced by automated pipelines the model is iteratively trained to improve.

On experiment compute, the objection is that a swarm dilutes each researcher's slice. Lab compute has more than quadrupled every year, so by the end of 2027 OpenAI could give 10,000 agents each enough compute to run a GPT-3 sized training run every day, while the original transformer took three days on eight GPUs that would cost about fifty dollars to rent today. And a compute-bound lab with abundant labor would work differently: far more small, careful experiments and ablations, agents reading every rollout and rebuilding broken environments on the spot, teams spending subjective decades designing one pooled experiment, and a better science of learning from small runs, of the kind that let the GPT-4 team predict final loss from models trained with ten thousand times less compute. He closes by promising a follow-up essay on superintelligence itself.

Open questions

  1. The timeline rests on AI research being nearly as verifiable as mathematics, yet Dwarkesh concedes that taste, choosing the hill and the next paradigm, is the least checkable part of research, and it is the part that decided past breakthroughs. If the swarm automates everything except choosing the hill, has the bottleneck moved rather than disappeared?
  2. Both of his scenarios, the spark and the software-only singularity, assume frontier lab compute keeps more than quadrupling every year. If compute growth, chip supply, or energy slows first, which parts of his argument survive, and which collapse?
  3. His transfer case leans on simulated economies and historical backtests teaching real-world judgment. What early, pre-deployment evidence would show that an AI trained this way can actually run a company or win a court case, rather than merely scoring well inside the simulation?

Baron Capital · Elon Musk and Ron Baron on Terafab, Starship, and data centers in space

Elon Musk & Ron Baron: Terafab, Starship & Data Centers in Space | Baron Investment Conference 2026

On Baron Capital, "Elon Musk & Ron Baron: Terafab, Starship & Data Centers in Space | Baron Investment Conference 2026" is a conversation from the 33rd Annual Baron Investment Conference in New York. Ron Baron interviews from the stage, Elon Musk joins remotely, and it is Musk's fourth appearance at the conference. The talk runs from chip fabrication to data centers on Earth and in orbit, Starship, Starlink, China, and what Musk wants his legacy to be.

Baron opens by framing Musk as a builder working at a scale nobody else attempts: factories of ten or twenty million square feet, and now a planned chip plant of about one hundred million square feet, the Terafab. His question is why no one else tries anything like it, when the United States needs both silicon and power brought back home.

Musk first corrects the premise. TSMC is building a large collection of fabs in Arizona, some already operational, and Samsung has operational fabs in Texas, including one dedicated to Tesla's AI5 and AI6 chip designs. But measured against Asia, American fabrication is tiny: even under the best assumptions for every announced fab, he puts it at perhaps ten percent of Asia's output, optimistically. That concentration carries geopolitical risk, he argues, and if the risk materializes and the pieces to build chips at scale are not in America, there may be very few chips at all.

The Terafab is his answer, and its name comes from its goal: a terawatt a year of AI chip production. The rest of the world will make roughly twenty to twenty-five gigawatts of AI chips this year, and perhaps thirty-five to forty-five gigawatts next year, so a terawatt means about thirty times next year's global production, built essentially for his own companies, in one plant whose output Baron compares to the GDP of a sizable country. Musk agrees it might crack the top ten. The cost, he says, will be a trillion dollars, probably more, spent in phases over time. If it fails it will look crazy, he says, and if it succeeds it will look audacious.

He also argues the industry leaves large efficiency gains on the table. A wafer in a fab spends more than half its time simply being transported or waiting, and the share of time in which something useful is actually happening to its atoms may be closer to twenty percent. Incumbents do not chase those gains, he says, because fabs cost so much capital that nobody wants to risk a recipe that already works. Progress comes in small, safe steps.

Tesla's approach is to attack the iteration cycle. A research chip lab at Gigafactory Texas in Austin will do logic, memory, packaging, and lithography under one roof, so a new chip can be designed, made, and tested in perhaps a month instead of a year. The production Terafab itself will sit in Grimes County, Texas, near Texas A&M. Progress in any technology, Musk says, is set by how many iterations you can run and how much you gain per iteration, and an order of magnitude faster iteration compounds into an order of magnitude faster advance.

Baron spends part of the conversation on the culture behind that speed. Intel's chief executive told him that visiting executives tour his Oregon facility for ninety minutes and leave, while Musk stayed four and a half hours, interviewing one engineer after another and questioning every concept. A production head who came from Ford told Baron that a change at Ford required six levels of approval, while at Tesla he just walks upstairs to Elon. Musk lives in an Airstream beside the Memphis site, next to a power plant his team built to over a gigawatt, heading toward one and a half to two gigawatts, in about a year rather than the usual five. He walks the entire site several times a day and insists on doing some of the manual work himself, pulling power cables, running fiber, working switches, so he is not numb to the difficulty of the jobs.

On data centers, Baron lays out the industry's standard economics: about two and a half years and roughly sixty billion dollars to build a gigawatt, with developers needing long leases to raise the capital. His companies instead build for their own use, for cars, robots, rockets, and Grok, in eight or nine months at about thirty-five billion dollars a gigawatt, and rent the spare capacity to others at something like fifty billion dollars a year until they need it themselves. Other people's finished data centers, he notes, sit dark for lack of power.

Musk then puts numbers on SpaceX's growth. The company is tracking to an annualized revenue rate above one hundred billion dollars in December. Next December, he thinks, the annual recurring revenue rate will exceed five hundred billion dollars, and a trillion is no longer out of the question, though he pegs its likelihood at only about two percent and rising. He stresses that this is a guess about what will occur, not a promise. Bringing compute online at scale is insanely hard, he says, and the country's construction capacity is the real bottleneck: too many people issuing purchase orders to the one person actually digging, and partly finished data centers spread across the country as a result. SpaceX and Tesla, he says, may be the two best companies at physical hardware outside China, and Tesla perhaps the best in the world.

China runs through the conversation. Tesla was allowed one hundred percent ownership of its China factory, an exception to the usual forty-nine percent cap, after years of meetings in which officials, as Musk tells it, yelled at him to take a local partner. His reply was that a baby company should not get married. China now has roughly three times America's power output, trending toward four times, and industrial capacity tracks electricity output to a first approximation. Its chip industry is behind for now but moving fast. The only way to compete, Musk argues, is to take intelligence to space.

That is where the conversation turns to orbital data centers. Satellites carrying an Nvidia GPU and a Google TPU have already been launched and are working in space, he says. The first StarMind computers, co-designed with Nvidia, should start launching around the third quarter of next year, ramping through 2028 and reaching very large volume in 2029. Solar panels in space face the sun constantly and get about thirty percent more power than on the ground, with almost no battery needed. And there is a hard ceiling on Earth: average American power consumption is just over five hundred gigawatts, so adding two hundred gigawatts a year of AI compute on the ground would mean growing the entire national grid by forty percent annually, which he calls simply not feasible.

Starship is the unlock for all of it. Falcon 9 is only about eighty percent reusable, and its unrecovered upper stage costs roughly as much as a medium-sized jet, thrown away on every flight. Starship is designed for full and rapid reusability: the tower arms, the chopsticks, catch the booster and set it back on the launch pad, which in principle allows a booster to refly once an hour and supports more than ten thousand launches a year. Musk expects the cost of a kilogram to orbit, eighteen thousand five hundred dollars in 2010, to fall below one hundred dollars.

The hardest remaining problem is the heat shield. No one has ever built a truly reusable orbital heat shield; the space shuttle's needed nine months of repair between flights. Starship test ships have made precise soft landings at sea, on trajectories where a tower would have caught them, and Musk says that with Ship 42, in about a week, they will try the first tower catch of a ship, once they are confident enough not to damage the tower.

Starlink is growing into the same story. About eleven thousand satellites are up, version three satellites carry ten times the capability of version two and will be launched in ten times the numbers, and next generation laser links jump from one hundred gigabits per second to ten terabits. Citing Cloudflare, Musk says agentic AI traffic already exceeds human internet traffic and could be a thousand times larger within five years, an estimate he thinks is low. At one hundred gigawatts a year of AI compute in space, even a much cheaper price per unit of compute stacks into a trillion dollars a year of new revenue, year after year, because the satellites stay up. If legacy telecom companies cannot meet demand with their spectrum, he adds, SpaceX could simply acquire one and use the spectrum ten times more effectively.

Baron asks why anyone needs that much intelligence. Musk's answer is that the demand for digital superintelligence will come from digital superintelligence itself. He predicts it will saturate human needs: universal high income, medical care better than the best available today, and goods made almost as side projects. Digital AI alone, he guesses, adds twenty to thirty percent to the global economy, on the order of twenty-five trillion dollars, roughly doubling white collar productivity. Robotics multiplies it further: a humanoid robot works one hundred sixty-eight hours a week and could deliver at least five times a human's weekly output, so twenty to thirty million robots could match the entire goods and services output of the United States. The robots would cost about as much as a car at first and less over time, and would probably be rented, not sold.

Baron also raises the politics of data centers, asking whether the companies should subsidize host communities, holding down utility bills or funding schools and hospitals. Musk says the industry has explained itself badly, but insists the opposition fades where the data centers actually are. In Memphis, he says, restaurants and hotels are full, pay runs at up to twice the prevailing wage, schools have been helped, and tax revenue is tremendous. The unpopularity, he argues, lives mostly in places that do not have one.

In an aside on OpenAI, Musk repeats his long-standing grievance: he started it as an open source nonprofit and it has become the polar opposite, a closed, maximum profit entity. Anthropic, he notes, was founded by seven people who left OpenAI, and when he himself left OpenAI he declined an ownership interest, saying he could not see how a commercial interest in a nonprofit made sense after he had supplied nearly all of its early capital, its name, and its key recruits.

Baron closes by asking how Musk wants people to think of him when he is eighty-three, Baron's own age. Musk answers that perception is out of his hands, but he wants the reality to be that he made a significant contribution to the furtherance of civilization.

Open questions

  1. The Terafab aims at roughly thirty times next year's projected global AI chip output, at a cost above a trillion dollars, primarily to supply Musk's own companies. What demand actually absorbs a terawatt a year of chips, and what happens to chip prices and to TSMC and Samsung if the plant reaches even a fraction of that scale?
  2. The orbital data center plan depends on Starship achieving rapid full reusability, including a first tower catch of a ship that Musk says is about a week away and a heat shield no one has ever made reusable. How much of the StarMind timeline, penciled in for launches starting in late 2027 and large volume in 2029, survives if the heat shield takes years longer?
  3. Musk predicts digital superintelligence will saturate human needs and produce universal high income, while also saying the demand for intelligence will come from intelligence itself. If the machines are both the main producers and the main customers, what anchors human income and ownership in that economy?
