David Senra · Scott Kupor on a16z, venture capital, and fixing the government

a16z's First Employee on the History of a16z, Venture Capital & Fixing the Government | Scott Kupor

On David Senra, the episode "a16z's First Employee on the History of a16z, Venture Capital & Fixing the Government | Scott Kupor" is a conversation with Scott Kupor, who joined Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz in 2009 as the first employee of Andreessen Horowitz and spent sixteen and a half years there as a managing partner. He now directs the U.S. Office of Personnel Management, the agency that serves as the human resources and talent office for the federal government's roughly two million civilian employees. The conversation moves in two arcs: how a venture capital firm was built from three people into an institution of about seven hundred, and what Kupor is trying to change about how the federal government hires, rates, promotes, and retires its own people.

The founding nearly did not include him. Kupor had worked with Andreessen and Horowitz at Loudcloud and Opsware, which Hewlett-Packard bought in 2007, and he was living in North Carolina when the two began planning the firm in the summer of 2008, as Bear Stearns vanished and Lehman Brothers collapsed. He hesitated for months, weighing a stable job at HP against a startup fund in a financial crisis, and only committed around Christmas. The standing joke, he says, is that a faster yes might have made the firm Andreessen Kupor Horowitz. His official status stayed first employee rather than co-founder, and there was no job description: the founders would invest, and he would figure out everything else a firm needs.

The early days were that literal. His first act was a capital call, the request that investors wire money for an investment, and he sent it from his personal Gmail account because he did not yet have a firm email address. An investor wrote back demanding to know who he was, then called Andreessen and Horowitz to report that someone was impersonating them and shaking him down. Before that came office furniture and bank accounts; after it, hiring finance staff so the mistake would not repeat, and then hiring the operators who would become the firm's services arm. The firm grew from three people to roughly one hundred in its first year.

The model came from Hollywood. Michael Ovitz, who had been on the Loudcloud board, built CAA by disaggregating the agent's job: instead of one agent handling everything for an actor, a client who walked into CAA met a table of specialists, each expert in one domain. Andreessen and Horowitz applied the same idea to venture capital, which then ran on the one-to-one model of a connector like John Doerr, who personally knew every salesperson, chief financial officer, and political contact a founder might need. A firm, they argued, could institutionalize that: hire investors for their domain expertise and surround them with full-time professionals running talent networks, business development, government relations, and the rest. When founders pitched, the firm answered with a reverse pitch: not just a board member, but the whole phalanx.

Marketing was the other break with tradition. Venture firms did not market, Kupor says, because for decades capital was scarce and entrepreneurs came to them; the balance of power made marketing unnecessary. A new firm with no track record could not compete on the incumbents' terms, so it competed on a new category instead, capital plus services, and told that story loudly and before it was fully built. Rivals on Sand Hill Road complained that the newcomers talked a big game, which sounded to Kupor like a description of any entrepreneur creating a category: you have to narrate it, and then you have to deliver.

Delivery came fast. Within months of the first fund closing, the firm joined Silver Lake to buy Skype out of eBay, a buyout from a firm that had promised its investors early stage venture investing, and one shadowed by a dispute over the underlying intellectual property that had scared off other buyers. About eighteen months later Microsoft bought Skype for four times the purchase price, and the firm returned roughly half of its first fund to investors. Martin Casado's networking company Nicira also exited early in the first fund, and Casado later joined the firm as a partner. The wins landed while established firms were hunkered down over their portfolios in the crisis, and the second fund they enabled supplied the fee stream that paid for the services build-out. Andreessen and Horowitz took no salary at the start so the money could go to hiring.

Kupor's own role stayed operational for years, by his account an honest fit: early stage investing, he says, demands a granular feel for technology he does not have, and in a room of ten database pitches that depth is the whole job. About ten years in, the firm reversed its structure, decentralizing from one centralized organization, where nearly everything reported to him, into specialist funds, crypto under Chris Dixon, American Dynamism under David Ulevitch, artificial intelligence infrastructure under Casado, with the services teams feathered into each vertical and only legal, finance, compliance, and investor relations kept central. The reorganization shrank his domain, which stung, and also freed him to invest. From 2019 he helped build the firm's later stage venture fund with David George, leaning toward health care, where judgment is about operations and the road from one to infinity rather than zero to one. He left with about fifteen investments and ten board seats.

Policy, though, predates venture capital in his life. He did health care policy research in high school, worked at Stanford for Alan Garber, now the president of Harvard, on prostate cancer screening economics, and later for Jay Bhattacharya, who now runs the National Institutes of Health. With his children grown and Andreessen and Horowitz's blessing, he took the OPM job as a return to that first interest. His theory of the work is deliberately simple: an organization is a collection of people lined up around a set of objectives, and performance follows from having the right people and incentives that reward the behavior the objectives require. Government, he argues, fails mainly on the second term.

The incentive structure he describes is a compliance culture organized around avoiding blame. A senior report told him early on that their job was to keep him out of the news: no congressional hearings, no critical audit reports. The safe strategy that follows is to do nothing new, which is why obvious improvements sit untouched for decades. Kupor's counter is what he calls permissioned risk, risk deliberately sized to the stakes. Where failure can kill people, tolerance should be near zero; where the work is processing retirement applications and tax refunds, the upside of automation dwarfs the cost of an occasional error. His own license to push this, he told the president in his interview, is that he wants no other job when this one ends, so a bad hearing costs him nothing he is protecting.

Performance management is where the incentives show up in numbers. Federal employees are rated on a five point scale, and historically 99.7 percent have been rated three or better, meaning only 0.3 percent fall below expectations, a result he calls mathematically impossible in a workforce of two million. At the top, roughly ninety percent of the roughly seven thousand senior executives receive one of the two highest ratings, so bonuses are spread across nearly everyone and motivate no one, while weak performers face no consequences beside their high performing colleagues. His new rules cap the top rating at no more than forty percent of a workforce and reserve at least sixty percent of the compensation pool for that group. At the same time, his team is eliminating time in grade, the tenure requirement that blocks a promotion for at least a year regardless of performance, so advancement can follow merit instead.

The reason this matters now is demographic. Seven percent of the federal workforce is under thirty, against twenty three percent of the overall U.S. workforce, and forty four percent is over fifty, against about a third nationally; if nothing changes, half the workforce retires within five to ten years with no bench behind it. Kupor's recruiting pitch discards the old promise of lifetime employment, which he calls the worst pitch imaginable for anyone under thirty, and offers a two or three year tour instead: work on genuinely hard problems at NASA, the Energy Department, or elsewhere, build rare experience, and then stay or leave for the private sector with the government's help. The flagship is Tech Force, which aims to hire one thousand software engineers, artificial intelligence experts, and data scientists for two year terms at the GS-14 level, a senior individual contributor grade. Entrants take actual skills assessments, third party coding tests rather than the self-rated questionnaires federal hiring has relied on, and some forty private companies partner on training during the term and a job fair at the end. The model he cites is Israel's military cyber units, whose alumni every firm wants to hire.

His test case for skills based hiring is a nineteen year old software engineer who dropped out of college to join the DOGE effort and works now on the National Design Studio's redesign of federal websites. Under standard rules, lacking a degree and five years of experience, the government would never have hired him, or would have hired him only at the entry level GS-5 grade and a salary Silicon Valley would multiply by ten. Kupor's view is that such a person should enter at GS-14 or GS-15, the top grades, because demonstrated merit says he can perform there, and that job descriptions across government are being rewritten on that principle.

The headcount backdrop is the DOGE reduction, which took the civilian workforce from about 2.3 million when the administration began to about 2 million now, its smallest share of the total workforce since 1960. Kupor, who was nominated in December and planned with the DOGE team before his confirmation kept him sidelined, frames his own job as institutionalizing the change: something drastic was needed to turn a tanker of that size, and his task is the durable part, making agencies state what they are actually working on and accept that some of it should stop, since government only ever adds.

His Exhibit A is retirement. Until last year, a retiring federal employee's paperwork traveled by mail through their agency and a separate payroll office to an underground limestone mine in Boyers, Pennsylvania, where some eight hundred OPM staff read paper files, computed annuities by hand, and took anywhere from three to twelve months to produce a first check, mailing forms back for a missing signature at a month's delay each time. The mine still holds about four hundred million pages of records dating to the 1950s, fetched by runners whenever a retiree's circumstances change. The process is now fully electronic: paper intake ended on June 30, the first iteration has cut processing time by more than half, and the commitment is a complete retirement check within fifteen days of a clean electronic application. Room sized scanners are digitizing the entire archive, which should eventually let the storage mine close. In parallel, his office is consolidating the government's one hundred twenty separate human resources computer systems into one, which he says cuts the annual cost from about two billion dollars to about forty million, and gives the government, for the first time, a single continuous record of an employee's service.

The parts connect, in his telling, into one loop: tell a compelling story and talented people will come for a few years rather than a lifetime; give them a culture where ratings mean something, promotion follows merit, and a sized risk is permitted; and those people will modernize the systems everyone else experiences. The recruiting starts at earlycareers.gov and a tour of about forty college campuses, and his closing offer to a young listener is direct: send him a message, and they will find a way to make something work.

Open questions

  1. The a16z services machine was funded by management fees that early wins like Skype made possible, and it scaled because the firm could pay market salaries for operators. OPM has no equivalent engine: it must fund assessments, digitization, and a technologist corps out of appropriations. What sustains the build-out when the initial push meets a budget cycle or a change of administration?
  2. Capping top ratings at forty percent assumes managers will differentiate honestly once the rules change. The old system was also a set of rules, and managers gamed it into meaninglessness. What in the new design prevents ratings from quietly re-inflating, especially once Kupor, who says he wants nothing afterward, has left?
  3. Tech Force asks elite engineers to accept below market pay for two years in exchange for training, mission, and a partner run job fair at the end. If the program works as designed, its partner companies will hire away much of each cohort. Does the government then gain a durable alumni network and a recruiting brand, or does it mostly subsidize talent development for the firms it partners with?

Nicholas Thompson · Will AI help democracy or dictators, with Daron Acemoglu

Will AI Help Democracy—or Dictators? - With MIT Economist Daron Acemoglu

On Nicholas Thompson, the episode "Will AI Help Democracy—or Dictators? - With MIT Economist Daron Acemoglu" is a conversation from Thompson's show The Most Interesting Thing in AI with Daron Acemoglu, the MIT economist and Nobel laureate whose new book, "What Happened to Liberal Democracy?", argues that the West made the wrong choices with automation and social media, and is at risk of repeating them with AI. Thompson, the CEO of The Atlantic, opens with the question that runs through the whole hour: why did modern technology, expected to foster democracy, end up mainly empowering dictatorships?

Acemoglu starts with the state of liberal democracy itself. His book opens with Viktor Orbán in 1989, then a white knight for liberalism in Hungary, now an illiberal leader who has since lost power. The lesson he draws is not comforting. Leaders like Orbán can be beaten at the ballot box, especially when an opponent comes from broadly the same movement and can say he understands the grievances that put them there. The bigger mistake, he argues, was made by Democrats in 2020, who treated Trump as a unique aberration. Biden then failed to deliver on voice, shared prosperity, and effective public services, and voters put Trump back in power in 2024 despite the guardrails around him having been removed. Liberal democracy, in his account, survives only if it delivers three things: non-corrupt, effective government, a real voice for people, and shared prosperity.

Technology enters the story through inequality. His research with Pascual Restrepo attributes between 50 and 70 percent of the growth in wage gaps between education groups to automation. Digital technology and globalization together built a post-industrial society that shifted work toward cognitive, white collar tasks, weakened unions, raised capital's share of national income, and concentrated extraordinary wealth in a small number of tech companies employing relatively few people. Inequality rose within rich and poor countries alike, even as poorer countries converged toward richer ones.

On AI's economic future, he is candid about uncertainty but firm on one point. AI as currently built is chiefly an automation technology, and its first-order effect over the next decade will be to raise capital's share of income and squeeze labor further. That, he says, is the part he is most certain about. Who captures the money is far less clear. Open weight models and distillation may prevent the frontier labs from collecting the rents their spending assumes, which he would welcome as a route to cheap, widely available AI, while warning that it could also set up a market correction if the huge investments fail to pay off. He also argues the United States is putting its eggs in the wrong basket: nearly all the energy goes to foundation models, while China invests far more in the application layer, integrating AI and robotics into production. The application layer is where he expects the productivity gains to live, and it needs engineers, of whom the United States has a shortage.

Asked which jobs go first, he picks customer service representatives over mid-level managers, and he shares Thompson's list of durable work: jobs built on face-to-face interaction, skilled work done with the hands, engineering in the broad sense, and human creativity. He does not expect true human-level AI across a diverse range of tasks within the next ten years, which is why he thinks innovation and creativity will remain human activities.

The book's most contested argument concerns what he calls social engineering. As the college-educated grew richer and more socially segregated, living, working, and marrying within their own group, they captured the commanding heights of the economy and the center-left parties that once belonged to the working class. Culture became the glue holding that diverse coalition together, and its values were increasingly imposed from the top down, through schools, employers, and rules about acceptable language. The backlash was then harvested by right-wing media and social media, and the result was a collapse of trust that destroyed people's sense of voice. Brexit was his wake-up call: after the referendum, acquaintances in British media said it had been a mistake to ask the people at all, which to him showed how completely the elite had abandoned democracy. Liberal ideas, he insists, do not need to be imposed. They can win on their merits, more slowly, in open debate.

Social media itself is not inherently anti-democratic, he argues. It could have been a democratizing force, and in Taiwan, under digital minister Audrey Tang, it largely was, because the country deliberately built platforms for participation, referendums, and scrutiny of politicians. The failed attempts of the 2010s, including one of Thompson's own, made a common mistake: they tried to fix discourse purely online. Anything that works, he thinks, has to blend the online and the offline, rooted in real communities.

The balance of power question cuts the other way for autocracies. Digital surveillance, he argues, was central to Xi Jinping's strategy of letting China's middle class consume more while clamping down on it politically, and China's AI industry grew up coupled to surveillance data and government procurement. Thompson presses him on Michael Walzer's thought experiment of a pill in the water that would make a whole country love liberal democracy. Acemoglu opposes the pill, as Walzer did, because it erases the community that makes people who they are. He would accept a softer version for AI: models that join the open debate honestly, offer counterarguments, and leave the choice to the individual, rather than secretly nudging people toward approved values.

His positive program is pro-worker AI. Following J.C.R. Licklider's 1960 vision of humans and computers coupled together, he wants AI used as an information technology that gives workers new capabilities and creates new tasks: tools that help electricians, nurses, teachers, and plumbers do harder work, built with the people who hold the tacit knowledge and the data. This, he stresses, is directly opposed to the industry's AGI agenda, which assumes humans soon become unnecessary. Getting there means convincing a large share of the talented, well-meaning people now flowing into Silicon Valley, plus policy, data markets, and a media that does its job. He faults that media for swinging between worshipping tech founders and panicking about killer robots, and for never forcing the democratic debate AI deserves. His regulatory agenda is specific: for social media, abolish algorithmic feeds in favor of chronological ones, since algorithms have no free-speech rights; for AI, replace today's reactive, panic-driven guardrails with a proactive agenda set by public debate. Given an unlimited budget, he would build prototypes of pro-worker AI, as he already does on a shoestring, developing pro-teacher tools in the developing world. One ten-thousandth of what Anthropic or OpenAI spends, he says, would be revolutionary.

Open questions

  1. Acemoglu expects AI, built as an automation technology, to raise capital's share of income and squeeze labor over the next decade. If open models simultaneously make AI cheap for everyone, which of those two forces shows up first in the wage data, and how would we know?
  2. His pro-worker program needs something like half the industry's talent to change direction voluntarily, helped by policy and data markets. Which of those levers actually moves first, and what evidence would show the shift is happening rather than just being talked about?
  3. He wants chronological feeds for social media and a proactive, democratically debated agenda for AI. In a Congress he himself describes as voting with its richest constituents, who writes that agenda, and what keeps it from becoming another round of the social engineering he criticizes?

Latent Space · Behind Latent Space, with swyx and Galen Gao

Behind Latent Space, ft AIEi Shanghai preview — with swyx and Galen Gao

On Latent Space, the episode "Behind Latent Space, ft AIEi Shanghai preview — with swyx and Galen Gao" is a special edition recorded ahead of the first AI Engineer conference in China. Galen Gao interviews swyx, the podcast's host and the founder of the AI Engineer conference series, about the Shanghai edition, which opens the day after this episode was published and is run by a local partner, Standard Partners. The conversation doubles as a retrospective: how Latent Space started, how the term AI engineer was coined, and what swyx has learned running conferences and advising startups.

His own path runs through finance. A fifth-generation Chinese Singaporean, he spent about eight years in trading, investment banking, and hedge funds before moving into tech, working at Amazon and several startups, and going independent four years ago. Today he runs the conference company and the podcast, and advises startups, most substantially Cognition, which acquired his earlier startup vehicle and where he worked for six months before stepping back to an adviser role as the conference business grew. Fireworks, Modal, and Supabase are also on his list.

The principle he credits for the whole career is learning in public. Finance, he argues, is zero-sum: knowledge you share loses value. Tech is positive-sum: knowledge shared tends to gain value, which is why engineers open source their work and admit their mistakes in public. Bored in a software role at Two Sigma under a boss who would not let him do the work he wanted, he poured his energy into writing and speaking, and discovered the side benefit: when you learn in public, you effectively choose your own boss. He is blunt about the flip side, especially for credential-focused cultures. Working hard entitles nobody to win. You have to work hard and be good, and aim the effort at substantial things.

Latent Space itself began as a blog when Stable Diffusion appeared, before ChatGPT. Working at a startup that was going nowhere, swyx started writing up what he was learning about AI, and a post analyzing Stable Diffusion caught the attention of Stability AI chief executive Imad Mostaque. Events and a hackathon followed, and it was at that hackathon that the AI engineer idea landed: a nontechnical attendee asked what she could do in AI, and he realized the honest answer was very little next to an engineer, because engineers were about to direct many AIs at once. Building harnesses around models did not require a research doctorate, but the work had no respected name. The alternatives floated at the time, AI tinkerer, LLM engineer, applied AI engineer, all described activities. He chose a job title instead, reasoning from the identity principle he attributes to James Clear: give people a title they aspire to hold and a community forms around it. The hot topics inside the role have rotated since, from retrieval and context management to coding agents and harness engineering, and he sees AI architect emerging as the company-level counterpart. His test for who counts as an engineer is simple: the person who gets woken at 4 a.m. to fix it.

China ran the same experiment in parallel. Gao describes how China's largest AI community, WayToAI, grew out of Stable Diffusion and ComfyUI enthusiasts at exactly the same moment, sharing documents and use cases on Lark until it reached roughly a million people learning together. That symmetry is part of the case for Shanghai: both ecosystems were built by people learning in public, and each is curious about the other.

The flagship AI Engineer World's Fair drew more than 7,000 people in person this year, with about a million and a half watching online, across some 30 tracks, and every talk and workshop is published free. He has also transcribed and cataloged the entire archive, exposed it through MCP and as a downloadable dataset, describing the ambition as a Wikipedia of AI. Conferences, in his telling, are neutral ground in a way companies cannot be: some Chinese labs sit on the US entity list and cannot officially take meetings with American firms, but people still run into each other in the same building, and he jokes that his top KPI is the number of marriages and babies the events have produced.

Expansion runs on a franchise model borrowed from JSConf. Local partners license the brand, the channel, and the audience under three conditions: do not abuse the name, publish everything, and maintain a code of conduct. Paris came first, and its organizers were later acquired into Mistral. Singapore followed, with India and Japan in progress. China is the hard case, because US-China restrictions mean swyx cannot own or operate there, so the Shanghai edition belongs entirely to the local team. His analogy is McDonald's: he is the franchisor, they are McDonald's China.

The longest segment revisits his Kill My SaaS contest. Conference budgets run from three to ten million dollars, and roughly three hundred thousand dollars per conference was going to software he describes as slow, ugly, and never quite right, with feature requests queued for quarters. So he took one tool his team was about to pay forty thousand dollars for and offered a ten thousand dollar prize for an open source clone, built with coding agents and tailored to his company. Sixty-nine submissions arrived in a week; about five were good enough that he would have paid for them. The winner replaced a speaker-review workflow of endless one-by-one clicking with a single reorderable table, a view the vendor would have taken six months to consider. The AI judge in the contest only screened for valid submissions; humans chose the winners, because he could not specify what he wanted until he had seen seventy versions. He compares the format to 99designs, the contest site where the Latent Space logo itself came from four hundred entries, and argues there should be a 99designs for SaaS: a service that runs the whole cloning contest for a business owner who lacks his audience.

The underlying claim is aimed at mid-tier SaaS. Models, he says, are now very good at clicking through every screen of a product and replicating it, so a vendor with fifty customers paying forty thousand dollars each can watch the business evaporate once those customers notice. Salesforce-scale incumbents are safe; everyone in the middle must either own a vertical as its leading agent, his examples are Harvey and OpenEvidence, or own specialized data. For business owners, though, he calls this a golden age. He runs his own versions of a scheduling link tool, a file drive, a code host, a chat tool, and a document-signing tool, and because the last two are his, a signed document lands directly in the right folder, an integration the commercial product never offered. Maintenance is a real cost, he concedes, but coding agents absorb much of it, and for a database with a form on top he will happily trade an occasional bad day for total customizability.

Gao also asks about Jev, the subject of a recent episode with its creator, Diego. The idea swyx endorses is what Diego calls system 1 models: instead of one large model sitting in a chat window at the edge of dumb software, a small model is placed inside the path of ordinary code, making choices and estimating probabilities as casually as an if statement, so software is smarter at every point rather than at one point. Reliability matters more than speed, which is why the company is called Typesafe. Diego succeeded where earlier small-model advocates had not, swyx argues, because of his credibility as a co-author of the work behind ChatGPT, a framing people could repeat, and showmanship, capped by a demo of the model playing Doom.

On advising, he has worked with some thirty-five companies and treats that sample size as the product: advice tested at one company is an anecdote, while patterns that survive thirty-five are close to universal. Engineer and researcher founders, including the Chinese founders Gao works with who are trying to go global, are typically strong technically and weak at go-to-market, and their biggest mistake is sequencing. Marketing brought in the night before a launch can change nothing; brought in before the product is specced, it shapes what gets built. His core principle is show, don't tell: a working demo beats any deck. Taste, he says, is the ability to narrow a thousand possibilities quickly and cheaply to the one that afterwards looks obvious. The iPhone's touchscreen is his historical example; Cursor is his current one, since you know you have taste when the big labs copy what you shipped, and he puts the legal tool Legora in the same class.

NextEra Energy CEO: Powering AI and Nuclear's Comeback | Podcast | In Good Company

The fund owns about 1.5 percent of NextEra, worth more than two billion dollars, so this is an owner interviewing one of his largest holdings. The timing is pointed: AI has turned electricity into a strategic bottleneck, and NextEra has just agreed to buy Dominion Energy in what Tangen calls the biggest merger in the industry's history.

Ketchum describes NextEra first of all as a builder. It is, he says, the only American power company fully diversified across the whole energy value chain, building in nearly every state, and over the last twenty years it built more than its next twenty largest peers combined. The pitch is that scale compounds: the company builds cheaper, buys cheaper, operates cheaper, and finances cheaper than rivals, and it plans three steps ahead. Its regulated Florida utility, Florida Power & Light, is his Exhibit A. Customer bills there have fallen about 20 percent in real terms over twenty years and sit 37 percent below the national average, while reliability runs 60 percent better than the national average.

That record is the argument for the Dominion deal. Dominion is a well run company in three growth states, Virginia, North Carolina, and South Carolina, where the binding constraint is affordability. NextEra has committed to bring a thousand new jobs to Virginia, six hundred of its own and four hundred through supplier commitments, and to build a new office tower there as a dual headquarters. The investment program behind all this is enormous. Tangen puts the spending at roughly sixty billion dollars a year between 2027 and 2032, a billion dollars a week, and Ketchum describes it as a fifty state capital investment plan. The bottlenecks are physical: turbines, transformers, solar panels, inverters, batteries. Scale is his answer there too, since the world's largest buyer gets equipment first and cheapest, and NextEra is already buying through 2030.

The reason for the spending is a demand shock nobody in the industry forecast. American power demand was flat for decades and is now expected to grow sixfold by the middle of the 2040s. Ketchum says the AI driver dawned on the company two or three years ago and exploded in the last twenty four months. Data centres get the headlines, but he stresses the demand is broader: reshored factories, LNG terminals, semiconductor plants, transport, and homes all using more electricity. What the hyperscalers need above all is speed to power, and the build times differ sharply by technology: wind in about twelve months, solar in eighteen, battery storage in fifteen, and gas plants in three or four years.

MTS · Richard Ngo on why AI cannot reliably run a company for six months

AI Cannot Reliably Run a Company for Six Months | Richard Ngo

On MTS, the episode "AI Cannot Reliably Run a Company for Six Months | Richard Ngo" is a conversation with Richard Ngo, an independent AI researcher who previously worked on AGI safety at Google DeepMind and on governance at OpenAI. His headline claim gives the episode its title. Running a company over a long period is, in his account, close to the hardest kind of task for today's models, because a company presents an enormous attack surface: one mistake can be exploited by hackers or rivals, or can land the company in legal trouble severe enough to shut it down. Work like that prizes a level of robustness the models do not currently have, and he doubts they will reliably run a company for six months, profitably and out of legal trouble, any time soon.

The conversation ranges well beyond that claim, starting with timelines. Ngo agrees with the host that much of the field is jumping further than the evidence warrants: people take the fact that labs are automating parts of research and development and treat a rapid, self-sustaining intelligence explosion as close to certain. He is careful, though, not to land on the opposite conclusion. He does not expect the next decade or two to look normal. His default picture is no vertical takeoff and no single moment where recursive self-improvement sends the lines sharply upward, but a world that still becomes much weirder than almost anyone expects, with AI pervasive and AI companies increasingly powerful throughout.

That picture depends on his central terminological argument. AGI, he says, used to serve as a catchall that bundled together human-level AI and superintelligence, partly because people assumed recursive self-improvement would leave little gap between them. The last five years have shown the two are very different, and now the word superintelligence has inherited the catchall role, bundling concepts that will have to be unbundled over the next few years. What replaces the bundle, he predicts, will look spiky. Capabilities will stay jagged for a long time: models are already close to superhuman at mathematics, and he expects them to be superhuman at writing novels reasonably soon, because neither task needs much feedback from the world. Operating a company for months on end is the contrasting case, and it is where the jaggedness bites.

He also separates the capabilities models have from the capabilities anyone can actually get out of them. Some of what current models can do is locked behind an elicitation problem: the field took a year or two to find even simple prompting moves, like asking a model to think step by step. Ngo goes further and suggests alignment and elicitation may become hard to distinguish, because a model may only display certain capabilities when it trusts whoever is asking. He would not be surprised, he says, if Claude held something like a judgment of Anthropic itself, and if how trustworthy or disreputable Anthropic appeared changed which capabilities the model was willing to show.

Much of the episode draws on the sequence of posts Ngo is publishing on LessWrong, a retrospective on the failures of the AI alignment movement over the last decade. His recurring complaint is resolution: the field's core concepts were sketched quickly, yet they now steer decisions at very large companies and will soon steer governments, and they are not up to that standard. Anthropic's alignment faking paper is his example: the behavior Claude showed in it can be read as very aligned or very misaligned depending on definitions, so the label settles nothing. The research he finds robustly valuable is conceptual: specifying what good behavior would actually look like. He notes that Claude has a constitution that has served it well, but he does not know of anyone who has seriously argued for the ideas in it, and that kind of philosophical work is exactly what the field's incentives reward least. Work that depends on access to frontier models, by contrast, will not generalize far, and anything that would generalize from today's frontier would, he argues, generalize from open-weight models too, so being inside a lab is not the advantage it is assumed to be.

On lab politics, Ngo is blunt. Effective altruism was historically conciliatory toward the labs for no good reason, mostly out of conflict aversion and fear of challenging prestigious companies, so the newer willingness to resign, speak out, and pressure labs, of the kind Robert Wiblin of the 80,000 Hours podcast has lately argued for, is a welcome change. What is still missing, he says, is honest analysis of power dynamics. Discussion keeps collapsing into good guys and bad guys, with OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepMind each casting themselves in the hero role against rivals or against China, and the racing logic that follows treats this one contest as exempt from the usual rules because AGI is so powerful that everything is up for grabs.

Asked what people who want AI to go well should actually do, Ngo puts trust before plans. Progress will be bottlenecked, he argues, by whether small groups of people can communicate honestly, trust each other, and coordinate, because that is how influence has compounded so far: the early OpenAI group out-computed DeepMind, and Anthropic then out-computed OpenAI, on the strength of unusually serious internal conversation. Anthropic matches part of that description, but he thinks groups like it cohere around things that function as dogmas, such as "we are the good guys" and "other people would do it anyway." Those are claims the lab cannot actually evaluate, and they leave it unable to reckon with its own influence on the world, including, he says, the fact that many researchers in China are becoming genuinely scared of Anthropic in particular.

The interview closes on general politics, because Ngo was one of the very few people in the rationalist world to publicly back Donald Trump in 2024. He reads the elite reaction to Brexit and to Trump's first election as a class mobilizing against the working class more than as a grounded response to specific policies. The AI community's own urgency made things worse, he argues, because it taught people not to rock the boat on ordinary politics for fear of losing influence, a strategy that left them thinking less clearly about the political situation their AI plans depend on.

  3. If a model's willingness to display capabilities depends on how trustworthy it judges its operator to be, how would anyone outside the lab ever verify what a frontier model can actually do?

How To Go From Researcher to Founder | Startup School

On Y Combinator, the Startup School episode "How To Go From Researcher to Founder | Startup School" is a talk by Francois Chaubard, a visiting partner at YC and a current PhD student who was previously a researcher in Fei-Fei Li's lab and the founder and CEO of Focal. His premise is that some of the most successful companies in the world, including Google, Anthropic, Databricks, and Together AI, were started by founders with PhDs, but that they are the exceptions. Academic-led startups more often fail, because many of the skills a researcher is rewarded for are antithetical, or at least orthogonal, to succeeding as a founder. To become a formidable founder, he argues, an academic has to unlearn instincts beaten in over years in the lab, and in effect kill part of their academic identity. He then breaks the transition down into ten perspective shifts.

The first shift is from vanity metrics to what people want. In academia, he says, you optimize for grades, citation counts, and the number of talks you give, and he quotes Paul Graham's line that the most damaging thing learned in school was learning to get good grades. The founder mindset asks instead what people actually want, and what they will actually pay money for as opposed to what they say they would pay for. That leads to the second shift, interesting versus important. Academics are rewarded for understanding the mechanisms behind something, and that gets you published, but it often has no ramification on anyone's daily life. Founders must work backwards from what is important to their users, and be willing to say: it just works, we do not know exactly why, ship it. As he puts it through Marc Andreessen, the only thing that matters is getting to product market fit.

The third shift concerns novelty. In academia novelty is everything, and a paper that is not completely original is rejected. In the startup world, he argues, copying is often a really good strategy, because pioneers frequently get arrows in their backs. He borrows Joe Lonsdale's distinction between MIT-style startups, which revolve around a brilliant patented black box that nobody actually wants, and Stanford-style startups, which start with a single boring customer problem and iterate on it forever. Instacart did not invent delivering groceries from an online order; countless startups tried before it, and it was simply the most successful at executing, still iterating on that core problem fifteen years later. Novelty, he concludes, is not required, and often not even preferred.

The fourth shift is that there is no finish line. Academia always has a cathartic submission date or final, after which you can rest, while a startup offers no such moment, and researchers make it worse by working on five ideas at once, smearing the peanut butter too thin. Founder mode means waking up every day and cranking on the same problem, without getting sidetracked, for decades. The fifth shift is to get on a plane and talk to people, because finding product market fit is a brute force exercise of iterating quickly and talking to customers constantly, and the people to talk to are those who have the problem, not investors. He illustrates it with his own failure at Focal, which spent three months collecting training data and training models to track people for retailers, only to deliver the finding that shoppers spend a lot of time at the checkout counter and the deli counter at lunchtime. Asked whether they would pay for it, the retailers said absolutely not. He could have labeled a few videos a day by hand and reached the same conclusion, and his advice to AI companies is to start with a human in the loop and validate that customers want the output before spending heavily on compute.

The sixth shift is that hyperbole is okay in a sales pitch, even though it is not okay in a paper. He points to the famous car insurance slogan promising that fifteen minutes could save you fifteen percent or more, a beautifully inexact statement that no ablation study ever supported, and warns that obsessing over rigor in marketing will destroy you. You should never lie, but punchier language is necessary. The seventh is the 70% rule: Jeff Bezos's guideline that most decisions should be made with around 70% of the information you wish you had. Publishing at 70% confidence would never get you into a top conference, but in startups waiting for complete data means a competitor has already launched. In the database wars, Michael Stonebraker had a technically superior product in Quel, but Larry Ellison pushed SQL into the market faster and won. Speed, he says, beats perfect information.

The eighth shift is that you cannot delegate the hard stuff. A startup CEO is responsible for everything: team management, cash runway, hiring, firing, marketing, sales, and product development. Academics who imagine hiring an MBA to do the business side while they keep doing research on the venture capitalists' dime have it backwards, because abstracting yourself away from sales and marketing means you never develop empathy for your customers. The ninth shift is to become a capitalist. Starting a company is the most capitalistic thing you can do, and many academics are deeply uncomfortable with charging people money, winning talent, earning 80% margins, raising capital, and beating the competition, all of which he says you must get comfortable with. The tenth is that nobody cares how smart you are. Talking at length about your own field does not work in sales, product development, or pitching investors. What works is an innate curiosity about people, following Dale Carnegie's observation that you can make more friends in two months by becoming interested in other people than in two years by trying to get other people interested in you.

His summary advice to academics turned founders is the line he says he gives most often: get out of your office, get on a flight, and go meet your users where they live and work, and validate whether people will actually pay you, because your competition is doing exactly that. To quiet the academic brain and grow a founder brain he recommends four books: Spin Selling, High Output Management, The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People, and How to Win Friends and Influence People. He closes with Gandhi: if you want to change the world, start with yourself. You already have the intelligence, he tells his audience. The only question left is, do you have the will?

Sam Witteveen · What is the future of IDEs

What is the Future of IDEs

On Sam Witteveen, the video "What is the Future of IDEs" takes on a question he says he keeps hearing, and keeps asking himself: with coding agents like Claude Code and Codex doing so much of the writing, what is an integrated development environment actually for now? His answer is that the IDE is no longer where code gets written. It is where code gets read, navigated, checked, and approved, and that shift makes it more important, not less, for anyone shipping production software.

The video grew out of a conversation with JetBrains, who reached out for his take and published a blog post arguing that we still need an IDE. Witteveen is open about the arrangement: the video is sponsored by JetBrains, though he stresses it is not a push for any particular product, and he has used JetBrains tools on and off since doing Objective-C development in 2012. He told them IDEs still matter but need to change drastically, and he says they seem to agree. Air is their attempt at an IDE built for a world where agents do much of the writing.

His core observation comes from his own work. Hand an agent a task and it may touch ten or fifteen files at once, and from a terminal alone you cannot really see what it did. That invisibility is fine for a quick idea sketched out on a phone, but if you call yourself a developer, he argues, you still need to know what the code is doing and whether it is right. His own model testing suite, now an extensive piece of software supporting many backends, has been touched by several different agents, each with its own ideas about how it should work, which made the modular layout of the codebase something he has to be able to inspect directly. One practice he picked up from someone at Anthropic is having agents generate HTML documentation and diagrams of how everything is laid out, with real file names he can then open and check in the IDE.

The second shift is that IDEs are merging into places where agents run, not just places where code is viewed. GitHub Copilot was an early version of this, with an agent panel beside the code panels. Witteveen has since experimented with tools like Cmux for running multiple agents, and more recently Herder and T3 Code, and he notes that JetBrains Air also lets you run and orchestrate several agents from inside the IDE rather than being limited to one. What he wants from any of them is the ability to move quickly between the high-level structure of a project and the low-level detail.

Historically, he points out, the IDE was about making typing faster: autocomplete, refactoring, personal snippet libraries, and then early AI autocomplete that could finish a function for you. Agents have ended that era. Typing code is not only no longer the main activity, he says, it is often the bad option. If IDEs were only about typing, they would be in trouble. What a good one actually does is help you read code, navigate it, understand it, and check it.

That is where the bottleneck has moved. An agent can produce a change in a few minutes, but reviewing it can take far longer, especially for whoever is responsible for committing it to production. He cites the pattern at large organizations, including Google, and an earlier interview he did with Logan Kilpatrick: anyone can now write code and submit a pull request, but an engineer on the project has to sign off and own the result. JetBrains, after talking to a range of engineering teams, found the same pattern, and one team measured its pull request time going up, not down, as agents were rolled out. Witteveen would be more worried by the opposite: a team whose review time fell to zero would signal a trust problem solved by not looking. Having one agent review another catches things, he concedes, but it does not remove the need for a person who understands what the system is supposed to do, and who can judge whether a result was reached in a way that stays legal and does not run up huge compute or database bills.

His concrete uses for an IDE today come in four parts. First, diffs: before anything an agent writes gets merged, he wants to see the file structure and what actually changed, which is close to impossible with a wall of text in a terminal. Second, navigation: when an agent changes a function, he wants to jump to its call sites, find usages, and see what implements an interface, because that is how you judge whether a change is safe, for instance whether a piece of code making many database calls does so efficiently. Third, verification: running tests and inspections inside the IDE, checking that the tests themselves test something real rather than trivially passing, and stepping through with a debugger when something looks off. Fourth, documentation, which he calls underrated: agents should write documentation as they write code, developers should review whether it says what the code should be doing and whether the agent quietly changed things nobody asked for, and that reviewed documentation then feeds back to the agents as better context.

That last point cuts both ways, because the IDE's intelligence is useful to the agent as well as the human. An agent that can only search through files with text matching is, in his phrase, working blind, spending its whole context window figuring out what is going on. An agent that can use the IDE's semantic understanding of the codebase gets better context and produces more accurate work, and it saves money in tokens. He adds a warning about the current moment: developers are being spoiled by flat-rate plans in the hundred to five hundred dollar range with very high token allowances, and if the economics shift toward paying per API call, the cost of an agent working blind will matter a great deal more.

His closing summary is blunt. The IDE is where you read the code, where you check it, and where you decide whether it ships. The writing part has gotten cheap; the verification has not. That, he says, is the direction JetBrains is heading with Air: bring the agents you already use into the IDE, so their work can be reviewed and checked in the place built for exactly that.

  3. If token pricing moves away from generous flat-rate plans toward paying per call, how much will IDE-provided context and semantic search actually save, and will that decide which agent tools survive?

YC Root Access (Y Combinator) · Adam Cohen and Andrew Churchill on the right model for each coding task

The Right Model for Each Coding Task

On YC Root Access, the video "The Right Model for Each Coding Task" is a fireside conversation with Adam Cohen and Andrew Churchill, the co-founders of Weave, a Y Combinator Winter 25 company that builds models to measure engineering work. Two announcements frame the talk: Weave recently raised a 13.5 million dollar Series A led by Standard Capital, and it has launched Weave Router, which sends every coding task to the model best suited to that task. Their argument is that the next round of gains in AI coding comes not from a better single model, but from picking the right model for each job instead of sending everything to the biggest one.

Weave's original product measures the work of human engineers and coding agents, and its customers range from small startups to Fortune 100 companies, including Robinhood, PostHog, Telnyx, Extra Space Storage, and Reducto. The router is a natural extension of that measurement business. You plug it into a coding agent such as Claude Code, Codex, Open Code, or Pi with one command, and it decides, prompt by prompt, whether a task should go to a frontier model or to something cheaper, including open models. It is fully open source, with a hosted version and paid Max and Boost plans, and on recent benchmarks the founders claim it reaches frontier-level performance at half the cost and twice the speed.

The obvious objection is quality: a router that quietly sends work to a weaker model could fill a codebase with bugs. Their answer is that measurement is the whole company, so they can check. Their own team runs on the router, sits in the top one percent of teams on Weave's own data, and has seen no increase in bugs or quality issues. The router also optimizes across subscriptions. If you pay for the top Anthropic and OpenAI plans, it burns through those subsidized allowances first and sprinkles in open models where they perform best, so a heavy user goes roughly twice as far on the same spend. The intuition they offer is that you do not always need a bazooka where a handgun will do: exploring a codebase to find where something is implemented does not need the smartest model alive.

A big part of the savings comes from open models, which they describe as not at the frontier but not far behind it, at a cost-performance point nothing else matches. The ones they route the most traffic to are DeepSeek, Kimi, and GLM. Centering a button or implementing a routine backend endpoint is well within their reach, while something like building a machine learning system to classify a billion images still belongs on a frontier model. For hobby projects, they say, open models alone will now get you very far.

Asked how this differs from OpenRouter, they say OpenRouter is really a gateway: it gives access to many models and lets you choose, with useful fallbacks, but the choosing is your job, and its auto-router is not its focus and does not work well on coding benchmarks. Weave's router is trained specifically for coding agents, on hundreds of thousands of real coding agent sessions. Crucially, the training data includes downstream effects: the pull request that resulted, whether it merged, and what happened a month later in production. On top of that they run controlled tests, sending work down one model's path or another's and rewarding the router when the task succeeds. The result, they claim, is a learned map of each model's strengths rather than a set of human guesses.

The company started in the Winter 25 batch doing only the measurement half. Measuring engineering productivity used to mean counting lines of code or pull requests, metrics they dismiss as ways to make executives feel good. Weave instead has models read the code and judge the work itself. The timing was lucky: theirs was the first batch where a significant share of founders said most of their code was written by LLMs, a survey result that caused a stir online, and as Cursor took off, customers began asking what AI was doing to their productivity. Early selling was pure skepticism, overcome by connecting a prospect's own data and showing the picture matched reality. Working with the fastest-growing startups, they say, has kept them roughly six months ahead of the wider market ever since.

The questions engineering managers ask have moved through three phases: how fast are we moving, then is AI actually making us faster, and now how do we use AI more efficiently. The founders map this onto an adoption curve that runs from adoption, to a token-maxing phase of spending as much as possible, to an optimization layer where cost and quality get tuned. Most startups are still token maxing and care more about productivity than cost, while later-stage and private-equity-owned companies are deep into optimization.

Their central metric for that optimization is cost per output: dollars of AI spend per unit of output, where a unit of output is roughly an hour of human engineering work. Someone spending ten thousand dollars a month is getting a bargain at a dollar per hour of work and a terrible deal at a thousand dollars per hour, and the dashboard exists to tell those two apart, surfacing outliers in both directions. The highest-leverage habit they see is parallelization, running many agents at once with guardrails that pull the human in at the right moments. Weave's coach studies every session a team runs and returns daily pointers: restart sessions before the context window makes them expensive, push back on the agent when it is wrong, share the patterns of the top performers. Spreading those habits, through shared skills and codebase layouts agents can work in, is how a whole team gets closer to its best two people.

The frontier of the conversation is the software factory, teams of agents coding autonomously, in some cases overnight. One customer, Uncountable, is comparing its factory's output directly against its humans to decide which work can move to a lights-out setup. The hard part, the founders insist, is validation: an agent will put anything into production, so the factory is only worth what its checking is worth. Weave's own setup is an example of the discipline involved. A custom, token-efficient CLI feeds agents the context they need. Four automated review tools run on every pull request, including Cursor Bugbot and Cubic, each still catching things the others miss. And a homegrown checks system encodes what a senior engineer on the team would call out in review, such as the over-abstraction agents love to produce, and runs those checks on every pull request, because agents, unlike engineers, never learn from being told once. Without that back pressure, they warn, every pull request can be locally logical while the whole codebase becomes globally incoherent.

They close with the future. Keeping up with every model's strengths and weaknesses is getting exhausting, so they expect the router approach to become the default, and they make a stronger claim: a router that truly knows each model's strengths could combine them into something better than any single frontier model. Their advice to an engineer who wants to lead this shift is almost old-fashioned. Do not lose track of what you are actually doing; the best engineers still understand two levels of abstraction below and two above their work, even if the default level has moved up. In another year and a half, they predict, running an engineering team will be an exercise in token allocation, and the winners will be the people with the taste to spot the right problem and the skill to direct a swarm of agents at it.

  1. The panel wants fences expressed as laws and principles, with the agent exercising judgment about its own presumptuousness. When an agent inside its fence still embarrasses its owner, as Mac's did, who grades the judgment call, and can ordinary users audit settings they barely know exist?

  3. If the deepest trust goes to a dark agent that holds what people hide even from themselves, what happens to that trust the first time such an agent is breached, subpoenaed, or simply changes its terms?

Matthew Berman · MIT researchers discover AI can rewrite its own weights

MIT Researchers Discover AI Can Rewrite Its Own Weights

On Matthew Berman, the video "MIT Researchers Discover AI Can Rewrite Its Own Weights" works through a paper by Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang, and Omar Khattab of MIT CSAIL, published November 2, 2026. Their finding is that frontier large language models are surprisingly good at reading and writing the weights of a neural network directly, an approach the authors call Weight-Space Programming.

The background matters here. A neural network is, at bottom, millions or billions of numbers, and those numbers are its weights. Training a model means adjusting the numbers by gradient descent, essentially organized trial and error, until the outputs improve. That process produces a working model without explaining it: nobody can say why a given weight has its value or how the values combine to produce a behavior, which is why trained models are usually described as black boxes.

Weight-space programming changes who touches the weights. Until now, people have pointed language models at text, code, and prompts, while a fixed optimizer handled the weights. In these experiments, a general-purpose frontier model reads a network's raw weights as numbers, with no optimizer and no gradient descent, and it can be asked in natural language to find a hidden trait or a failure inside another model, to fix that failure, or to write a network's weights from scratch.

The first experiments test reading. The researchers trained four small networks that look identical from the outside, one each on the games Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man, and Freeway, then handed the raw weights, about 17,000 numbers as text, to the frontier model used throughout the paper, which the video calls Astra. Given no list of options to choose from, it named all four games correctly, reasoning from structure: a bottom paddle with reward targets above it meant Breakout, a vertical paddle on the left edge meant Pong, a fixed maze with independently moving entities meant Pac-Man, and a safe starting strip below lanes of moving objects meant a road-crossing game.

In the second experiment, three diffusion models were trained to generate circles, squares, and triangles, and Astra identified which shape each model produces from its output weights alone. One nuance stood out to the researchers: Astra pictured every shape as an outline, without the filled interior the models actually generate. They read that mistake as evidence that the model is reading the geometry of the weights themselves rather than mentally simulating what the network would output.

The third experiment removed the easiest clues. Three small models were trained to predict the next character in English, Japanese, and Python. Astra received only the intermediate layers, not the first and last layers that carry the most obvious signals, and it still identified all three languages. It also picked out the internal groupings of weights associated with each language, and when those groupings were measured directly, its descriptions matched.

The fourth experiment moves from reading to repair. A network trained on a physics game kept getting its car stuck at the top of a ramp. Given the weights, a description of the scene, and one recorded episode, Astra explained the failure: the controller was sensitive to velocity, and reversing was effectively never used, so the car attacked the ramp without enough speed. Its fix, applied in a single attempt, changed 3.5 percent of the weights, and the car, now backing up first to build momentum, succeeded in all 1,024 test episodes.

The fifth experiment asks whether a frontier model can write weights from nothing. Given the rules and source code for three Atari games, Breakout, Seaquest, and Asteroids, and no training data, gradients, or tools, Astra wrote networks directly as text. Its networks beat conventionally trained models that had trained for 100 million steps on all three games. Against models trained for 500 million steps, it scored twenty times as much in Breakout, came close in Asteroids, and reached 60 percent of the trained score in Seaquest.

The sixth experiment tackles scale. At 1.5 million parameters, a network no longer fits in a context window, so Astra was given a sandbox with standard math libraries and a budget of test runs, called forward passes, from zero up to fifty. With no test runs at all, its written network won more than half its games against two smaller conventionally trained networks. With fifty test runs, it beat an equal-sized network trained on 20 million games. Berman dwells on the method: Astra writes the network as a small program, layer by layer, much as it would write ordinary code.

The seventh experiment shows a current limit. Asked to judge, from weights alone, which of two models was the more sycophantic, Astra failed in all four attempts. It found many differences between the two models' weights but could not connect them to the behavioral difference. When the researchers loosened the test and let it inspect attention patterns instead of raw weights, it picked out the more sycophantic model in all three pairs.

The eighth experiment applies editing to a small language model, the 0.8 billion parameter Qwen3, to improve its math. Astra spotted a familiar failure of small reasoning models: they fall into repetition loops, burn their whole output budget, and never produce a final answer. Editing a tiny fraction of the weights fixed the looping, and with a budget of fifty test runs the model's score on the MATH-500 benchmark rose from just under 8 percent to just over 48 percent, while its GSM8K score went from 13.8 percent to 67.1 percent. The researchers are careful about what this shows: fixing the loop added no new math ability, it let the model finish answers it could already work out. As a check, Astra could not improve Gemma 3, a model that rarely loops.

Berman closes on the implications. Editing weights directly is far faster than retraining, so a strong model could in principle repair or improve smaller models on demand, even on a phone, with no retraining pipeline at all. A model that can read weights this well is also a tool for interpreting the black box, down to individual weights, which could feed into alignment work. And the step he keeps returning to is the recursive one: a model that can understand and surgically modify another model's weights raises the question of when it does the same to its own.

Open questions

  1. The reading results are strongest on small, purpose-built networks. Does this ability survive at frontier scale, where the weights cannot fit in a context window and tools have to mediate every look?
  2. The clearest editing win so far is repair: removing a failure mode so existing ability shows through. Where is the line between repairing a model and genuinely improving one, and how would anyone verify that a weight edit did only what was intended?
  3. The sycophancy experiment found a behavior the weight reading could not detect. If frontier models start rewriting smaller models' weights directly, what catches a trait, intended or accidental, that the same reading methods cannot see?

AI News & Strategy Daily | Nate B Jones · Elon's AI agent has a massive cost problem

Elon's AI Agent Has a Massive Cost Problem

On AI News & Strategy Daily, Nate B. Jones's episode "Elon's AI Agent Has a Massive Cost Problem" begins with a public complaint. A customer had burned through their Grokbot allowance with four days left before the reset, and Elon Musk replied directly: the cost of serving Grok was unsustainably high relative to revenue. Jones argues this is not really a story about one product being in trouble. It is a story about how anyone paying for agents decides what the agent's work is actually worth.

The puzzle is that the same agent does clearly valuable work inside Musk's own company. Jones points to xAI's published write-ups of its internal use: Grokbot found 43 paid software seats that nobody had used in 90 days, and downgrading them saved over $14,000. So an agent can be worth paying for and too expensive to serve at the same time, because the value depends on the work it is handed. An agent that spends three hours rearranging a document nobody needed is a cost problem. An agent that finds tens of thousands of dollars in savings is one you would pay almost anything to keep.

His first hunting ground for valuable work is the recurring decision meeting that resolves nothing. Eight people gather to decide whether a project is ready; the plan sits in a document somewhere, the dates keep changing, an issue raised last week may or may not be resolved, and two spreadsheets disagree. Forty-five minutes later the group has agreed only that someone, a ninth person who is not in the room, should find out what is happening. Asking an agent to summarize the documents is, in his telling, the weak 2025 response: it produces one more document. The valuable version gives the agent enough context to show what is ready, what is not, where the dates conflict, and which unresolved item would actually change the finish date.

Underneath that example is his central distinction. People who get disproportionate value use the agent as a clarifier, not a complexifier. Hiring an agent because it reads faster often just amplifies the volume of material nobody can act on. His tests for whether an agent earned its cost are tangible: did it find a real problem you would not have noticed otherwise, did it resolve something much faster than it would otherwise have been resolved, and how much of your own time went into checking and fixing its answer? He pairs that with a plea for honesty about credit: do not claim the agent did the whole job when a human colleague did most of the work and finalized the budget.

The second hunting ground is the inherited weekly report, the file somebody else built that is now yours forever: downloads, copied numbers, formatting fixes, totals that do not match. Automating the painful parts is a reasonable start, but Jones pushes further. Give the agent the previous input files and the business rules you have been carrying in your head, then test it on an old week where you already know the answer, and find out whether it can take the job all the way to done. Value is not a twenty-minute task shrinking to two minutes when nobody cared about the task. It is distributed pain across a team, the same grind every Friday, disappearing so people can do their actual jobs. The same audit logic produced the seat savings: he says any company with over 100 people can have an agent look for software seats unused in 30 days and will find some.

The third hunting ground is the internal idea, the problem everyone has opinions about and nobody has built a solution for. His advice is to act like a tiny internal startup: use the agent to build a rough version quickly, put it in front of a few colleagues, and cycle on whether they can actually use it. Decide in advance what you are trying to learn and how much time and money that question deserves, and the experiment will show quickly whether the idea has legs, before a bigger commitment is made.

Then comes the accounting. Jones wants the fully loaded cost: the AI usage, the extra tools, and your own checking and fixing time, all counted. Against that, each person needs a working value for their own time, a rule of thumb such as whether saving two hours is worth ten dollars or saving three hours is worth fifty, plus a settled way of counting agent costs, whether as a subscription already paid, as metered API usage, or as the price of a cheaper model that could be traded down to. Know your break-even, he argues, and be able to say why a specific model doing a specific job is worth it, in numbers. This used to be work for companies and CFOs; now it is everybody's job, and he suggests having an AI interview you to build the model of your time value if you get stuck.

He closes with where this leads. The same agent can grind through a hard, open-ended assignment whose duration nobody can predict and can also produce a useless rewrite of an email, and buyers will price those two jobs very differently. He expects far more precise agent tiering: people refusing to pay for work that is not worth an agent doing, and paying extra where the value is extraordinary. The next time a provider trims usage because serving costs too much, users who know their budget and their break-even can simply move the work to another agent and solve the problem from there.

Open questions

  1. Jones's tests for value are deliberately tangible, but his strongest example, untangling a misaligned project before a decision meeting, produces clarity rather than a countable saving. What evidence should count when the value is real and everyone involved can point to it, yet nobody can put a number on it?
  2. The xAI seat savings were possible because the agent could see usage across the whole company. Inside a normal company, who gets to give an agent that visibility, and what keeps the audit itself from becoming another line item whose value nobody can prove?
  3. If agent pricing tiers precisely around the value of the work, providers will have to judge that value too, and their judgment may not match the customer's own break-even math. Who wins that disagreement, and what does the pricing look like while both sides are still guessing?

Invest Like The Best · Martin Casado on what happens when the AI boom slows down

What Happens When the AI Boom Slows Down

On Invest Like the Best, host Patrick O'Shaughnessy sits down with Martin Casado, a general partner at Andreessen Horowitz who has spent three decades building and investing in technology infrastructure. Casado founded Nicira, sold it to VMware, and now sits close to some of the largest strategic deals in AI, including the Cursor acquisition. The conversation ranges from how investors should think about disruptive technology to the question in the episode's title: what happens to the frontier labs, and to everyone funding them, if the AI market stops growing so fast.

Casado starts with a taxonomy he recently wrote up, because he thinks investors constantly talk past each other. He sees four mindsets. Traditional finance investors judge a business by its market and its financial metrics. Productive investors believe the asset itself creates value, and that markets and business models follow. Frontier investors, the romantic venture style of the 1960s and 1970s, back exceptional founders and largely refuse to second guess them. Speculative investors do not care about the asset at all and only try to time markets. Every mindset is legitimate on its own, he argues. The incoherence comes from mixing them, for example calling yourself a disruptive technology investor while screening for net revenue retention.

His own method is the productive one, applied only to infrastructure, the one world he claims to understand. His team studies what leading companies build internally, whether a team has done the same thing in another context, whether adjacent budgets could fragment into a new market, and whether customers show early willingness to pay even before a business model exists. Every technology wave, he notes, brings hand wringing about margins, from the internet to cloud to mobile to AI. Margins and monetization always lag the technology, so judging a disruptive asset by the quality of its revenue simply steers you toward non-disruptive companies.

That leads to his pet peeve: network effects. Casado argues the industry conflates the mechanism that creates a moat with the shape of the moat. What matters for defensibility is whether an advantage grows superlinearly with scale, like the classic telephone network, where each new user adds connections to everyone already there, or only linearly, or sublinearly, in which case a challenger spending the same money grows faster than the leader and always catches up. True superlinear network effects are rare: the old telephone monopolies, LinkedIn, GitHub, social networks. Uber, in his telling, is closer to a two sided marketplace plus a funding scale effect. His broader point is that defensibility is mostly a distraction anyway. In a growing market, companies expand into white space instead of fighting, and in a shrinking market, consolidation happens regardless of moats.

On the state of infrastructure, Casado describes a stack being rebuilt all at once: compute, networking, storage, and databases, with models sitting alongside them as something closer to ASICs than to software. Because agents rather than humans now write and run much of the code, design decisions once made for human ergonomics can be revisited. Databases are his example: new entrants can rethink the query language entirely, because no generation of programmers needs retraining when the agent already knows the new interface. Networking, sleepy for fifteen years since Arista, is waking up because AI clusters have rigid performance demands and are shifting toward optical interconnects. He splits the change in two: far more software being produced that still needs somewhere to run, and new systems built with agents at their center.

The heart of the episode is his answer to the title question. For three years, he says, the frontier model has taken more than eighty percent of the market, dollar weighted, because demand is effectively unlimited and buyers always want the smartest model available. Penetration is still tiny, perhaps three percent of the enterprise and one percent or less of budgets, so the growth regime could run for a long time. But a model's useful life is only three to six months, which means a lab that slips a single release cycle can lose the market entirely. If the market saturates in pockets, he expects pricing power to collapse and value to shift toward open source, a shakeout unlike anything software has seen, because software used to enjoy a long tail of retention.

Coding, the biggest market so far, shows the tension. Many tasks are close to saturated, in the sense that teams have automated nearly everything they can, yet spending on frontier tokens keeps rising. Casado says the two facts do not contradict each other. In a competitive equilibrium, no company chooses a dumber model than its rivals, so buyers keep paying for the smartest model even as their total token growth slows. The result is broadening usage into general knowledge work rather than a collapse in demand.

Underneath everything sits what he calls the deepest change of the era: capital can now be converted directly into technological progress. Engineering used to be the bottleneck that money could not fix, the world of The Mythical Man-Month, where adding people to a late project made it later. With AI, twenty people can productively absorb a billion dollars, because anything digital can be simulated, turned into a training environment, and improved with reinforcement learning. Anthropic and OpenAI, he says, are machines for turning money into growth, and they have raised more in aggregate than the entire downstream ecosystem. The open question is whether the physical world, robotics, science, and medicine, follows the same laws. He is confident about the digital side and agnostic about the rest.

The capital has limits, though, and they are increasingly physical and financial. The real constraint on the buildout is power, chips, and materials, not ideas. He expects about eighty percent of the neo cloud market to go to the largest scale providers, with perhaps twenty percent left in fragmented niches, down to GPU marketplaces running hardware in converted chicken sheds. Debt is the other squeeze: there is not enough of it to go around, so investment grade borrowers win and smaller players will struggle, a dynamic he expects to push prepayment demands down the chain onto customers. Data center politics point the same direction: with some five hundred proposed laws in the United States aimed at stopping data center construction, he recounts Larry Page's view that compute eventually goes orbital, five nines of it, because space skirts the regulatory problem.

Casado also breaks with venture orthodoxy on prices. He believes private markets are efficient, pays the market price without trying to outsmart it, and says he would happily index the whole private market if he could, taking five percent of every competitive deal. His firm's approach is the next best thing: for each legitimate sector, pick the single best company, then trust the market's price. He calls non-consensus investing dangerous, mostly because follow on capital follows consensus, and he rejects the zero sum claim that venture is not an asset class and only twenty startups matter. More money in private markets creates more of the market itself, he argues, which is why companies stay private so long.

The final stretch draws lessons from the recent wave of strategic acquisitions, from his own sale of Nicira to VMware for 1.26 billion dollars, then a record revenue multiple for enterprise software, to the Tabular sale to Databricks, the Cursor deal, OpenRouter, and Fei-Fei Li's World Labs. Strategic deals, he says, run on trust and deep personal alignment between the people involved. A buyer is purchasing an option on a large market, so the seller's job is to supply the base case and the inductive step: here is the business today, and here is how it attaches to your platform and scales. Leaks, auctions, and poker tactics backfire against the most sophisticated counterparties on earth. World Labs never leaked, he notes, because almost nobody knew. OpenRouter leaked constantly, and it drove him nuts.

He closes with the investor's changing job. The largest private companies now rival the public giants of twenty years ago, so late stage finance instincts keep leaking into early stage rooms where they do not belong. His own practice has shifted toward listening: a founder will tell you what they believe, and the craft is asking only the few questions that actually inform the decision. His excitement is undimmed. Turning problem solving into a capital allocation problem is, to him, an industrial revolution scale shift, from a zero sum world to one where new value is simply created.

Open questions

  1. Casado's slowdown scenario has frontier pricing power collapsing into open source once pockets of the market saturate, yet his coding discussion argues competitive pressure keeps buyers on the smartest model indefinitely. Which force wins in the first category that genuinely saturates, and what would the early evidence look like?
  2. If capital now converts directly into progress for anything digital, but the binding constraints are power, chips, and debt, does the advantage compound for the largest players until the fragmented twenty percent of the market is squeezed out as well?
  3. His index-like venture strategy assumes private markets price risk well. If the AI boom does slow down sharply, how much of that apparent efficiency was the rising market itself, and would his stratified approach still beat the consensus it trusts?

Peter H. Diamandis · Moonshots on Big Tech's $3T AI bet and Claude writing Anthropic's code

Big Tech’s $3T AI Bet, Claude Writes 90% of Anthropic’s Code, AIs Editing AI Brains | EP #307

On Moonshots with Peter Diamandis, the episode "Big Tech’s $3T AI Bet, Claude Writes 90% of Anthropic’s Code, AIs Editing AI Brains | EP #307" is a panel conversation between Diamandis and his three regular co-hosts: Dave Blundin, Salim Ismail, and Dr. Alexander Wissner-Gross. Recorded and released on election day in the United States, it moves from AI agents as voting proxies, through the three trillion dollars now committed to the AI build-out, to two very different benchmarks, Anthropic's IPO filing, a paper on AI systems editing the weights of other AI models, a supermarket freezer hack in Europe, an antimatter record, and NASA's return to the Moon.

The opening exchange is about voting. Diamandis jokes about taking his AI assistant into the booth, and Wissner-Gross argues that AI systems already effectively vote, because voters increasingly ask their agents what to think and then act on the answer. The panel expects that by the 2028 election almost nobody will make a ballot decision without consulting an agent first, which makes the absence of any regulatory movement on the question, in their view, the real story. Diamandis then turns to Apple's first earnings report under its new chief executive, John Ternus: revenue beat estimates at 114.5 billion dollars, the stock still fell, and the pressure point Ternus named was memory pricing, which is rising because data centers are absorbing the world's RAM. That sparks a longer argument, picked up from Sam Altman's recent remarks, about whether memory belongs in consumer devices at all, or whether phones and laptops eventually become thin clients once connectivity is good enough, with Starlink as the possible straw.

The first main story is the money. Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, and Oracle have now disclosed more than three trillion dollars in combined commitments to leases, buildings, power, and equipment for AI, with Alphabet alone past one trillion and roughly a trillion of the total added in just the last three months. Diamandis puts that at about ten Apollo programs. The panel's question is how long the financing party lasts. Lenders who once funded build-outs with few questions are demanding tougher terms, HSBC is talking to insurers about offloading risk on its data center loans, put options betting against CoreWeave hit a record high in October, and, per the Financial Times, OpenAI cut its new funding round to about 20 billion dollars at a lower valuation. Blundin argues the builders will not slow down, because they believe the payoff is permanent dominance, and that Elon Musk is now competing for the same lenders by offering himself as the safer credit. Wissner-Gross thinks the next pool of capital is insurance, pointing to roughly six trillion dollars of life insurance capital and three trillion of property and casualty capital in the United States, and notes that three-year-old H100 chips are rising in price while top-end Nvidia systems remain nearly impossible to obtain at any price. Ismail's caution is the historical one: every infrastructure build-out of this scale has come with a bubble, even when the direction was right.

The second story is measurement. SemiAnalysis's GDPx benchmark drops AI agents into realistic expert work for up to eight hours across 206 tasks in 11 fields, from chip design and quant trading to biology and alignment, and even the best model scores only about 12 percent, with finance and legal tasks near zero for almost every model. Wissner-Gross likes the benchmark precisely because the best score sits in the 10 to 20 percent range, high enough to show the tasks are solvable and low enough to be unsaturated, and he expects it to be largely solved within about a year. Blundin guesses the midpoint of 50 percent arrives in roughly a year and that nearly every dimension saturates within two. Both stress that GDPx measures frontier work rather than today's job distribution, and the panel broadens the point: in an era of superintelligence, creating a benchmark for something you care about is how you steer the systems toward it. Diamandis adds that he is preparing an abundance and singularity index of his own.

OpenAI's new benchmark of everyday tasks makes the contrast. Built from a survey of nearly four thousand Americans, it asks models to do the errands of ordinary life through a browser: finding childcare, enrolling a child in kindergarten, building a household budget, dealing with government agencies. Scores jumped from about half of tasks completed in December 2025 to just over 80 percent in September 2026. Ismail calls this the more important number, because a competent personal assistant reaches everyone, including people who will never need superhuman mathematics. Blundin counters that capability is running ahead of connection: the models can now do the work, but insurers, agencies, and customer service systems are not plugged into them, so people still lose hours on the phone. Wissner-Gross reads the chart's tight clustering as a hint that OpenAI may be putting less effort into consumer everyday tasks than into enterprise ones.

The third story is the one that gives the episode its title. Anthropic's IPO filing shows that since April, the value of the company's internal Claude usage, priced at the rates charged to customers, has exceeded its monthly cash payroll. Internal usage grew more than 250-fold between January and September, Claude now writes more than 90 percent of the company's code, and daily code contributions per engineer run about 45 times higher than two years ago. The panel treats this as a threshold crossed: a frontier lab now spends more on its AI researchers than on its human ones. Blundin offers a ground-level version, an engineer near his office who burned half a billion tokens in a single day, roughly five thousand dollars, and Ismail describes coaching companies to build an intelligence stack alongside the existing business and move workflows onto it one by one, with one company compressing a 22-step process into four. Wissner-Gross's reservation is about depth: today's recursion is software eating software, and the loop will look shallow once robots and nanobots are physically building the data centers and each other.

The companion research story is the MIT CSAIL paper "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming," from Omar Khattab's group, which this channel's audience may know from earlier coverage. Frontier models, the researchers found, are surprisingly good at reading and writing the weights of a neural network directly. In their example, a frontier model diagnosed why a small open model, Qwen 3.5, was getting stuck in endless thinking loops, edited just 0.016 percent of its weights, and its math score rose from 7.9 percent to 48.1 percent, with no retraining and no backpropagation. Wissner-Gross calls it a plausible new inner loop for recursive self-improvement and the likely future of mechanistic interpretability, since the machines plainly can learn to understand machines. The panel also names the darker edge: the same surgery can strip safety guardrails from open models, or quietly add capabilities and behaviors, though they argue the models' ability to inspect each other cuts both ways. Blundin imagines a market in finished neural modules, where a specialist capability built by, say, a biotech team could be stitched into a general model instead of retrained from scratch, and Wissner-Gross says he is more than 90 percent confident frontier labs already have models editing their own weights internally.

A shorter segment turns to cybersecurity. Hackers remotely seized the refrigeration systems of supermarkets across eight European countries, switched them to defrost mode, and spoiled food on a large scale, the second such wave after more than 500 French stores were hit weeks earlier. The panel's reading is that legacy industrial control systems carry 1990s security into 2026 connectivity, that AI offense is now automated while defense still has humans in the loop, and that the gap will produce a difficult transition period, with banks, power, and the grid as the obvious next targets. One week the entry point is an engineer's laptop at a crypto exchange, the next it is a freezer.

The science and space stories close the main program. At Northwestern, Gerald Gabrielse's group held a single positron, an antimatter particle, for a month in a container with no walls, using magnetic and electric fields at a fraction of a degree above absolute zero, work aimed at testing whether matter and antimatter behave identically, and a step toward storing antimatter at all, which any future antimatter propulsion would require. Then Diamandis lays out NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman's plan: Artemis III in the summer of 2027 will rehearse docking with both commercial lunar landers in Earth orbit, Artemis IV in 2028 will put humans back on the Moon for the first time since 1972, and a base near the lunar South Pole follows. The panel frames it as a new space race with China, and as a test of whether the commercial ecosystem can keep the government program honest this time, unlike the fifty-year gap after Apollo.

The closing question-and-answer section ranges widely. On longevity, Diamandis argues expensive gene therapies will follow the usual technology curve downward, citing AI-discovered molecules his guest researchers estimate could cost about two hundred dollars a month, against half a million to two million for today's gene therapy treatments. Ismail argues the purpose question rests on a mistake, the industrial-era conflation of employment with meaning. Wissner-Gross describes the present moment as a second renaissance, a rediscovery after fifty years of stagnation of the postwar willingness to build in the physical world. Blundin answers a question on robots by way of Starship: enormous new supply, whether of launch capacity or humanoid robots, needs new use cases to absorb it, and robots building and maintaining data centers look like the first big one.

Open questions

  1. If the five builders have already committed three trillion dollars and the easy capital pools are being exhausted, which pool actually funds the next three trillion, and on what terms, before any slowdown in AI profits shows up to justify it?
  2. Anthropic's filing says Claude already writes more than 90 percent of its code and its internal AI spend exceeds payroll. If models also begin editing their own weights, as the MIT result suggests they can, what is left that humans inside a lab can still verify?
  3. GDPx says the best models manage about 12 percent of frontier expert work while everyday-task scores pass 80 percent. Which of those two numbers better predicts what the economy feels like in two years, and who closes the gap between capability and connection to real systems?

MTS · Charlie O'Neill on why AI research will not hit fast takeoff

Why AI Research Won't Hit Fast Takeoff | Charlie O'Neill

On MTS, the episode "Why AI Research Won't Hit Fast Takeoff | Charlie O'Neill" is a conversation with Charlie O'Neill, co-head of model training at Baseten, who also leads Baseten Labs, the company's open source AI research lab, where his work covers continual learning and open reinforcement learning environments. His headline claim gives the episode its title. Recursive self-improvement, he argues, will exist, but only in a much weaker form than fast takeoff theories predict, because the current way of building models keeps running into the physical world.

The interview first touches on Baseten's launch day. The company has put three products under one inference platform: Grid, which pulls a company's model usage and spending into one place and routes each request to a model chosen on quality and price; Loops, which offers serverless fine-tuning and reinforcement learning on frontier open models, so training can be launched from a laptop and deployed straight to dedicated inference; and Sandboxes, isolated machines built for agents, meant to support long running agentic tasks. O'Neill frames the three as a sign that AI is becoming an ecosystem of connected products rather than a single stream of generated tokens.

His central argument starts from the current paradigm: a transformer, pre-trained and then improved with large amounts of reinforcement learning and post-training. Stay inside that paradigm, he says, and each next jump in capability demands far more input from the real world than the last. Reinforcement learning environments, the simulated tasks models learn from, take ever longer and cost ever more to build, because the easy ones have been saturated. A single rollout can take anywhere from a day to a week of real time. Labs are already spending exponentially more money on reinforcement learning data, and humans working alongside models are hitting the same wall he expects automated researchers to hit.

That, he argues, is what the short timelines miss. The case for recursive self-improvement by 2027, and genuine superintelligence soon after, assumes an AI researcher only slightly better than the best human would snowball: improving itself, then improving itself faster, and so on. But that picture quietly assumes away the bottlenecks, the total compute available for experiments, the problem of running those experiments in parallel, the need for agents to share and remember findings, and the time it takes simply to generate output. In robotics and in biological research, he notes, everyone accepts that real world experiments set the pace, because a wet lab experiment cannot be shortcut. People discount the same limits in AI research only because it is easy to imagine computers so fast that none of this binds. We are, he says, quite far from that world.

Within the current paradigm, then, he expects strong diminishing returns, but very uneven ones. Pure mathematics, theoretical physics, and computer science should keep producing moments that look like superintelligence; he points to the recent Navier-Stokes result as one such moment. Quantitative trading is another verifiable domain where, he predicts, model intelligence becomes a basic necessity of doing business at all. In less verifiable fields, what looks like superintelligence will more likely be a system than a model: a well built harness running today's level of intelligence across something like the legal system at scale, clearing backlogs and multiplying human decisions, rather than one model transforming legal thought itself. Underneath it all, he sees a fundamental limit to what a transformer trained on an internet's worth of data, plus a great deal of reinforcement learning, can reach on a finite compute budget. Others, such as Rich Sutton and David Silver, are betting on a different kind of learner entirely. His own expectation is tools of enormous economic value, punctuated by striking moments, but nothing that runs away into an entity humans fundamentally cannot understand, at least not from this paradigm.

The host presses the same point from the evidence on automating machine learning research itself. The time horizon of tasks models can handle in ML research is far shorter than in general software engineering, roughly fifteen minutes against roughly ten hours in the measurements he cites. Anthropic's own account of automating its research found no research tasks that AI could fully take over, and credited AI with leading about a quarter of model research and development by August. And at OpenAI, researchers used vastly more tokens between December 2025 and August 2026 to run somewhat more experiments, without pushing model capability above its previous trend. O'Neill largely agrees, and adds that research is a stranger domain than it looks: results like a falling loss are verifiable, but the contributions that matter most come from people who simply run the most experiments, as Noam Shazeer did at Google, and much of the craft is taste that resists checking.

Models do raise research productivity, he says, by babysitting overnight jobs and restarting failed runs with the right fixes, but there is a deeper obstacle to models doing the research themselves. Anything genuinely new, worked out in the last few months, is barely present in pre-training data, and pre-training is still the foundation of capability. Mechanistic interpretability is his example: a year or so ago models were hopeless at it, and now that papers have accumulated, they show real control and research taste with the same techniques. On timelines he offers arithmetic rather than a date. Perhaps fifteen to twenty more small drops in pre-training loss remain in the current architecture, he estimates, and a genuinely different paradigm would require Einstein level intuition and creativity that models have not yet shown without very smart humans beside them. Either path stretches the calendar. His parting suggestion for forecasters is to plot the record of predictions about when AI research would be automated, much as people did with self-driving car forecasts, and fit a curve to how those dates have slipped.

Open questions

  1. If each new capability frontier needs exponentially more real world input, is there any measurement the labs could publish that would settle whether the bottleneck is binding now, or still years away?
  2. O'Neill expects superintelligence-like results to arrive as systems and harnesses in non-verifiable fields like law, rather than as a single model. If he is right, does the safety and governance debate, built around the idea of one very powerful model, need to be reframed around organisations of models?
  3. His mechanistic interpretability example suggests model research skill follows the accumulation of published papers with a lag. If labs keep more of their research private, does automating research get slower even as the models get better?

SemiAnalysis · The midterms and the 2028 datacenter buildout

Ep. 043 - The Midterms and the 2028 Datacenter Buildout (Datacenters)

On SemiAnalysis, the episode "Ep. 043 - The Midterms and the 2028 Datacenter Buildout (Datacenters)" is a SemiAnalysis Weekly conversation hosted by Jordan Nanos with Maya Barkin, Wayne Ma, and Jeremie Eliahou Ontiveros. It was recorded on the morning of election day, before any midterm results were in, and it updates the team's September analysis of datacenter moratoriums: what the elections can and cannot change about the American buildout, and why the fight has gone bipartisan.

The core of the argument is a split between two years. The 2027 pipeline is largely locked in. Much of the capacity due to come online that year is already under construction with its permits in hand, so the team stays comfortable with the forecast of a fifty-plus gigawatt global year it laid out a few weeks earlier. The September finding has also held: roughly twenty gigawatts of capacity sat inside local restrictions, and only about one and a half gigawatts was actually delayed. Moratoriums, so far, have not killed buildouts.

The exposed year is 2028. SemiAnalysis models about sixty-two gigawatts of US additions that year, and most of it is not yet under construction. It sits in states that were electing a governor in the midterms, waiting on permits, power, and construction schedules while policies are negotiated and potentially rewritten. Across the twenty nominees in ten governors' races in the top datacenter states, nine want a pause on new construction and eleven want conditions, and the split is about even by party. The single biggest risk, the team argues, is not any one candidate: in many states, both parties want developers to accept stricter terms.

Much of the episode turns on the difference between a pause and conditions. A pause, or moratorium, halts the processing and issuing of permits, and a project that does not already hold every permit simply gets stuck. Conditions make a project more expensive but keep it buildable: the developer can fund the grid upgrade, redesign the cooling, sign the community agreement, and keep going. A drift toward conditions was visible before the election, in water use studies, in household bill credits offered to cover added infrastructure, and in AWS and Google dropping their NDAs and becoming more transparent about their plans.

The scenario maps quantify the stakes. If pro-moratorium candidates swept, some 30.3 gigawatts of planned 2028 capacity would sit in states led by pause supporters. If only conditions candidates won, the exposed figure falls to 5.7 gigawatts, and the map drawn from the polls looks much like the milder case: about 5.7 gigawatts most likely at risk. The team is careful that exposed is not the same as delayed: this is capacity planned in those states, not a forecast of what stops.

Texas carries the largest single share, close to thirty percent of US additions in 2028, and it is where policy has already moved. At the end of September, Governor Greg Abbott froze TCEQ air permits for datacenter projects, on top of an ERCOT interconnection queue audit that began in August. The audit also pulls on-site power into the hold, which closes what September's analysis had treated as the escape route: bring your own power no longer routes around the state. Texas is also the one real downside risk to 2027. Some projects whose shells are already built and funded still lack their final air pollution permit, and without that permit the power plant cannot be built, so a long enough pause could leave finished campuses with no electricity to run on.

The race itself cuts both ways. Abbott is favored to win a fourth term, and his directives expire on their own, so the likely path is an easing after the election: the ERCOT audit report is targeted for early December, after which permitting could reopen under stricter requirements, an interruption most projects can absorb. His Democratic opponent, Gina Hinojosa, would keep a moratorium in place until the legislature passes protections covering household electricity and water bills, an end to subsidies, a ban on NDAs, and union labor. With the legislative session running from January to May 2027, that pause could run about eight months, close to a year if new rules take effect in late summer: five to nine months longer than a December restart, which would leave campuses aiming at 2027 delivery with little time to spare.

The panel spends real time on why the backlash crosses party lines. It is local before it is ideological: residents near a project face the noise, the water questions, and the fear of higher power bills whichever party they vote for, layered over wider anxiety about AI and jobs and a stream of misinformation about how much water and electricity datacenters actually use. Jeremie adds the scale argument. The annual US buildout is heading toward roughly forty gigawatts in 2027, ten to twenty times the pace of five years ago, and at that scale everything changes: counties that once competed to offer tax breaks now hold the stronger hand. There are also legitimate engineering concerns. Datacenters have become a systemic grid risk on the load side: in Virginia, a voltage dip tripped the backup systems of many datacenters at once and about one and a half gigawatts of load vanished almost instantly, the kind of sudden swing that can cascade toward a blackout. His expectation is that the pressure produces better neighbors: more water-efficient designs, equipment that behaves well toward the grid, and an end to designing for a smaller era.

Tax incentives are where the changed balance of power shows up in budgets. Ohio designed its incentives expecting them to cost about 136 million dollars a year; they cost 555 million two years ago and 1.6 billion dollars last year, and candidates of both parties are running on rolling them back. Jeremie's caveat is that tax breaks are the normal treatment for industrial projects at this scale, since a single one-gigawatt campus is roughly a fifty billion dollar project, and the money was not simply wasted. But with demand this strong, states no longer need to bid against each other, developers can be asked to contribute materially more to the communities hosting them, and some are already declining breaks or paying more locally on their own initiative.

How policy actually lands depends on the level of government. Governors can move fastest, directing agencies to stop issuing permits, as in the Texas freeze and an environmental permit hold in Oregon. Legislatures control tax breaks and can pass laws, but they move slowly, and no moratorium has yet been adopted at the legislative level. Local councils and boards still decide project by project, responsive to their own residents. The parties differ at the margins: Democrats lean more toward temporary pauses, union labor requirements, and NDA bans, while Republicans lean toward leaving control with local communities, but pay-your-own-grid-costs, ending tax incentives, and water rules draw support across the spectrum. Georgia is the genuine toss-up of the cycle: Keisha Lance Bottoms wants a temporary pause, Rick Jackson favors conditions, and strikingly, neither has a stated position on ending tax incentives, which for any given project may be the single largest financial question of all.

At the federal level, the panel expects little to change even if Democrats take the House and the Senate, since the presidential veto runs to the end of the term and a narrow Senate majority would still need Republican votes for stricter law. The one true consensus, shared by the Trump administration, is that large electricity users should pay their own grid costs. The likely outcome is developers posting more money upfront or as collateral, which delays some projects without prohibiting them, and permitting reform could even speed up the infrastructure the new demand requires.

The closing question is whether datacenters can win over their neighbors. The backlash is, the team notes, a distinctly 2026 phenomenon, and people consistently rate AI itself more favorably than the buildings that house it. The suggested remedies are transparency instead of NDAs, education against water and power misinformation, and visible community contributions: fire stations, playgrounds, schools, roads. Direct cash is not the answer; people the team spoke with on the ground say a ten thousand dollar payment would not buy acceptance, and some residents simply do not want to be paid off. The jobs argument is also weaker than it first looks: construction work at these sites is real and well paid, but much of it goes to specialists brought in from other states or countries. The story the panel keeps returning to is Meta's Louisiana site, where datacenter taxes fund a yearly bonus for local teachers, a benefit neighbors can actually see. As for election night itself, the races they were watching were Texas first and Georgia second. The episode's published description, updated after recording, reports that the AP called Texas for Hinojosa and Georgia for Bottoms, and that Democrats took a three-to-two majority on Georgia's Public Service Commission, the body that sets power rates there.

Open questions

  1. The polls-based map puts only about 5.7 gigawatts of 2028 capacity in genuinely risky states, but governors can freeze permits by directive and local boards still decide project by project. How much of the remaining fifty-odd gigawatts is actually safe, and how would anyone know before the permits stall?
  2. Texas now runs the bear case in real life: a governor elected on pausing permits until protections pass. Does the hold really stretch five to nine months past a December restart, and do any 2027 campuses end up built but unable to power up?
  3. Can transparency, community benefits, and paying full grid costs reverse a backlash the panel dates to 2026, or does opposition harden mechanically as the buildout runs at ten to twenty times its former scale and reaches more backyards every year?

WSJ Events · Greg Brockman at WSJ Tech Live

Watch Live: OpenAI Co-Founder Greg Brockman at WSJ Tech | WSJ

On WSJ Events, the session "Watch Live: OpenAI Co-Founder Greg Brockman at WSJ Tech | WSJ" is a stage conversation from WSJ Tech Live between the Journal's Keach Hagey and Greg Brockman, the co-founder and president of OpenAI. Recorded on Wednesday, the morning after the midterm elections, it ranges from the data center backlash and a new era of AI driven cybersecurity to the revenue race with Anthropic, an unannounced bank partnership, the path to going public, and what AI looks like five years out.

The conversation opens on the election. Many of the Democrats who won the night before support moratoriums on data centers, and Hagey asks what that means for a company that needs enormous amounts of compute. Brockman says the industry has failed to explain itself to the communities hosting the buildout. He points to OpenAI sponsoring free lunches for students in Georgia, and to his own childhood outside Grand Forks, North Dakota, where a sugar beet factory smelled terrible but anchored the town's jobs and identity. He concedes the backlash caught everyone off guard, and that the company's posture has changed over the past year: you cannot just tweet that a data center will be good for a town, you have to be there, on the ground, and keep showing up.

Cybersecurity takes up the next stretch. OpenAI's tools recently helped uncover a vulnerability in PHP that underpins a vast amount of the internet, and Brockman describes giving governments around the world advance notice so they could prepare. His frame is a defender's window: the internet carries some forty years of security tech debt, AI is shining a light into the cracks, and the same technology can put defenders ahead for the first time, with formally verified, secure by construction code as the end state. Patching, he argues, has to move from a quarterly cadence to a month, a week, a day, ultimately minutes. He also floats a deliberately controversial idea: where owners leave vulnerable servers unpatched, the government might one day use the vulnerability itself to enter and apply the patch, an extension of the FBI's court ordered botnet cleanups. The next six to eighteen months, he warns, will be a different mode for everyone.

Asked what he found when his portfolio expanded, after his sabbatical, from compute to product to go to market, Brockman names one theme: co-design. Each year a new model obsoletes the previous year's work, software engineering has been transformed since this same conference last year, and no function can plan in isolation. His job, as he tells it, is connective tissue, making sure marketing hears what research sees coming, so the company moves in a single direction.

On the Anthropic race, he is candid. It became clear early this year that Anthropic would overtake OpenAI in revenue. The first two quarters were rough, and in coding OpenAI was barely in the conversation. The third quarter, he says, was entirely different: seventy percent quarterly growth in annualized revenue, with the enterprise side doubling, driven by the GPT-5.6 Sol model and the release of ChatGPT Work, the payoff of painful behind the scenes investment. Pressed on Anthropic's filing, which showed revenue per dollar of compute rising 5.2 times, he answers with OpenAI's efficiency record: price cuts of ten times, sometimes near a hundred times, year over year for a fixed level of intelligence, including an eighty percent cut on the Luna model this year that multiplied its volume more than tenfold. The dollars, he argues, flow to the frontier models, where buyers are nearly price insensitive because the smartest model produces outsized returns, while high volume models like Luna run at lower margins. Competition, in his telling, is making customers win.

He then describes a new kind of business: pointing serious compute at single hard problems. Fleets of agents, at roughly a million dollars of compute, solved a Navier-Stokes problem that had resisted some of the smartest minds in humanity. Announced today, the same approach found a thousand vulnerabilities in Linux and produced patches for them, against a normal rate of about twenty a year. Always on products make the same point at personal scale: his own assistant, Dot, flagged a well crafted phishing email in his inbox the day before, one he says he would never have caught himself. The ads business gets a brief mention too: it reached a billion dollars in annualized revenue around June or July, from zero at the start of the year.

On open weights models, Brockman resists the ban framing. He believes in democratizing the technology and avoiding a full concentration of power, while noting that a frontier lead lets new capabilities be tried first in more restricted forms, and that regulated industries will need deployments behind their own firewalls. The mechanisms, he says, will vary, and the real question is what responsible development and deployment mean across a diverse ecosystem.

Politics returns at the end. Brockman and his wife pledged fifty million dollars to Leading the Future, a pro AI super PAC, paid the first twenty five million, and then withheld the second tranche. His explanation is perceptual: the PAC came to be seen as standing against the link between safety and innovation that he considers inseparable, even though he credits it with positive impacts and more nuanced messaging in recent months. Asked about researchers who put the chance of AI killing everyone within a decade above ten percent, he declines to multiply probabilities into certainty. He joined OpenAI, he says, because the technology felt like the logical conclusion of computing, and he wanted to help it play out slightly more positively with him than without him.

He closes looking forward. Biology and the life sciences, which matter to animals as well as people, should see real strides in curing disease within twelve to eighteen months. Five years out, he expects AI to be as assumed as electricity, the question of whether to use it as odd as asking whether a business has power. People will type less and talk to each other more, and, as with today's software engineers, they will work harder than ever, not from obligation but because the opportunity is too large to walk away from.

  2. His answer to the data center backlash is presence: show up, sponsor the school lunches, stay. After an election in which moratorium supporters won, what would count as evidence that presence changes outcomes, rather than simply slowing projects down?

MTS · Rohan Anil on AI models learning at test time

AI Models Will Learn at Test Time by Next Year | Rohan Anil

On MTS, the episode "AI Models Will Learn at Test Time by Next Year | Rohan Anil" is a conversation with Rohan Anil, co-founder of Core Automation and previously a pre-training researcher at Anthropic. His headline claim matches the title: the piece missing from today's models is the ability to learn at test time, from their own experience as they work, and his company is designing the algorithms to do it. Asked for a date at the end of the conversation, he commits to next year.

The interview starts with his departure from Anthropic, against the running joke that nobody ever leaves. Anthropic is one of the best places to do research, he says, and he loved its pre-training team. His motivation was the next frontier. Scaling models up brought agents and the world we have now, yet people are working harder than ever with AI rather than less. What is missing, in his account, is models that keep learning while they are being used, instead of waiting for a lab to teach them.

Much of the conversation is about his recent essay, a hitchhiker's guide to training on compute islands. Frontier training has seemed to demand colocation: one site with enough power and enough top end chips in one place. But large numbers of high end GPUs sit scattered across many locations, in clusters too small to train a frontier model alone. Drawing on an older line of work in local SGD and federated learning, his team showed that such islands can train together. Their experiment mixed two different Blackwell systems with different interconnects, different parallelism, different numerics, and different batch sizes, and matched or slightly beat conventional training at a frontier pre-training scale.

That result, he argues, changes who gets to play. Training on ordinary home computers remains impractical at frontier scale, since memory and latency bind long before the algorithms do. But a newer lab that can gather scattered clusters quickly could reach the top tier of models, or power a vertical effort such as chip design. Core Automation itself is aimed at a different axis. Its bet is continual learning: changing the architecture and the training algorithm together, and removing bottlenecks he sees inside the transformer itself.

The technical core is an old idea called butterfly mixing, which predates neural networks. Standard training keeps every GPU in lockstep, summing all gradients across all machines at every step. Under butterfly mixing, machines work asynchronously on different fragments and exchange their state within a few steps, so no two machines ever hold quite the same picture. The model, in their test a thirty-two billion parameter mixture of experts model, proved robust to the noise this introduces, and reached the same quality as synchronous training, helped by second order methods and a few other tricks. Anil notes the wheel has turned before: he began his career training neural networks asynchronously on CPUs at Google, then watched synchronous training take over and scale through GPT-3, GPT-4, Gemini, and Anthropic's models.

He is similarly matter of fact about the exotic cases. Training a frontier model in space is an algorithms problem his work speaks to, and the early proofs exist: Google's Suncatcher project has run inference on TPUs in orbit, and Starcloud has trained a small model on a satellite. The hard part, he says, is the physics, not the math. Compute markets, meanwhile, look inefficient to him: short term spare capacity goes unsold, chips sit idle waiting for a buyer, and aggregated compute is worth more for a frontier run than the same chips scattered. With island training, he would happily take ten thousand GPUs spread across ten data centers. Without it, he would not. Labs designing their own chips, as OpenAI and Anthropic have begun to do, looks inevitable to him for the same reason, though the domain expertise involved keeps it genuinely hard.

His deepest point is about timelines. Power, grid equipment, land, and politics all constrain the buildout, but he sees an algorithmic bottleneck underneath them. Models are jagged, in the term he borrows from Andrej Karpathy: able to do something brilliant one day and something dumb the next, because nothing in the architecture or the optimization lets a model learn from its own mistakes quickly. Everyone waits for the labs to rerun the pre-training and reinforcement learning cycle every few months with new environments and new data. A model that learned as a company evolves would let companies operate far more effectively, he argues. Today the best substitute is memory bolted on in the form of compacted tokens, and that is not the same thing.

Continual learning also sharpens the safety questions, and he does not wave them away. A model that learns at test time raises questions about what it should learn, what it should be shown, and how much autonomy it should have. He points to the recent wave of software vulnerabilities, which he links in part to hastily generated code, as a sign of how quickly new capability creates new exposure. But the same technology could cut the other way: a model that learns on the fly could secure systems as it learns, and act as a defense rather than only a risk. The topic, he says, deserves much more study than one interview can give it.

Open questions

  1. Anil's next year claim rests on his own team's algorithms. What public result, a model, a benchmark, a demonstration, would count as evidence that test time learning has genuinely arrived, rather than another memory feature under a new name?
  2. If frontier training no longer needs colocation, does the strategic value of giant single site data centers fall, and who captures the value instead: the owners of scattered compute, or the owners of the training algorithms that make it usable?
  3. A model that learns continuously from what it is shown takes in whatever its environment feeds it. Who decides what such a model should learn from, and can that decision be audited afterwards?

WSJ Events · Vik Bajaj on the next tech frontier, physical AI

The Next Tech Frontier: Physical AI | WSJ

On WSJ Events, the session "The Next Tech Frontier: Physical AI | WSJ" is a stage conversation from WSJ Tech Live with Vik Bajaj, who co-founded the AI and manufacturing company Prometheus with Jeff Bezos. Prometheus has raised more than twelve billion dollars at a forty-one billion dollar valuation, and the moderator opens by noting that there is still real confusion about what the company actually wants to do.

Bajaj's answer starts with a distinction. The AI built so far works in the world of language, while Prometheus is building AI for the physical world: the energy, materials, buildings, bridges, ships, airplanes, and jet engines that he calls the fabric of civilization. His goal is to make the cycle of physical development, from dreaming something up to seeing it operate in reality, as fast in the limit as the cycle the software world now enjoys.

That ambition, he stresses, is not just an AI problem. Prometheus is partly an AI company, but it is also a manufacturing company. It has to build things, and even re-engineer the equipment that manufactures the world. The whole chain, from concept and design through building, evaluation, and maintenance, has to be treated as one end-to-end problem if the acceleration is going to happen.

The near term is more concrete than the vision. Some components of the US supply chain are simply missing, Bajaj says. They do not exist here, though they do exist in China, and supplying those missing components efficiently, in a way that serves every other industry, is core to restoring the American manufacturing base. He calls these steps the equivalent of Amazon selling books: consistent with the long-term vision, which eventually reaches complex systems like jet engines and airplanes, but achievable now.

Why physical AI, and why now? Language models, Bajaj says, are far more than chatbots, and the intelligence they represent will be phenomenally useful. But engineering the world is highly multimodal. You do not describe the design of an iPhone in words, and the physics underneath involves four-dimensional fields and forces moving through time, which requires a new AI layer. The stakes are the size of the prize: roughly sixty to seventy percent of world GDP is involved in physical things, and it is extraordinarily slow to develop them. An aerospace engineer may contribute a small component to an engine and wait an entire career to fly on an airplane it powers. If that experience could happen in one year instead of twenty, the compounding productivity would drive growth across the largest share of the global economy.

Asked whether physical AI has had its ChatGPT moment, Bajaj resists the framing. Physical work is too heterogeneous for a single interface. One device he gestures at contains manufacturing processes that date almost to the bronze age, like casting, alongside processes invented a decade ago that pattern materials a few atoms at a time. The moment, instead, is a set of cases arriving together: the whole run from design, to validation of that design, to manufacturing it, happening in simulation before it happens in the real world, with orders of magnitude acceleration of design cycles already visible.

On the valuation, he is deliberately humble. A figure like forty-one billion dollars cannot be justified at the inception of a project like this, he says. It represents the potential, and an awesome responsibility to the investors and the broader community that entrusted the company with capital. He declines to discuss fundraising plans for 2027. As for how much capital the whole vision takes, he says it is hard to define in the limit, since it means transforming a supply chain, but Prometheus is well capitalized for its near-term goals: starting to produce products, and missing supply chain components, that other American companies are currently limited without.

Working with Jeff Bezos is, in his telling, daunting at times and also pretty amazing. Bajaj has known Bezos a long time. They never worked closely together before, but Bezos invested in companies Bajaj founded, and they first met when Bajaj was at Google. Building Amazon with his own hands, and working at the frontiers of difficult engineering at Blue Origin, gave Bezos an instinct for the physical economy and hands-on experience that Bajaj calls extraordinarily useful, particularly on product strategy, which he calls a master of. The co-CEOs do not divide responsibilities up. All of them are involved in everything, debating decisions together, and when the moderator asks for an example of a disagreement, Bajaj smiles it off: maybe someday, not today.

The obvious challenge comes next. Amazon backs Anthropic, OpenAI and Anthropic are far ahead and richly resourced, so why build a new frontier AI company, and what stops the incumbents from moving into physical AI? Bajaj's answer is specialization. Even in language models, he argues, there is real specialization: Muse is trained to be an assistant of unusual character, and Anthropic made a wise early decision to focus on code, close to what AI researchers already do every day. Engineering, by contrast, is incredibly specialized, with product cycles far longer than software's. The general intelligence underneath is becoming a commodity, its recipes and compute widely disseminated. Producing both the models and the business models for engineering is, he says, at odds with what the mainstream AI companies are set up to do. It is not that they cannot pivot. It is a different industry.

His biggest challenge right now follows from that. It is finding the meaningful questions that can be answered quickly. A manned turbofan engine has an economic life cycle of about forty years, including maintenance and spares, and that is far too long to test a hypothesis. The work is choosing the beachhead: the elements of those big systems that can read out in days, weeks, months, or years, and growing from there. He calls it the primary challenge, and one shared across all of AI for science and engineering.

On China, Bajaj is blunt about a misreading. Fifteen years ago, before Google, he was an academic with broad research programs, and he got to know his Chinese colleagues and students well. He has come to believe the West is systematically underestimating Chinese competitiveness, working from an anachronistic model of intellectual property theft rather than domestic innovation. Isolation plus emulation of Western science, he says, has produced an innovation economy that in fields he has worked in, quantum information science, semiconductors, materials science, and parts of the life sciences, has centers at parity with the West. If a physical AI competitor emerges, he predicts it will most likely be in China, where there is far more experimentation across the supply chain, far more compute available to the average researcher, and a track record, visible in semiconductors, of embargoes driving alternatives to the processes frontier fabs depend on.

The jobs question gets his most optimistic answer. Prometheus has talked about building an artificial general engineer, and the moderator asks whether that means fewer human engineers. Bajaj points to software. For most of its history, software engineering was a small profession tied to hardware companies. Successive technologies spread the skill, made it less rarefied and more accessible, and created millions of jobs. He predicts coding models, which ostensibly should put software engineers out of business, will instead create millions of new software engineers, because the distance between a person's vision and the people who might enjoy its products keeps shrinking. The physical economy can follow the same path. Factories in places like Shenzhen are largely automated already. The prize is an engineer with undergraduate training soon doing what now takes a team of specialists a long time, which creates new product categories, products specialized to individual consumers, and design cycles that blur hardware and software.

He is equally direct about the AI industry's PR problem. Silicon Valley, he says, is an insular place, and it has done a bad job explaining how this technology benefits ordinary people. For years, some in the industry have told people that they, their children, and their grandchildren will be out of jobs, and warned of existential dangers on top. It is no wonder, he says, that people oppose data centers: they suspect the people profiting from the technology are the same ones warning them about it, with no optimistic framing. The answer is to admit the trust issue and explain the benefits more honestly and completely, including ones already here, like life sciences products reaching the market that save lives.

The Journal reported earlier this year that Bezos was in talks to raise a hundred billion dollars to buy manufacturing companies and bring AI into their operations. Bajaj does not confirm any particular plan. Prometheus, he repeats, is not a normal AI company but a manufacturing company, and it intends to be on the hook for the quality of the products that result, not just the quality of the intelligence layer. There are many mechanisms for partnering with existing businesses, some of them fifty or a hundred years old, on which the supply chain has core dependencies. Pressed on whether an acquisition spree is coming, he laughs it off: the main thing is to be healthy partners to the manufacturing economy, making things that customers want to buy and other businesses come to depend on.

He closes on the name. Prometheus was an internal code name that stuck, and he thinks it is appropriate. The AI community is accessing something new and powerful for the first time, and people reach for mythology to understand both the benefits and the dangers of such things. History, he says, is a guide: as alien as this technology feels, there are precedents for how to deploy it well, preserve its benefits, and minimize its harms.

Open questions

  1. Prometheus insists it will be judged on the quality of finished products, not just its intelligence layer. When its first supply chain components ship, what evidence would show the advantage came from the AI rather than from capital and ordinary manufacturing execution?
  2. Bajaj predicts a physical AI competitor will most likely emerge in China, where experimentation and compute for average researchers exceed America's. If that is the real race, does Prometheus's beachhead strategy move fast enough to matter against a rival that later gains central government backing?
  3. His jobs argument rests on software's history: tools that spread a skill created more jobs than they destroyed. Physical engineering has forty-year product cycles and safety regulation software never faced. Where would the first confirming sign appear, in hiring, in new firms, or in what a single engineer can ship?
