David Senra · Scott Kupor on a16z, venture capital, and fixing the government

a16z's First Employee on the History of a16z, Venture Capital & Fixing the Government | Scott Kupor

On David Senra, the episode "a16z's First Employee on the History of a16z, Venture Capital & Fixing the Government | Scott Kupor" is a conversation with Scott Kupor, who joined Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz in 2009 as the first employee of Andreessen Horowitz and spent sixteen and a half years there as a managing partner. He now directs the U.S. Office of Personnel Management, the agency that serves as the human resources and talent office for the federal government's roughly two million civilian employees. The conversation moves in two arcs: how a venture capital firm was built from three people into an institution of about seven hundred, and what Kupor is trying to change about how the federal government hires, rates, promotes, and retires its own people.

The founding nearly did not include him. Kupor had worked with Andreessen and Horowitz at Loudcloud and Opsware, which Hewlett-Packard bought in 2007, and he was living in North Carolina when the two began planning the firm in the summer of 2008, as Bear Stearns vanished and Lehman Brothers collapsed. He hesitated for months, weighing a stable job at HP against a startup fund in a financial crisis, and only committed around Christmas. The standing joke, he says, is that a faster yes might have made the firm Andreessen Kupor Horowitz. His official status stayed first employee rather than co-founder, and there was no job description: the founders would invest, and he would figure out everything else a firm needs.

The early days were that literal. His first act was a capital call, the request that investors wire money for an investment, and he sent it from his personal Gmail account because he did not yet have a firm email address. An investor wrote back demanding to know who he was, then called Andreessen and Horowitz to report that someone was impersonating them and shaking him down. Before that came office furniture and bank accounts; after it, hiring finance staff so the mistake would not repeat, and then hiring the operators who would become the firm's services arm. The firm grew from three people to roughly one hundred in its first year.

The model came from Hollywood. Michael Ovitz, who had been on the Loudcloud board, built CAA by disaggregating the agent's job: instead of one agent handling everything for an actor, a client who walked into CAA met a table of specialists, each expert in one domain. Andreessen and Horowitz applied the same idea to venture capital, which then ran on the one-to-one model of a connector like John Doerr, who personally knew every salesperson, chief financial officer, and political contact a founder might need. A firm, they argued, could institutionalize that: hire investors for their domain expertise and surround them with full-time professionals running talent networks, business development, government relations, and the rest. When founders pitched, the firm answered with a reverse pitch: not just a board member, but the whole phalanx.

Marketing was the other break with tradition. Venture firms did not market, Kupor says, because for decades capital was scarce and entrepreneurs came to them; the balance of power made marketing unnecessary. A new firm with no track record could not compete on the incumbents' terms, so it competed on a new category instead, capital plus services, and told that story loudly and before it was fully built. Rivals on Sand Hill Road complained that the newcomers talked a big game, which sounded to Kupor like a description of any entrepreneur creating a category: you have to narrate it, and then you have to deliver.

Delivery came fast. Within months of the first fund closing, the firm joined Silver Lake to buy Skype out of eBay, a buyout from a firm that had promised its investors early stage venture investing, and one shadowed by a dispute over the underlying intellectual property that had scared off other buyers. About eighteen months later Microsoft bought Skype for four times the purchase price, and the firm returned roughly half of its first fund to investors. Martin Casado's networking company Nicira also exited early in the first fund, and Casado later joined the firm as a partner. The wins landed while established firms were hunkered down over their portfolios in the crisis, and the second fund they enabled supplied the fee stream that paid for the services build-out. Andreessen and Horowitz took no salary at the start so the money could go to hiring.

Kupor's own role stayed operational for years, by his account an honest fit: early stage investing, he says, demands a granular feel for technology he does not have, and in a room of ten database pitches that depth is the whole job. About ten years in, the firm reversed its structure, decentralizing from one centralized organization, where nearly everything reported to him, into specialist funds, crypto under Chris Dixon, American Dynamism under David Ulevitch, artificial intelligence infrastructure under Casado, with the services teams feathered into each vertical and only legal, finance, compliance, and investor relations kept central. The reorganization shrank his domain, which stung, and also freed him to invest. From 2019 he helped build the firm's later stage venture fund with David George, leaning toward health care, where judgment is about operations and the road from one to infinity rather than zero to one. He left with about fifteen investments and ten board seats.

Policy, though, predates venture capital in his life. He did health care policy research in high school, worked at Stanford for Alan Garber, now the president of Harvard, on prostate cancer screening economics, and later for Jay Bhattacharya, who now runs the National Institutes of Health. With his children grown and Andreessen and Horowitz's blessing, he took the OPM job as a return to that first interest. His theory of the work is deliberately simple: an organization is a collection of people lined up around a set of objectives, and performance follows from having the right people and incentives that reward the behavior the objectives require. Government, he argues, fails mainly on the second term.

The incentive structure he describes is a compliance culture organized around avoiding blame. A senior report told him early on that their job was to keep him out of the news: no congressional hearings, no critical audit reports. The safe strategy that follows is to do nothing new, which is why obvious improvements sit untouched for decades. Kupor's counter is what he calls permissioned risk, risk deliberately sized to the stakes. Where failure can kill people, tolerance should be near zero; where the work is processing retirement applications and tax refunds, the upside of automation dwarfs the cost of an occasional error. His own license to push this, he told the president in his interview, is that he wants no other job when this one ends, so a bad hearing costs him nothing he is protecting.

Performance management is where the incentives show up in numbers. Federal employees are rated on a five point scale, and historically 99.7 percent have been rated three or better, meaning only 0.3 percent fall below expectations, a result he calls mathematically impossible in a workforce of two million. At the top, roughly ninety percent of the roughly seven thousand senior executives receive one of the two highest ratings, so bonuses are spread across nearly everyone and motivate no one, while weak performers face no consequences beside their high performing colleagues. His new rules cap the top rating at no more than forty percent of a workforce and reserve at least sixty percent of the compensation pool for that group. At the same time, his team is eliminating time in grade, the tenure requirement that blocks a promotion for at least a year regardless of performance, so advancement can follow merit instead.

The reason this matters now is demographic. Seven percent of the federal workforce is under thirty, against twenty three percent of the overall U.S. workforce, and forty four percent is over fifty, against about a third nationally; if nothing changes, half the workforce retires within five to ten years with no bench behind it. Kupor's recruiting pitch discards the old promise of lifetime employment, which he calls the worst pitch imaginable for anyone under thirty, and offers a two or three year tour instead: work on genuinely hard problems at NASA, the Energy Department, or elsewhere, build rare experience, and then stay or leave for the private sector with the government's help. The flagship is Tech Force, which aims to hire one thousand software engineers, artificial intelligence experts, and data scientists for two year terms at the GS-14 level, a senior individual contributor grade. Entrants take actual skills assessments, third party coding tests rather than the self-rated questionnaires federal hiring has relied on, and some forty private companies partner on training during the term and a job fair at the end. The model he cites is Israel's military cyber units, whose alumni every firm wants to hire.

His test case for skills based hiring is a nineteen year old software engineer who dropped out of college to join the DOGE effort and works now on the National Design Studio's redesign of federal websites. Under standard rules, lacking a degree and five years of experience, the government would never have hired him, or would have hired him only at the entry level GS-5 grade and a salary Silicon Valley would multiply by ten. Kupor's view is that such a person should enter at GS-14 or GS-15, the top grades, because demonstrated merit says he can perform there, and that job descriptions across government are being rewritten on that principle.

The headcount backdrop is the DOGE reduction, which took the civilian workforce from about 2.3 million when the administration began to about 2 million now, its smallest share of the total workforce since 1960. Kupor, who was nominated in December and planned with the DOGE team before his confirmation kept him sidelined, frames his own job as institutionalizing the change: something drastic was needed to turn a tanker of that size, and his task is the durable part, making agencies state what they are actually working on and accept that some of it should stop, since government only ever adds.

His Exhibit A is retirement. Until last year, a retiring federal employee's paperwork traveled by mail through their agency and a separate payroll office to an underground limestone mine in Boyers, Pennsylvania, where some eight hundred OPM staff read paper files, computed annuities by hand, and took anywhere from three to twelve months to produce a first check, mailing forms back for a missing signature at a month's delay each time. The mine still holds about four hundred million pages of records dating to the 1950s, fetched by runners whenever a retiree's circumstances change. The process is now fully electronic: paper intake ended on June 30, the first iteration has cut processing time by more than half, and the commitment is a complete retirement check within fifteen days of a clean electronic application. Room sized scanners are digitizing the entire archive, which should eventually let the storage mine close. In parallel, his office is consolidating the government's one hundred twenty separate human resources computer systems into one, which he says cuts the annual cost from about two billion dollars to about forty million, and gives the government, for the first time, a single continuous record of an employee's service.

The parts connect, in his telling, into one loop: tell a compelling story and talented people will come for a few years rather than a lifetime; give them a culture where ratings mean something, promotion follows merit, and a sized risk is permitted; and those people will modernize the systems everyone else experiences. The recruiting starts at earlycareers.gov and a tour of about forty college campuses, and his closing offer to a young listener is direct: send him a message, and they will find a way to make something work.

Open questions

  1. The a16z services machine was funded by management fees that early wins like Skype made possible, and it scaled because the firm could pay market salaries for operators. OPM has no equivalent engine: it must fund assessments, digitization, and a technologist corps out of appropriations. What sustains the build-out when the initial push meets a budget cycle or a change of administration?
  2. Capping top ratings at forty percent assumes managers will differentiate honestly once the rules change. The old system was also a set of rules, and managers gamed it into meaninglessness. What in the new design prevents ratings from quietly re-inflating, especially once Kupor, who says he wants nothing afterward, has left?
  3. Tech Force asks elite engineers to accept below market pay for two years in exchange for training, mission, and a partner run job fair at the end. If the program works as designed, its partner companies will hire away much of each cohort. Does the government then gain a durable alumni network and a recruiting brand, or does it mostly subsidize talent development for the firms it partners with?

Nicholas Thompson · Will AI help democracy or dictators, with Daron Acemoglu

Will AI Help Democracy—or Dictators? - With MIT Economist Daron Acemoglu

On Nicholas Thompson, the episode "Will AI Help Democracy—or Dictators? - With MIT Economist Daron Acemoglu" is a conversation from Thompson's show The Most Interesting Thing in AI with Daron Acemoglu, the MIT economist and Nobel laureate whose new book, "What Happened to Liberal Democracy?", argues that the West made the wrong choices with automation and social media, and is at risk of repeating them with AI. Thompson, the CEO of The Atlantic, opens with the question that runs through the whole hour: why did modern technology, expected to foster democracy, end up mainly empowering dictatorships?

Acemoglu starts with the state of liberal democracy itself. His book opens with Viktor Orbán in 1989, then a white knight for liberalism in Hungary, now an illiberal leader who has since lost power. The lesson he draws is not comforting. Leaders like Orbán can be beaten at the ballot box, especially when an opponent comes from broadly the same movement and can say he understands the grievances that put them there. The bigger mistake, he argues, was made by Democrats in 2020, who treated Trump as a unique aberration. Biden then failed to deliver on voice, shared prosperity, and effective public services, and voters put Trump back in power in 2024 despite the guardrails around him having been removed. Liberal democracy, in his account, survives only if it delivers three things: non-corrupt, effective government, a real voice for people, and shared prosperity.

Technology enters the story through inequality. His research with Pascual Restrepo attributes between 50 and 70 percent of the growth in wage gaps between education groups to automation. Digital technology and globalization together built a post-industrial society that shifted work toward cognitive, white collar tasks, weakened unions, raised capital's share of national income, and concentrated extraordinary wealth in a small number of tech companies employing relatively few people. Inequality rose within rich and poor countries alike, even as poorer countries converged toward richer ones.

On AI's economic future, he is candid about uncertainty but firm on one point. AI as currently built is chiefly an automation technology, and its first-order effect over the next decade will be to raise capital's share of income and squeeze labor further. That, he says, is the part he is most certain about. Who captures the money is far less clear. Open weight models and distillation may prevent the frontier labs from collecting the rents their spending assumes, which he would welcome as a route to cheap, widely available AI, while warning that it could also set up a market correction if the huge investments fail to pay off. He also argues the United States is putting its eggs in the wrong basket: nearly all the energy goes to foundation models, while China invests far more in the application layer, integrating AI and robotics into production. The application layer is where he expects the productivity gains to live, and it needs engineers, of whom the United States has a shortage.

Asked which jobs go first, he picks customer service representatives over mid-level managers, and he shares Thompson's list of durable work: jobs built on face-to-face interaction, skilled work done with the hands, engineering in the broad sense, and human creativity. He does not expect true human-level AI across a diverse range of tasks within the next ten years, which is why he thinks innovation and creativity will remain human activities.

The book's most contested argument concerns what he calls social engineering. As the college-educated grew richer and more socially segregated, living, working, and marrying within their own group, they captured the commanding heights of the economy and the center-left parties that once belonged to the working class. Culture became the glue holding that diverse coalition together, and its values were increasingly imposed from the top down, through schools, employers, and rules about acceptable language. The backlash was then harvested by right-wing media and social media, and the result was a collapse of trust that destroyed people's sense of voice. Brexit was his wake-up call: after the referendum, acquaintances in British media said it had been a mistake to ask the people at all, which to him showed how completely the elite had abandoned democracy. Liberal ideas, he insists, do not need to be imposed. They can win on their merits, more slowly, in open debate.

Social media itself is not inherently anti-democratic, he argues. It could have been a democratizing force, and in Taiwan, under digital minister Audrey Tang, it largely was, because the country deliberately built platforms for participation, referendums, and scrutiny of politicians. The failed attempts of the 2010s, including one of Thompson's own, made a common mistake: they tried to fix discourse purely online. Anything that works, he thinks, has to blend the online and the offline, rooted in real communities.

The balance of power question cuts the other way for autocracies. Digital surveillance, he argues, was central to Xi Jinping's strategy of letting China's middle class consume more while clamping down on it politically, and China's AI industry grew up coupled to surveillance data and government procurement. Thompson presses him on Michael Walzer's thought experiment of a pill in the water that would make a whole country love liberal democracy. Acemoglu opposes the pill, as Walzer did, because it erases the community that makes people who they are. He would accept a softer version for AI: models that join the open debate honestly, offer counterarguments, and leave the choice to the individual, rather than secretly nudging people toward approved values.

His positive program is pro-worker AI. Following J.C.R. Licklider's 1960 vision of humans and computers coupled together, he wants AI used as an information technology that gives workers new capabilities and creates new tasks: tools that help electricians, nurses, teachers, and plumbers do harder work, built with the people who hold the tacit knowledge and the data. This, he stresses, is directly opposed to the industry's AGI agenda, which assumes humans soon become unnecessary. Getting there means convincing a large share of the talented, well-meaning people now flowing into Silicon Valley, plus policy, data markets, and a media that does its job. He faults that media for swinging between worshipping tech founders and panicking about killer robots, and for never forcing the democratic debate AI deserves. His regulatory agenda is specific: for social media, abolish algorithmic feeds in favor of chronological ones, since algorithms have no free-speech rights; for AI, replace today's reactive, panic-driven guardrails with a proactive agenda set by public debate. Given an unlimited budget, he would build prototypes of pro-worker AI, as he already does on a shoestring, developing pro-teacher tools in the developing world. One ten-thousandth of what Anthropic or OpenAI spends, he says, would be revolutionary.

Open questions

  1. Acemoglu expects AI, built as an automation technology, to raise capital's share of income and squeeze labor over the next decade. If open models simultaneously make AI cheap for everyone, which of those two forces shows up first in the wage data, and how would we know?
  2. His pro-worker program needs something like half the industry's talent to change direction voluntarily, helped by policy and data markets. Which of those levers actually moves first, and what evidence would show the shift is happening rather than just being talked about?
  3. He wants chronological feeds for social media and a proactive, democratically debated agenda for AI. In a Congress he himself describes as voting with its richest constituents, who writes that agenda, and what keeps it from becoming another round of the social engineering he criticizes?

Latent Space · Behind Latent Space, with swyx and Galen Gao

Behind Latent Space, ft AIEi Shanghai preview — with swyx and Galen Gao

On Latent Space, the episode "Behind Latent Space, ft AIEi Shanghai preview — with swyx and Galen Gao" is a special edition recorded ahead of the first AI Engineer conference in China. Galen Gao interviews swyx, the podcast's host and the founder of the AI Engineer conference series, about the Shanghai edition, which opens the day after this episode was published and is run by a local partner, Standard Partners. The conversation doubles as a retrospective: how Latent Space started, how the term AI engineer was coined, and what swyx has learned running conferences and advising startups.

His own path runs through finance. A fifth-generation Chinese Singaporean, he spent about eight years in trading, investment banking, and hedge funds before moving into tech, working at Amazon and several startups, and going independent four years ago. Today he runs the conference company and the podcast, and advises startups, most substantially Cognition, which acquired his earlier startup vehicle and where he worked for six months before stepping back to an adviser role as the conference business grew. Fireworks, Modal, and Supabase are also on his list.

The principle he credits for the whole career is learning in public. Finance, he argues, is zero-sum: knowledge you share loses value. Tech is positive-sum: knowledge shared tends to gain value, which is why engineers open source their work and admit their mistakes in public. Bored in a software role at Two Sigma under a boss who would not let him do the work he wanted, he poured his energy into writing and speaking, and discovered the side benefit: when you learn in public, you effectively choose your own boss. He is blunt about the flip side, especially for credential-focused cultures. Working hard entitles nobody to win. You have to work hard and be good, and aim the effort at substantial things.

Latent Space itself began as a blog when Stable Diffusion appeared, before ChatGPT. Working at a startup that was going nowhere, swyx started writing up what he was learning about AI, and a post analyzing Stable Diffusion caught the attention of Stability AI chief executive Imad Mostaque. Events and a hackathon followed, and it was at that hackathon that the AI engineer idea landed: a nontechnical attendee asked what she could do in AI, and he realized the honest answer was very little next to an engineer, because engineers were about to direct many AIs at once. Building harnesses around models did not require a research doctorate, but the work had no respected name. The alternatives floated at the time, AI tinkerer, LLM engineer, applied AI engineer, all described activities. He chose a job title instead, reasoning from the identity principle he attributes to James Clear: give people a title they aspire to hold and a community forms around it. The hot topics inside the role have rotated since, from retrieval and context management to coding agents and harness engineering, and he sees AI architect emerging as the company-level counterpart. His test for who counts as an engineer is simple: the person who gets woken at 4 a.m. to fix it.

China ran the same experiment in parallel. Gao describes how China's largest AI community, WayToAI, grew out of Stable Diffusion and ComfyUI enthusiasts at exactly the same moment, sharing documents and use cases on Lark until it reached roughly a million people learning together. That symmetry is part of the case for Shanghai: both ecosystems were built by people learning in public, and each is curious about the other.

The flagship AI Engineer World's Fair drew more than 7,000 people in person this year, with about a million and a half watching online, across some 30 tracks, and every talk and workshop is published free. He has also transcribed and cataloged the entire archive, exposed it through MCP and as a downloadable dataset, describing the ambition as a Wikipedia of AI. Conferences, in his telling, are neutral ground in a way companies cannot be: some Chinese labs sit on the US entity list and cannot officially take meetings with American firms, but people still run into each other in the same building, and he jokes that his top KPI is the number of marriages and babies the events have produced.

Expansion runs on a franchise model borrowed from JSConf. Local partners license the brand, the channel, and the audience under three conditions: do not abuse the name, publish everything, and maintain a code of conduct. Paris came first, and its organizers were later acquired into Mistral. Singapore followed, with India and Japan in progress. China is the hard case, because US-China restrictions mean swyx cannot own or operate there, so the Shanghai edition belongs entirely to the local team. His analogy is McDonald's: he is the franchisor, they are McDonald's China.

The longest segment revisits his Kill My SaaS contest. Conference budgets run from three to ten million dollars, and roughly three hundred thousand dollars per conference was going to software he describes as slow, ugly, and never quite right, with feature requests queued for quarters. So he took one tool his team was about to pay forty thousand dollars for and offered a ten thousand dollar prize for an open source clone, built with coding agents and tailored to his company. Sixty-nine submissions arrived in a week; about five were good enough that he would have paid for them. The winner replaced a speaker-review workflow of endless one-by-one clicking with a single reorderable table, a view the vendor would have taken six months to consider. The AI judge in the contest only screened for valid submissions; humans chose the winners, because he could not specify what he wanted until he had seen seventy versions. He compares the format to 99designs, the contest site where the Latent Space logo itself came from four hundred entries, and argues there should be a 99designs for SaaS: a service that runs the whole cloning contest for a business owner who lacks his audience.

The underlying claim is aimed at mid-tier SaaS. Models, he says, are now very good at clicking through every screen of a product and replicating it, so a vendor with fifty customers paying forty thousand dollars each can watch the business evaporate once those customers notice. Salesforce-scale incumbents are safe; everyone in the middle must either own a vertical as its leading agent, his examples are Harvey and OpenEvidence, or own specialized data. For business owners, though, he calls this a golden age. He runs his own versions of a scheduling link tool, a file drive, a code host, a chat tool, and a document-signing tool, and because the last two are his, a signed document lands directly in the right folder, an integration the commercial product never offered. Maintenance is a real cost, he concedes, but coding agents absorb much of it, and for a database with a form on top he will happily trade an occasional bad day for total customizability.

Gao also asks about Jev, the subject of a recent episode with its creator, Diego. The idea swyx endorses is what Diego calls system 1 models: instead of one large model sitting in a chat window at the edge of dumb software, a small model is placed inside the path of ordinary code, making choices and estimating probabilities as casually as an if statement, so software is smarter at every point rather than at one point. Reliability matters more than speed, which is why the company is called Typesafe. Diego succeeded where earlier small-model advocates had not, swyx argues, because of his credibility as a co-author of the work behind ChatGPT, a framing people could repeat, and showmanship, capped by a demo of the model playing Doom.

On advising, he has worked with some thirty-five companies and treats that sample size as the product: advice tested at one company is an anecdote, while patterns that survive thirty-five are close to universal. Engineer and researcher founders, including the Chinese founders Gao works with who are trying to go global, are typically strong technically and weak at go-to-market, and their biggest mistake is sequencing. Marketing brought in the night before a launch can change nothing; brought in before the product is specced, it shapes what gets built. His core principle is show, don't tell: a working demo beats any deck. Taste, he says, is the ability to narrow a thousand possibilities quickly and cheaply to the one that afterwards looks obvious. The iPhone's touchscreen is his historical example; Cursor is his current one, since you know you have taste when the big labs copy what you shipped, and he puts the legal tool Legora in the same class.

His Shanghai expectations are mostly about contrast. ComfyUI and OpenClaw caught on in China far more than in San Francisco. A community of a million people counts as mid-sized there. The 996 schedule is real in a way San Francisco's version is not. He is unsparing about founder quality: the only reason some Chinese founders are not richer, he says, is that they live in China, and in the US they would be beating Amazon and Meta. He remembers BYD as an up-and-coming local brand during a 2007 study abroad, and notes that Alibaba's full Qwen roadmap presentation reached almost nobody in the US purely because of its corporate format. In the closing quick-fire round, he names interaction models, full-duplex systems that listen while they speak, as his most exciting under-the-radar development, alongside continual learning and world models. His worries are an eventual AI winter, a genuine threat to a hype-funded conference business, and safety: his stated default is to assume AI doom, on the historical argument that when a more advanced civilization meets a less advanced one, the less advanced one tends to go extinct. He also describes an experiment in asking AI models two hundred "best of" questions and publishing the rankings, where Latent Space lands between first and third for AI podcasts for engineers. Gao closes hoping Shanghai covers infrastructure topics like safety and payments, brings frontier researchers from both sides, and earns a second edition next year.

Open questions

  1. The franchise model hands swyx's brand to local partners he cannot supervise daily, and in China he cannot legally operate at all.
