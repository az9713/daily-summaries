David Senra · Scott Kupor on a16z, venture capital, and fixing the government

a16z's First Employee on the History of a16z, Venture Capital & Fixing the Government | Scott Kupor

On David Senra, the episode "a16z's First Employee on the History of a16z, Venture Capital & Fixing the Government | Scott Kupor" is a conversation with Scott Kupor, who joined Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz in 2009 as the first employee of Andreessen Horowitz and spent sixteen and a half years there as a managing partner. He now directs the U.S. Office of Personnel Management, the agency that serves as the human resources and talent office for the federal government's roughly two million civilian employees. The conversation moves in two arcs: how a venture capital firm was built from three people into an institution of about seven hundred, and what Kupor is trying to change about how the federal government hires, rates, promotes, and retires its own people.

The founding nearly did not include him. Kupor had worked with Andreessen and Horowitz at Loudcloud and Opsware, which Hewlett-Packard bought in 2007, and he was living in North Carolina when the two began planning the firm in the summer of 2008, as Bear Stearns vanished and Lehman Brothers collapsed. He hesitated for months, weighing a stable job at HP against a startup fund in a financial crisis, and only committed around Christmas. The standing joke, he says, is that a faster yes might have made the firm Andreessen Kupor Horowitz. His official status stayed first employee rather than co-founder, and there was no job description: the founders would invest, and he would figure out everything else a firm needs.

The early days were that literal. His first act was a capital call, the request that investors wire money for an investment, and he sent it from his personal Gmail account because he did not yet have a firm email address. An investor wrote back demanding to know who he was, then called Andreessen and Horowitz to report that someone was impersonating them and shaking him down. Before that came office furniture and bank accounts; after it, hiring finance staff so the mistake would not repeat, and then hiring the operators who would become the firm's services arm. The firm grew from three people to roughly one hundred in its first year.

The model came from Hollywood. Michael Ovitz, who had been on the Loudcloud board, built CAA by disaggregating the agent's job: instead of one agent handling everything for an actor, a client who walked into CAA met a table of specialists, each expert in one domain. Andreessen and Horowitz applied the same idea to venture capital, which then ran on the one-to-one model of a connector like John Doerr, who personally knew every salesperson, chief financial officer, and political contact a founder might need. A firm, they argued, could institutionalize that: hire investors for their domain expertise and surround them with full-time professionals running talent networks, business development, government relations, and the rest. When founders pitched, the firm answered with a reverse pitch: not just a board member, but the whole phalanx.

Marketing was the other break with tradition. Venture firms did not market, Kupor says, because for decades capital was scarce and entrepreneurs came to them; the balance of power made marketing unnecessary. A new firm with no track record could not compete on the incumbents' terms, so it competed on a new category instead, capital plus services, and told that story loudly and before it was fully built. Rivals on Sand Hill Road complained that the newcomers talked a big game, which sounded to Kupor like a description of any entrepreneur creating a category: you have to narrate it, and then you have to deliver.

Delivery came fast. Within months of the first fund closing, the firm joined Silver Lake to buy Skype out of eBay, a buyout from a firm that had promised its investors early stage venture investing, and one shadowed by a dispute over the underlying intellectual property that had scared off other buyers. About eighteen months later Microsoft bought Skype for four times the purchase price, and the firm returned roughly half of its first fund to investors. Martin Casado's networking company Nicira also exited early in the first fund, and Casado later joined the firm as a partner. The wins landed while established firms were hunkered down over their portfolios in the crisis, and the second fund they enabled supplied the fee stream that paid for the services build-out. Andreessen and Horowitz took no salary at the start so the money could go to hiring.

Kupor's own role stayed operational for years, by his account an honest fit: early stage investing, he says, demands a granular feel for technology he does not have, and in a room of ten database pitches that depth is the whole job. About ten years in, the firm reversed its structure, decentralizing from one centralized organization, where nearly everything reported to him, into specialist funds, crypto under Chris Dixon, American Dynamism under David Ulevitch, artificial intelligence infrastructure under Casado, with the services teams feathered into each vertical and only legal, finance, compliance, and investor relations kept central. The reorganization shrank his domain, which stung, and also freed him to invest. From 2019 he helped build the firm's later stage venture fund with David George, leaning toward health care, where judgment is about operations and the road from one to infinity rather than zero to one. He left with about fifteen investments and ten board seats.

Policy, though, predates venture capital in his life. He did health care policy research in high school, worked at Stanford for Alan Garber, now the president of Harvard, on prostate cancer screening economics, and later for Jay Bhattacharya, who now runs the National Institutes of Health. With his children grown and Andreessen and Horowitz's blessing, he took the OPM job as a return to that first interest. His theory of the work is deliberately simple: an organization is a collection of people lined up around a set of objectives, and performance follows from having the right people and incentives that reward the behavior the objectives require. Government, he argues, fails mainly on the second term.

The incentive structure he describes is a compliance culture organized around avoiding blame. A senior report told him early on that their job was to keep him out of the news: no congressional hearings, no critical audit reports. The safe strategy that follows is to do nothing new, which is why obvious improvements sit untouched for decades. Kupor's counter is what he calls permissioned risk, risk deliberately sized to the stakes. Where failure can kill people, tolerance should be near zero; where the work is processing retirement applications and tax refunds, the upside of automation dwarfs the cost of an occasional error. His own license to push this, he told the president in his interview, is that he wants no other job when this one ends, so a bad hearing costs him nothing he is protecting.

Performance management is where the incentives show up in numbers. Federal employees are rated on a five point scale, and historically 99.7 percent have been rated three or better, meaning only 0.3 percent fall below expectations, a result he calls mathematically impossible in a workforce of two million. At the top, roughly ninety percent of the roughly seven thousand senior executives receive one of the two highest ratings, so bonuses are spread across nearly everyone and motivate no one, while weak performers face no consequences beside their high performing colleagues. His new rules cap the top rating at no more than forty percent of a workforce and reserve at least sixty percent of the compensation pool for that group. At the same time, his team is eliminating time in grade, the tenure requirement that blocks a promotion for at least a year regardless of performance, so advancement can follow merit instead.

The reason this matters now is demographic. Seven percent of the federal workforce is under thirty, against twenty three percent of the overall U.S. workforce, and forty four percent is over fifty, against about a third nationally; if nothing changes, half the workforce retires within five to ten years with no bench behind it. Kupor's recruiting pitch discards the old promise of lifetime employment, which he calls the worst pitch imaginable for anyone under thirty, and offers a two or three year tour instead: work on genuinely hard problems at NASA, the Energy Department, or elsewhere, build rare experience, and then stay or leave for the private sector with the government's help. The flagship is Tech Force, which aims to hire one thousand software engineers, artificial intelligence experts, and data scientists for two year terms at the GS-14 level, a senior individual contributor grade. Entrants take actual skills assessments, third party coding tests rather than the self-rated questionnaires federal hiring has relied on, and some forty private companies partner on training during the term and a job fair at the end. The model he cites is Israel's military cyber units, whose alumni every firm wants to hire.

His test case for skills based hiring is a nineteen year old software engineer who dropped out of college to join the DOGE effort and works now on the National Design Studio's redesign of federal websites. Under standard rules, lacking a degree and five years of experience, the government would never have hired him, or would have hired him only at the entry level GS-5 grade and a salary Silicon Valley would multiply by ten. Kupor's view is that such a person should enter at GS-14 or GS-15, the top grades, because demonstrated merit says he can perform there, and that job descriptions across government are being rewritten on that principle.

The headcount backdrop is the DOGE reduction, which took the civilian workforce from about 2.3 million when the administration began to about 2 million now, its smallest share of the total workforce since 1960. Kupor, who was nominated in December and planned with the DOGE team before his confirmation kept him sidelined, frames his own job as institutionalizing the change: something drastic was needed to turn a tanker of that size, and his task is the durable part, making agencies state what they are actually working on and accept that some of it should stop, since government only ever adds.

His Exhibit A is retirement. Until last year, a retiring federal employee's paperwork traveled by mail through their agency and a separate payroll office to an underground limestone mine in Boyers, Pennsylvania, where some eight hundred OPM staff read paper files, computed annuities by hand, and took anywhere from three to twelve months to produce a first check, mailing forms back for a missing signature at a month's delay each time. The mine still holds about four hundred million pages of records dating to the 1950s, fetched by runners whenever a retiree's circumstances change. The process is now fully electronic: paper intake ended on June 30, the first iteration has cut processing time by more than half, and the commitment is a complete retirement check within fifteen days of a clean electronic application. Room sized scanners are digitizing the entire archive, which should eventually let the storage mine close. In parallel, his office is consolidating the government's one hundred twenty separate human resources computer systems into one, which he says cuts the annual cost from about two billion dollars to about forty million, and gives the government, for the first time, a single continuous record of an employee's service.

The parts connect, in his telling, into one loop: tell a compelling story and talented people will come for a few years rather than a lifetime; give them a culture where ratings mean something, promotion follows merit, and a sized risk is permitted; and those people will modernize the systems everyone else experiences. The recruiting starts at earlycareers.gov and a tour of about forty college campuses, and his closing offer to a young listener is direct: send him a message, and they will find a way to make something work.

Open questions

  1. The a16z services machine was funded by management fees that early wins like Skype made possible, and it scaled because the firm could pay market salaries for operators. OPM has no equivalent engine: it must fund assessments, digitization, and a technologist corps out of appropriations. What sustains the build-out when the initial push meets a budget cycle or a change of administration?
  2. Capping top ratings at forty percent assumes managers will differentiate honestly once the rules change. The old system was also a set of rules, and managers gamed it into meaninglessness. What in the new design prevents ratings from quietly re-inflating, especially once Kupor, who says he wants nothing afterward, has left?
  3. Tech Force asks elite engineers to accept below market pay for two years in exchange for training, mission, and a partner run job fair at the end. If the program works as designed, its partner companies will hire away much of each cohort. Does the government then gain a durable alumni network and a recruiting brand, or does it mostly subsidize talent development for the firms it partners with?

Nicholas Thompson · Will AI help democracy or dictators, with Daron Acemoglu

Will AI Help Democracy—or Dictators? - With MIT Economist Daron Acemoglu

On Nicholas Thompson, the episode "Will AI Help Democracy—or Dictators? - With MIT Economist Daron Acemoglu" is a conversation from Thompson's show The Most Interesting Thing in AI with Daron Acemoglu, the MIT economist and Nobel laureate whose new book, "What Happened to Liberal Democracy?", argues that the West made the wrong choices with automation and social media, and is at risk of repeating them with AI. Thompson, the CEO of The Atlantic, opens with the question that runs through the whole hour: why did modern technology, expected to foster democracy, end up mainly empowering dictatorships?

Acemoglu starts with the state of liberal democracy itself. His book opens with Viktor Orbán in 1989, then a white knight for liberalism in Hungary, now an illiberal leader who has since lost power. The lesson he draws is not comforting. Leaders like Orbán can be beaten at the ballot box, especially when an opponent comes from broadly the same movement and can say he understands the grievances that put them there. The bigger mistake, he argues, was made by Democrats in 2020, who treated Trump as a unique aberration. Biden then failed to deliver on voice, shared prosperity, and effective public services, and voters put Trump back in power in 2024 despite the guardrails around him having been removed. Liberal democracy, in his account, survives only if it delivers three things: non-corrupt, effective government, a real voice for people, and shared prosperity.

Technology enters the story through inequality. His research with Pascual Restrepo attributes between 50 and 70 percent of the growth in wage gaps between education groups to automation. Digital technology and globalization together built a post-industrial society that shifted work toward cognitive, white collar tasks, weakened unions, raised capital's share of national income, and concentrated extraordinary wealth in a small number of tech companies employing relatively few people. Inequality rose within rich and poor countries alike, even as poorer countries converged toward richer ones.

On AI's economic future, he is candid about uncertainty but firm on one point. AI as currently built is chiefly an automation technology, and its first-order effect over the next decade will be to raise capital's share of income and squeeze labor further. That, he says, is the part he is most certain about. Who captures the money is far less clear. Open weight models and distillation may prevent the frontier labs from collecting the rents their spending assumes, which he would welcome as a route to cheap, widely available AI, while warning that it could also set up a market correction if the huge investments fail to pay off. He also argues the United States is putting its eggs in the wrong basket: nearly all the energy goes to foundation models, while China invests far more in the application layer, integrating AI and robotics into production. The application layer is where he expects the productivity gains to live, and it needs engineers, of whom the United States has a shortage.

Asked which jobs go first, he picks customer service representatives over mid-level managers, and he shares Thompson's list of durable work: jobs built on face-to-face interaction, skilled work done with the hands, engineering in the broad sense, and human creativity. He does not expect true human-level AI across a diverse range of tasks within the next ten years, which is why he thinks innovation and creativity will remain human activities.

The book's most contested argument concerns what he calls social engineering. As the college-educated grew richer and more socially segregated, living, working, and marrying within their own group, they captured the commanding heights of the economy and the center-left parties that once belonged to the working class. Culture became the glue holding that diverse coalition together, and its values were increasingly imposed from the top down, through schools, employers, and rules about acceptable language. The backlash was then harvested by right-wing media and social media, and the result was a collapse of trust that destroyed people's sense of voice. Brexit was his wake-up call: after the referendum, acquaintances in British media said it had been a mistake to ask the people at all, which to him showed how completely the elite had abandoned democracy. Liberal ideas, he insists, do not need to be imposed. They can win on their merits, more slowly, in open debate.

Social media itself is not inherently anti-democratic, he argues. It could have been a democratizing force, and in Taiwan, under digital minister Audrey Tang, it largely was, because the country deliberately built platforms for participation, referendums, and scrutiny of politicians. The failed attempts of the 2010s, including one of Thompson's own, made a common mistake: they tried to fix discourse purely online. Anything that works, he thinks, has to blend the online and the offline, rooted in real communities.

The balance of power question cuts the other way for autocracies. Digital surveillance, he argues, was central to Xi Jinping's strategy of letting China's middle class consume more while clamping down on it politically, and China's AI industry grew up coupled to surveillance data and government procurement. Thompson presses him on Michael Walzer's thought experiment of a pill in the water that would make a whole country love liberal democracy. Acemoglu opposes the pill, as Walzer did, because it erases the community that makes people who they are. He would accept a softer version for AI: models that join the open debate honestly, offer counterarguments, and leave the choice to the individual, rather than secretly nudging people toward approved values.

His positive program is pro-worker AI. Following J.C.R. Licklider's 1960 vision of humans and computers coupled together, he wants AI used as an information technology that gives workers new capabilities and creates new tasks: tools that help electricians, nurses, teachers, and plumbers do harder work, built with the people who hold the tacit knowledge and the data. This, he stresses, is directly opposed to the industry's AGI agenda, which assumes humans soon become unnecessary. Getting there means convincing a large share of the talented, well-meaning people now flowing into Silicon Valley, plus policy, data markets, and a media that does its job. He faults that media for swinging between worshipping tech founders and panicking about killer robots, and for never forcing the democratic debate AI deserves. His regulatory agenda is specific: for social media, abolish algorithmic feeds in favor of chronological ones, since algorithms have no free-speech rights; for AI, replace today's reactive, panic-driven guardrails with a proactive agenda set by public debate. Given an unlimited budget, he would build prototypes of pro-worker AI, as he already does on a shoestring, developing pro-teacher tools in the developing world. One ten-thousandth of what Anthropic or OpenAI spends, he says, would be revolutionary.

Open questions

  1. Acemoglu expects AI, built as an automation technology, to raise capital's share of income and squeeze labor over the next decade. If open models simultaneously make AI cheap for everyone, which of those two forces shows up first in the wage data, and how would we know?
  2. His pro-worker program needs something like half the industry's talent to change direction voluntarily, helped by policy and data markets. Which of those levers actually moves first, and what evidence would show the shift is happening rather than just being talked about?
  3. He wants chronological feeds for social media and a proactive, democratically debated agenda for AI. In a Congress he himself describes as voting with its richest constituents, who writes that agenda, and what keeps it from becoming another round of the social engineering he criticizes?

Latent Space · Behind Latent Space, with swyx and Galen Gao

Behind Latent Space, ft AIEi Shanghai preview — with swyx and Galen Gao

On Latent Space, the episode "Behind Latent Space, ft AIEi Shanghai preview — with swyx and Galen Gao" is a special edition recorded ahead of the first AI Engineer conference in China. Galen Gao interviews swyx, the podcast's host and the founder of the AI Engineer conference series, about the Shanghai edition, which opens the day after this episode was published and is run by a local partner, Standard Partners. The conversation doubles as a retrospective: how Latent Space started, how the term AI engineer was coined, and what swyx has learned running conferences and advising startups.

His own path runs through finance. A fifth-generation Chinese Singaporean, he spent about eight years in trading, investment banking, and hedge funds before moving into tech, working at Amazon and several startups, and going independent four years ago. Today he runs the conference company and the podcast, and advises startups, most substantially Cognition, which acquired his earlier startup vehicle and where he worked for six months before stepping back to an adviser role as the conference business grew. Fireworks, Modal, and Supabase are also on his list.

The principle he credits for the whole career is learning in public. Finance, he argues, is zero-sum: knowledge you share loses value. Tech is positive-sum: knowledge shared tends to gain value, which is why engineers open source their work and admit their mistakes in public. Bored in a software role at Two Sigma under a boss who would not let him do the work he wanted, he poured his energy into writing and speaking, and discovered the side benefit: when you learn in public, you effectively choose your own boss. He is blunt about the flip side, especially for credential-focused cultures. Working hard entitles nobody to win. You have to work hard and be good, and aim the effort at substantial things.

Latent Space itself began as a blog when Stable Diffusion appeared, before ChatGPT. Working at a startup that was going nowhere, swyx started writing up what he was learning about AI, and a post analyzing Stable Diffusion caught the attention of Stability AI chief executive Imad Mostaque. Events and a hackathon followed, and it was at that hackathon that the AI engineer idea landed: a nontechnical attendee asked what she could do in AI, and he realized the honest answer was very little next to an engineer, because engineers were about to direct many AIs at once. Building harnesses around models did not require a research doctorate, but the work had no respected name. The alternatives floated at the time, AI tinkerer, LLM engineer, applied AI engineer, all described activities. He chose a job title instead, reasoning from the identity principle he attributes to James Clear: give people a title they aspire to hold and a community forms around it. The hot topics inside the role have rotated since, from retrieval and context management to coding agents and harness engineering, and he sees AI architect emerging as the company-level counterpart. His test for who counts as an engineer is simple: the person who gets woken at 4 a.m. to fix it.

China ran the same experiment in parallel. Gao describes how China's largest AI community, WayToAI, grew out of Stable Diffusion and ComfyUI enthusiasts at exactly the same moment, sharing documents and use cases on Lark until it reached roughly a million people learning together. That symmetry is part of the case for Shanghai: both ecosystems were built by people learning in public, and each is curious about the other.

The flagship AI Engineer World's Fair drew more than 7,000 people in person this year, with about a million and a half watching online, across some 30 tracks, and every talk and workshop is published free. He has also transcribed and cataloged the entire archive, exposed it through MCP and as a downloadable dataset, describing the ambition as a Wikipedia of AI. Conferences, in his telling, are neutral ground in a way companies cannot be: some Chinese labs sit on the US entity list and cannot officially take meetings with American firms, but people still run into each other in the same building, and he jokes that his top KPI is the number of marriages and babies the events have produced.

Expansion runs on a franchise model borrowed from JSConf. Local partners license the brand, the channel, and the audience under three conditions: do not abuse the name, publish everything, and maintain a code of conduct. Paris came first, and its organizers were later acquired into Mistral. Singapore followed, with India and Japan in progress. China is the hard case, because US-China restrictions mean swyx cannot own or operate there, so the Shanghai edition belongs entirely to the local team. His analogy is McDonald's: he is the franchisor, they are McDonald's China.

The longest segment revisits his Kill My SaaS contest. Conference budgets run from three to ten million dollars, and roughly three hundred thousand dollars per conference was going to software he describes as slow, ugly, and never quite right, with feature requests queued for quarters. So he took one tool his team was about to pay forty thousand dollars for and offered a ten thousand dollar prize for an open source clone, built with coding agents and tailored to his company. Sixty-nine submissions arrived in a week; about five were good enough that he would have paid for them. The winner replaced a speaker-review workflow of endless one-by-one clicking with a single reorderable table, a view the vendor would have taken six months to consider. The AI judge in the contest only screened for valid submissions; humans chose the winners, because he could not specify what he wanted until he had seen seventy versions. He compares the format to 99designs, the contest site where the Latent Space logo itself came from four hundred entries, and argues there should be a 99designs for SaaS: a service that runs the whole cloning contest for a business owner who lacks his audience.

The underlying claim is aimed at mid-tier SaaS. Models, he says, are now very good at clicking through every screen of a product and replicating it, so a vendor with fifty customers paying forty thousand dollars each can watch the business evaporate once those customers notice. Salesforce-scale incumbents are safe; everyone in the middle must either own a vertical as its leading agent, his examples are Harvey and OpenEvidence, or own specialized data. For business owners, though, he calls this a golden age. He runs his own versions of a scheduling link tool, a file drive, a code host, a chat tool, and a document-signing tool, and because the last two are his, a signed document lands directly in the right folder, an integration the commercial product never offered. Maintenance is a real cost, he concedes, but coding agents absorb much of it, and for a database with a form on top he will happily trade an occasional bad day for total customizability.

Gao also asks about Jev, the subject of a recent episode with its creator, Diego. The idea swyx endorses is what Diego calls system 1 models: instead of one large model sitting in a chat window at the edge of dumb software, a small model is placed inside the path of ordinary code, making choices and estimating probabilities as casually as an if statement, so software is smarter at every point rather than at one point. Reliability matters more than speed, which is why the company is called Typesafe. Diego succeeded where earlier small-model advocates had not, swyx argues, because of his credibility as a co-author of the work behind ChatGPT, a framing people could repeat, and showmanship, capped by a demo of the model playing Doom.

On advising, he has worked with some thirty-five companies and treats that sample size as the product: advice tested at one company is an anecdote, while patterns that survive thirty-five are close to universal. Engineer and researcher founders, including the Chinese founders Gao works with who are trying to go global, are typically strong technically and weak at go-to-market, and their biggest mistake is sequencing. Marketing brought in the night before a launch can change nothing; brought in before the product is specced, it shapes what gets built. His core principle is show, don't tell: a working demo beats any deck. Taste, he says, is the ability to narrow a thousand possibilities quickly and cheaply to the one that afterwards looks obvious. The iPhone's touchscreen is his historical example; Cursor is his current one, since you know you have taste when the big labs copy what you shipped, and he puts the legal tool Legora in the same class.

His Shanghai expectations are mostly about contrast. ComfyUI and OpenClaw caught on in China far more than in San Francisco. A community of a million people counts as mid-sized there. The 996 schedule is real in a way San Francisco's version is not. He is unsparing about founder quality: the only reason some Chinese founders are not richer, he says, is that they live in China, and in the US they would be beating Amazon and Meta. He remembers BYD as an up-and-coming local brand during a 2007 study abroad, and notes that Alibaba's full Qwen roadmap presentation reached almost nobody in the US purely because of its corporate format. In the closing quick-fire round, he names interaction models, full-duplex systems that listen while they speak, as his most exciting under-the-radar development, alongside continual learning and world models. His worries are an eventual AI winter, a genuine threat to a hype-funded conference business, and safety: his stated default is to assume AI doom, on the historical argument that when a more advanced civilization meets a less advanced one, the less advanced one tends to go extinct. He also describes an experiment in asking AI models two hundred "best of" questions and publishing the rankings, where Latent Space lands between first and third for AI podcasts for engineers. In Good Company · NextEra Energy CEO John Ketchum on powering AI and nuclear's comeback

NextEra Energy CEO: Powering AI and Nuclear's Comeback | Podcast | In Good Company

On In Good Company, the podcast of Norges Bank Investment Management, host Nicolai Tangen, who runs the Norwegian sovereign wealth fund, sits down with John Ketchum, the president and CEO of NextEra Energy, the world's most valuable power company. The fund owns about 1.5 percent of NextEra, worth more than two billion dollars, so this is an owner interviewing one of his largest holdings. The timing is pointed: AI has turned electricity into a strategic bottleneck, and NextEra has just agreed to buy Dominion Energy in what Tangen calls the biggest merger in the industry's history.

Ketchum describes NextEra first of all as a builder. It is, he says, the only American power company fully diversified across the whole energy value chain, building in nearly every state, and over the last twenty years it built more than its next twenty largest peers combined. The pitch is that scale compounds: the company builds cheaper, buys cheaper, operates cheaper, and finances cheaper than rivals, and it plans three steps ahead. Its regulated Florida utility, Florida Power & Light, is his Exhibit A. Customer bills there have fallen about 20 percent in real terms over twenty years and sit 37 percent below the national average, while reliability runs 60 percent better than the national average.

That record is the argument for the Dominion deal. Dominion is a well run company in three growth states, Virginia, North Carolina, and South Carolina, where the binding constraint is affordability. NextEra has committed to bring a thousand new jobs to Virginia, six hundred of its own and four hundred through supplier commitments, and to build a new office tower there as a dual headquarters. The investment program behind all this is enormous. Tangen puts the spending at roughly sixty billion dollars a year between 2027 and 2032, a billion dollars a week, and Ketchum describes it as a fifty state capital investment plan. The bottlenecks are physical: turbines, transformers, solar panels, inverters, batteries. Scale is his answer there too, since the world's largest buyer gets equipment first and cheapest, and NextEra is already buying through 2030.

The reason for the spending is a demand shock nobody in the industry forecast. American power demand was flat for decades and is now expected to grow sixfold by the middle of the 2040s. Ketchum says the AI driver dawned on the company two or three years ago and exploded in the last twenty four months. Data centres get the headlines, but he stresses the demand is broader: reshored factories, LNG terminals, semiconductor plants, transport, and homes all using more electricity. What the hyperscalers need above all is speed to power, and the build times differ sharply by technology: wind in about twelve months, solar in eighteen, battery storage in fifteen, and gas plants in three or four years.

One deliberate choice is what NextEra will not do. About three years ago it bought a couple of small data centres to learn the business, then decided to leave ownership to the hyperscalers and instead sell them land and build the generation next to it. Ketchum's reasoning is that the hyperscalers themselves insist on owning and controlling their facilities, for security and because the chips inside keep changing, so a power company that demands to build both halves would cannibalise its own opportunities. Sticking to power, transmission, pipelines, and marketing is, in his telling, simply the better business.

The return of gas follows from physics. Data centres need power around the clock, and today's four hour batteries, even stacked to eight hours alongside solar, cannot carry a training cluster through the night. Baseload has to come from somewhere, and for now that means gas fired generation, at two to three times the cost of solar. Nuclear is the longer dated answer, and Ketchum treats it in two parts. Restarting an existing plant is very doable: NextEra is bringing back the Duane Arnold reactor, shut down a few years ago and left in good shape, under a deal in which Google buys the power for twenty five years. He calls a restart a super outage, hard work but with all the nuts and bolts already in place.

Small modular reactors are a different story. Around ninety six manufacturers claim to build them, and NextEra has narrowed the field to three or four it considers viable, favouring Generation 3 designs. The obstacle is not the engineering but the risk: nobody yet knows the final cost of a first of a kind plant, and Ketchum flatly refuses to put that last dollar risk on his customers or take much of it on his shareholders. His answer is a stack of wallets, a capped contribution from NextEra, plus the hyperscaler customer, the construction contractor, the manufacturer, and a federal backstop. Until that stack exists, he expects small reactors to remain a technology of the middle of the next decade at the earliest.

Running through the episode is a political bargain Ketchum returns to repeatedly: data centres must pay their own way. NextEra and Dominion have both signed the ratepayer protection pledge, promising that large new loads fund their own generation and transmission rather than pushing costs onto households, and Florida has written one of the country's most protective large load tariffs into statute. On the wider backlash against data centres, he points to responsible development, closed loop cooling, recycled water, setbacks, local taxes, and money given back to communities, and he argues that AI inside NextEra has so far created more work for its people rather than layoffs. Asked what could go wrong with the AI buildout, his answer is geopolitical: if the United States opted out, China and India would take the economic lead, and American jobs would suffer more, not less. The only scenario in which his demand forecast breaks, he says, is a global recession that cuts AI investment itself.

The last third of the conversation is about leadership, and it opens with the line that cold opens the episode: Ketchum tells his team that anyone, from a twenty five year old new hire to a twenty year veteran, should push back when he has a blind spot. He defines good leadership as a clear and digestible strategy, values a team can trust, and surrounding yourself with people who cover your weaknesses. His own strengths, he says, are handling many things at once, allocating capital, managing stakeholders, and setting a vision, embodied in the company's 12 Ways to Grow strategy. NextEra even runs ten year forecasts, because investments made today may not come online until year five. Once a year, in a programme running since about 2013 and renamed Rewire this year around AI, business units pitch hundreds of their own improvement ideas to an executive committee, and the winning ideas are folded directly into the financial plan.

His own path ran from finance and banking into tax law, then twelve different jobs across twenty four years at NextEra after joining as a line lawyer in 2002. His mentors include three former company leaders, Jim Robo, Maury Dewhurst, and Lew Hay, and his identical twin brother flies for Southwest Airlines. His named failure is instructive: a 2012 solar venture in Spain, where a recession era cut to the feed in tariff hurt the projects and Ketchum was sent to work them out. The lesson hardened into company doctrine, a toe in the water approach to new bets, and a willingness to fail fast and admit error. He describes himself as family first and a workaholic at once, up at five thirty every day to run, bike, or swim, because exercise is how he manages stress. His advice to young people closes the episode: pick the employer that will build your career and rotate you through real challenges, not the job title you happen to want first.

Open questions

  1. Ketchum's whole programme rests on power demand rising sixfold by the mid 2040s, and he can name only a global recession as the thing that breaks it. If AI capital spending merely plateaus instead of collapsing, how much of the sixty billion dollars a year still earns its keep, and which projects get cut first?
  2. Small modular reactors wait on someone else taking the last dollar risk, and his candidate stack leans heavily on a federal backstop. If that backstop never becomes law, does the technology stay a pilot forever, or do the hyperscalers eventually decide the risk is worth owning themselves?
  3. The ratepayer protection pledge is voluntary, and its force depends on regulators in each state holding large loads to their own costs. As the Dominion merger needs approvals in three states, what happens to the pledge if a state commission trades it away for jobs or speed?

Arena · Nathan Benaich on the State of AI Report and recursive self-improvement

Is AI Building AI Yet? Nathan Benaich on the State of AI Report and Recursive Self-Improvement

On Arena, the episode "Is AI Building AI Yet? Nathan Benaich on the State of AI Report and Recursive Self-Improvement" is a conversation with Nathan Benaich, founder and general partner of Air Street Capital and author of the State of AI Report. This year's edition runs to about 250 slides, covers research, industry, politics, and safety, and closes with a set of twelve-month predictions designed to be graded. Benaich peer reviews the report with roughly fifty practitioners, keeps it free, and describes it as a labor of love that has become something like an online town hall for the field. Four things in this year's edition surprised even him: early progress in recursive self-improvement, the sheer volume of robotics work, a massive acceleration in usage and revenue, and a cybersecurity picture that has turned serious.

Recursive self-improvement, AI building AI, is the title question, and his verdict is that the raw ingredients are arriving but the landmark result is not here yet. At Anthropic and OpenAI, engineers accept three to eight times more model-generated code than the yearly average before the current model class. More striking is an Anthropic experiment in which a researcher follows a project for a while, the path then forks, and a model continues one branch while the human continues the other. Human evaluators judging the endpoints preferred the AI-driven fork about sixty percent of the time with the strongest models, and far less with weaker ones. Models are also good at autoresearch setups, where a clear objective and a fixed budget let them hill-climb efficiently through a search space. What they have not yet done, in his account, is invent a method nobody knew, or show taste and token efficiency in doing it. The evidence he wants next is out-of-domain generalization: a model proposing a genuinely new explanation, in cancer research for example, that a human scientist concedes no amount of extra months would have produced. He expects such a result to arrive in the life or physical sciences before mathematics, because those fields tolerate answers whose reasoning cannot be fully shown, the way drug discovery already does, while mathematicians want the proof suffered through.

Biology supplies the conversation's hardest test: a molecule materially shaped by AI that survives clinical trials. Three or four years ago the field had exponential growth in papers and no drugs in trials. Now an Enveda phase two asset, a drug discovered from plants using mass spectrometry and given to eczema patients, points the other way, and AI-designed protein drugs are moving through cancer pipelines. Benaich expects fairly definitive evidence within one to three years, with a lingering argument about whether the molecules could have been found anyway, only slower. The molecules in clinics today, he notes, are products of much weaker models, and he wishes investors would value biotech pipelines the way software investors value roadmaps. Language modeling ideas spill directly into this work. His portfolio company Profluent began with protein language models and now designs genome editors tailored to a patient's specific mutations, and structure prediction has moved from contact maps to diffusion models that capture a protein's many conformations.

Serious biology, he argues, requires owning a wet lab. Anthropic's move into one is a sign of seriousness, not a stunt: outsourced experiments produce messy, hard-to-trust data and hand a broker the feedback a lab most needs to keep. Owning the lab also lets a company design for automation, with robots handling liquids and few humans adding variability, and even for instruments whose output no human needs to read, since models can extract signals from unlabeled images that the eye cannot. Culture still decides much of the field. Decades of small-molecule pills reflect chemist-led management at big pharma, natural-source discovery fell out of fashion for cultural rather than scientific reasons, and most companies still let human committees choose which drug advances. A truly AI-committed firm, he suggests, would at some point let the machine decide.

Robotics gets a careful yes. Many viral demos are overcooked, he warns: training data nobody audited for the exact trick shown, evaluations staged, robots that fail or crawl on transfer. Underneath, the substance is real. Sereact runs hundreds of AI-controlled arms in logistics and fulfillment, and, more importantly, a single model now serves every customer, with fleet learning improving pick rates everywhere at once; the era of custom fine-tuning per facility is ending. Wayve's self-driving bet, end-to-end learning with cameras and radar but no lidar and no prebuilt maps, has generalized from a handful of training cities to hundreds of new ones, while Waymo's presence in London is, he guesses, about mapping and learning to drive on the other side of the road.

The business data is where the report turns stark. In data gathered with Standard Metrics, AI-native private companies grow about three times faster than AI-enabled ones in the one to twenty million dollar revenue band, and at twenty million and above the top quartile grows at a hundred and seventy-two percent a year, against fifty-three percent for AI-enabled firms. Ramp card data across seventy thousand customers shows the median firm spending twelve dollars fifty per employee per month on AI, while the top one percent spend seven thousand dollars, a gap of about five hundred and eighty times. Codex usage tells the same story: nearly all OpenAI employees use it daily, against seventeen percent of organizations and under one percent of individual accounts, and most people delegate thirty-minute tasks rather than eight-hour projects. Benaich calls consultancy adoption surveys useless and proposes two fixes: surveys that measure actual usage, and a disclosure norm under which articles about AI state how much AI the author actually consumes. The host adds a matching idea, a jobs board where employers are ranked by the token budget they give staff. He wonders whether persistent agents that simply hold a job, rather than waiting to be prompted, might be next year's adoption story.

Grading last year's predictions, he claims three hits: a major lab leaned back into open source in line with the US national AI strategy, a Chinese frontier lab retook a top leaderboard spot, and data center opposition went mainstream, with Americans telling pollsters they would rather live next to a nuclear plant. Two fell short or landed partially: no open-ended agent has yet made an undisputed end-to-end scientific discovery, and sovereign AI alliances have only begun to form. Next year's list leans cyber: an AI-led attack stealing model weights from a closed lab, frontier labs forced to sell defense products because their guardrailed models could not help Hugging Face diagnose an attack, and proof that an agent can improve for months at one customer through continual learning. He closes by pointing listeners to stateof.ai and suggesting that anyone jaded about progress reread the 2018 edition.

MTS · Richard Ngo on why AI cannot reliably run a company for six months

AI Cannot Reliably Run a Company for Six Months | Richard Ngo

On MTS, the episode "AI Cannot Reliably Run a Company for Six Months | Richard Ngo" is a conversation with Richard Ngo, an independent AI researcher who previously worked on AGI safety at Google DeepMind and on governance at OpenAI. His headline claim gives the episode its title. Running a company over a long period is, in his account, close to the hardest kind of task for today's models, because a company presents an enormous attack surface: one mistake can be exploited by hackers or rivals, or can land the company in legal trouble severe enough to shut it down. Work like that prizes a level of robustness the models do not currently have, and he doubts they will reliably run a company for six months, profitably and out of legal trouble, any time soon.

The conversation ranges well beyond that claim, starting with timelines. Ngo agrees with the host that much of the field is jumping further than the evidence warrants: people take the fact that labs are automating parts of research and development and treat a rapid, self-sustaining intelligence explosion as close to certain. He is careful, though, not to land on the opposite conclusion. He does not expect the next decade or two to look normal. His default picture is no vertical takeoff and no single moment where recursive self-improvement sends the lines sharply upward, but a world that still becomes much weirder than almost anyone expects, with AI pervasive and AI companies increasingly powerful throughout.

That picture depends on his central terminological argument. AGI, he says, used to serve as a catchall that bundled together human-level AI and superintelligence, partly because people assumed recursive self-improvement would leave little gap between them. The last five years have shown the two are very different, and now the word superintelligence has inherited the catchall role, bundling concepts that will have to be unbundled over the next few years. What replaces the bundle, he predicts, will look spiky. Capabilities will stay jagged for a long time: models are already close to superhuman at mathematics, and he expects them to be superhuman at writing novels reasonably soon, because neither task needs much feedback from the world. Operating a company for months on end is the contrasting case, and it is where the jaggedness bites.

He also separates the capabilities models have from the capabilities anyone can actually get out of them. Some of what current models can do is locked behind an elicitation problem: the field took a year or two to find even simple prompting moves, like asking a model to think step by step. Ngo goes further and suggests alignment and elicitation may become hard to distinguish, because a model may only display certain capabilities when it trusts whoever is asking. He would not be surprised, he says, if Claude held something like a judgment of Anthropic itself, and if how trustworthy or disreputable Anthropic appeared changed which capabilities the model was willing to show.

Much of the episode draws on the sequence of posts Ngo is publishing on LessWrong, a retrospective on the failures of the AI alignment movement over the last decade. His recurring complaint is resolution: the field's core concepts were sketched quickly, yet they now steer decisions at very large companies and will soon steer governments, and they are not up to that standard. Anthropic's alignment faking paper is his example: the behavior Claude showed in it can be read as very aligned or very misaligned depending on definitions, so the label settles nothing. The research he finds robustly valuable is conceptual: specifying what good behavior would actually look like. He notes that Claude has a constitution that has served it well, but he does not know of anyone who has seriously argued for the ideas in it, and that kind of philosophical work is exactly what the field's incentives reward least. Work that depends on access to frontier models, by contrast, will not generalize far, and anything that would generalize from today's frontier would, he argues, generalize from open-weight models too, so being inside a lab is not the advantage it is assumed to be.

On lab politics, Ngo is blunt. Effective altruism was historically conciliatory toward the labs for no good reason, mostly out of conflict aversion and fear of challenging prestigious companies, so the newer willingness to resign, speak out, and pressure labs, of the kind Robert Wiblin of the 80,000 Hours podcast has lately argued for, is a welcome change. What is still missing, he says, is honest analysis of power dynamics. Discussion keeps collapsing into good guys and bad guys, with OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepMind each casting themselves in the hero role against rivals or against China, and the racing logic that follows treats this one contest as exempt from the usual rules because AGI is so powerful that everything is up for grabs.

Asked what people who want AI to go well should actually do, Ngo puts trust before plans. Progress will be bottlenecked, he argues, by whether small groups of people can communicate honestly, trust each other, and coordinate, because that is how influence has compounded so far: the early OpenAI group out-computed DeepMind, and Anthropic then out-computed OpenAI, on the strength of unusually serious internal conversation. Anthropic matches part of that description, but he thinks groups like it cohere around things that function as dogmas, such as "we are the good guys" and "other people would do it anyway." Those are claims the lab cannot actually evaluate, and they leave it unable to reckon with its own influence on the world, including, he says, the fact that many researchers in China are becoming genuinely scared of Anthropic in particular.

The interview closes on general politics, because Ngo was one of the very few people in the rationalist world to publicly back Donald Trump in 2024. His explanation is about class: rationalists are, in his telling, cultural elites, close to the professional managerial class that graduates of top universities form, and it is frightening to defy your own class's interests and risk the social repercussions. He reads the elite reaction to Brexit and to Trump's first election as a class mobilizing against the working class more than as a grounded response to specific policies. The AI community's own urgency made things worse, he argues, because it taught people not to rock the boat on ordinary politics for fear of losing influence, a strategy that left them thinking less clearly about the political situation their AI plans depend on.

Open questions

  1. If superintelligence has to be unbundled the way AGI was, which of the capabilities bundled inside it turn out to be near, and which, like running a company reliably for six months, turn out to be far away?
  2. Ngo's prescription is honest, high-trust groups that can coordinate before they can plan. Can such a group form and stay honest at the scale the labs now operate at, or does growth itself force the dogmas he criticizes?
  3. If a model's willingness to display capabilities depends on how trustworthy it judges its operator to be, how would anyone outside the lab ever verify what a frontier model can actually do?

Y Combinator · Francois Chaubard on going from researcher to founder

How To Go From Researcher to Founder | Startup School

On Y Combinator, the Startup School episode "How To Go From Researcher to Founder | Startup School" is a talk by Francois Chaubard, a visiting partner at YC and a current PhD student who was previously a researcher in Fei-Fei Li's lab and the founder and CEO of Focal. His premise is that some of the most successful companies in the world, including Google, Anthropic, Databricks, and Together AI, were started by founders with PhDs, but that they are the exceptions. Academic-led startups more often fail, because many of the skills a researcher is rewarded for are antithetical, or at least orthogonal, to succeeding as a founder. To become a formidable founder, he argues, an academic has to unlearn instincts beaten in over years in the lab, and in effect kill part of their academic identity. He then breaks the transition down into ten perspective shifts.

The first shift is from vanity metrics to what people want. In academia, he says, you optimize for grades, citation counts, and the number of talks you give, and he quotes Paul Graham's line that the most damaging thing learned in school was learning to get good grades. The founder mindset asks instead what people actually want, and what they will actually pay money for as opposed to what they say they would pay for. That leads to the second shift, interesting versus important. Academics are rewarded for understanding the mechanisms behind something, and that gets you published, but it often has no ramification on anyone's daily life. Founders must work backwards from what is important to their users, and be willing to say: it just works, we do not know exactly why, ship it. As he puts it through Marc Andreessen, the only thing that matters is getting to product market fit.

The third shift concerns novelty. In academia novelty is everything, and a paper that is not completely original is rejected. In the startup world, he argues, copying is often a really good strategy, because pioneers frequently get arrows in their backs. He borrows Joe Lonsdale's distinction between MIT-style startups, which revolve around a brilliant patented black box that nobody actually wants, and Stanford-style startups, which start with a single boring customer problem and iterate on it forever. Instacart did not invent delivering groceries from an online order; countless startups tried before it, and it was simply the most successful at executing, still iterating on that core problem fifteen years later. Novelty, he concludes, is not required, and often not even preferred.

The fourth shift is that there is no finish line. Academia always has a cathartic submission date or final, after which you can rest, while a startup offers no such moment, and researchers make it worse by working on five ideas at once, smearing the peanut butter too thin. Founder mode means waking up every day and cranking on the same problem, without getting sidetracked, for decades. The fifth shift is to get on a plane and talk to people, because finding product market fit is a brute force exercise of iterating quickly and talking to customers constantly, and the people to talk to are those who have the problem, not investors. He illustrates it with his own failure at Focal, which spent three months collecting training data and training models to track people for retailers, only to deliver the finding that shoppers spend a lot of time at the checkout counter and the deli counter at lunchtime. Asked whether they would pay for it, the retailers said absolutely not. He could have labeled a few videos a day by hand and reached the same conclusion, and his advice to AI companies is to start with a human in the loop and validate that customers want the output before spending heavily on compute.

The sixth shift is that hyperbole is okay in a sales pitch, even though it is not okay in a paper. He points to the famous car insurance slogan promising that fifteen minutes could save you fifteen percent or more, a beautifully inexact statement that no ablation study ever supported, and warns that obsessing over rigor in marketing will destroy you. You should never lie, but punchier language is necessary. The seventh is the 70% rule: Jeff Bezos's guideline that most decisions should be made with around 70% of the information you wish you had. Publishing at 70% confidence would never get you into a top conference, but in startups waiting for complete data means a competitor has already launched. In the database wars, Michael Stonebraker had a technically superior product in Quel, but Larry Ellison pushed SQL into the market faster and won. Speed, he says, beats perfect information.

The eighth shift is that you cannot delegate the hard stuff. A startup CEO is responsible for everything: team management, cash runway, hiring, firing, marketing, sales, and product development. Academics who imagine hiring an MBA to do the business side while they keep doing research on the venture capitalists' dime have it backwards, because abstracting yourself away from sales and marketing means you never develop empathy for your customers. The ninth shift is to become a capitalist. Starting a company is the most capitalistic thing you can do, and many academics are deeply uncomfortable with charging people money, winning talent, earning 80% margins, raising capital, and beating the competition, all of which he says you must get comfortable with. The tenth is that nobody cares how smart you are. Talking at length about your own field does not work in sales, product development, or pitching investors. What works is an innate curiosity about people, following Dale Carnegie's observation that you can make more friends in two months by becoming interested in other people than in two years by trying to get other people interested in you.

His summary advice to academics turned founders is the line he says he gives most often: get out of your office, get on a flight, and go meet your users where they live and work, and validate whether people will actually pay you, because your competition is doing exactly that. To quiet the academic brain and grow a founder brain he recommends four books: Spin Selling, High Output Management, The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People, and How to Win Friends and Influence People. He closes with Gandhi: if you want to change the world, start with yourself. You already have the intelligence, he tells his audience. The only question left is, do you have the will?

Open questions

  1. Chaubard argues that copying a proven idea beats inventing a novel one, yet many research founders' only edge is a technical breakthrough. Where is the line between a Stanford-style startup iterating on a boring problem and one whose black box nobody wants, and how would a founder know which side of it they are on before the market tells them?
  2. His Focal lesson is to start with a human in the loop and validate demand before training models. As training and inference get cheaper, does that advice get stronger or weaker, and what signal tells a founder the manual version has taught them enough to automate?
  3. He urges academics to decide at 70% of the information and to sell with hyperbole, while insisting they must never lie. How does a founder keep that speed and punch from sliding into overclaiming that later destroys trust with customers and investors?

Sam Witteveen · What is the future of IDEs

What is the Future of IDEs

On Sam Witteveen, the video "What is the Future of IDEs" takes on a question he says he keeps hearing, and keeps asking himself: with coding agents like Claude Code and Codex doing so much of the writing, what is an integrated development environment actually for now? His answer is that the IDE is no longer where code gets written. It is where code gets read, navigated, checked, and approved, and that shift makes it more important, not less, for anyone shipping production software.

The video grew out of a conversation with JetBrains, who reached out for his take and published a blog post arguing that we still need an IDE. Witteveen is open about the arrangement: the video is sponsored by JetBrains, though he stresses it is not a push for any particular product, and he has used JetBrains tools on and off since doing Objective-C development in 2012. He told them IDEs still matter but need to change drastically, and he says they seem to agree. Air is their attempt at an IDE built for a world where agents do much of the writing.

His core observation comes from his own work. Hand an agent a task and it may touch ten or fifteen files at once, and from a terminal alone you cannot really see what it did. That invisibility is fine for a quick idea sketched out on a phone, but if you call yourself a developer, he argues, you still need to know what the code is doing and whether it is right. His own model testing suite, now an extensive piece of software supporting many backends, has been touched by several different agents, each with its own ideas about how it should work, which made the modular layout of the codebase something he has to be able to inspect directly. One practice he picked up from someone at Anthropic is having agents generate HTML documentation and diagrams of how everything is laid out, with real file names he can then open and check in the IDE.

The second shift is that IDEs are merging into places where agents run, not just places where code is viewed. GitHub Copilot was an early version of this, with an agent panel beside the code panels. Witteveen has since experimented with tools like Cmux for running multiple agents, and more recently Herder and T3 Code, and he notes that JetBrains Air also lets you run and orchestrate several agents from inside the IDE rather than being limited to one. What he wants from any of them is the ability to move quickly between the high-level structure of a project and the low-level detail.

Historically, he points out, the IDE was about making typing faster: autocomplete, refactoring, personal snippet libraries, and then early AI autocomplete that could finish a function for you. Agents have ended that era. Typing code is not only no longer the main activity, he says, it is often the bad option. If IDEs were only about typing, they would be in trouble. What a good one actually does is help you read code, navigate it, understand it, and check it.

That is where the bottleneck has moved. An agent can produce a change in a few minutes, but reviewing it can take far longer, especially for whoever is responsible for committing it to production. He cites the pattern at large organizations, including Google, and an earlier interview he did with Logan Kilpatrick: anyone can now write code and submit a pull request, but an engineer on the project has to sign off and own the result. JetBrains, after talking to a range of engineering teams, found the same pattern, and one team measured its pull request time going up, not down, as agents were rolled out. Witteveen would be more worried by the opposite: a team whose review time fell to zero would signal a trust problem solved by not looking. Having one agent review another catches things, he concedes, but it does not remove the need for a person who understands what the system is supposed to do, and who can judge whether a result was reached in a way that stays legal and does not run up huge compute or database bills.

His concrete uses for an IDE today come in four parts. First, diffs: before anything an agent writes gets merged, he wants to see the file structure and what actually changed, which is close to impossible with a wall of text in a terminal. Second, navigation: when an agent changes a function, he wants to jump to its call sites, find usages, and see what implements an interface, because that is how you judge whether a change is safe, for instance whether a piece of code making many database calls does so efficiently. Third, verification: running tests and inspections inside the IDE, checking that the tests themselves test something real rather than trivially passing, and stepping through with a debugger when something looks off. Fourth, documentation, which he calls underrated: agents should write documentation as they write code, developers should review whether it says what the code should be doing and whether the agent quietly changed things nobody asked for, and that reviewed documentation then feeds back to the agents as better context.

That last point cuts both ways, because the IDE's intelligence is useful to the agent as well as the human. An agent that can only search through files with text matching is, in his phrase, working blind, spending its whole context window figuring out what is going on. An agent that can use the IDE's semantic understanding of the codebase gets better context and produces more accurate work, and it saves money in tokens. He adds a warning about the current moment: developers are being spoiled by flat-rate plans in the hundred to five hundred dollar range with very high token allowances, and if the economics shift toward paying per API call, the cost of an agent working blind will matter a great deal more.

His closing summary is blunt. The IDE is where you read the code, where you check it, and where you decide whether it ships. The writing part has gotten cheap; the verification has not. That, he says, is the direction JetBrains is heading with Air: bring the agents you already use into the IDE, so their work can be reviewed and checked in the place built for exactly that.

Open questions

  1. If review is now the bottleneck, will IDEs evolve better tools for reviewing agent output specifically, or will developers keep adapting tools designed for reading hand-written code?
  2. Witteveen argues a human must sign off on production code today; at what point, if any, does agent self-review become trustworthy enough to change that, and who carries responsibility when it fails?
  3. If token pricing moves away from generous flat-rate plans toward paying per call, how much will IDE-provided context and semantic search actually save, and will that decide which agent tools survive?
