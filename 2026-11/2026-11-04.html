David Senra · Scott Kupor on a16z, venture capital, and fixing the government

a16z's First Employee on the History of a16z, Venture Capital & Fixing the Government | Scott Kupor

On David Senra, the episode "a16z's First Employee on the History of a16z, Venture Capital & Fixing the Government | Scott Kupor" is a conversation with Scott Kupor, who joined Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz in 2009 as the first employee of Andreessen Horowitz and spent sixteen and a half years there as a managing partner. He now directs the U.S. Office of Personnel Management, the agency that serves as the human resources and talent office for the federal government's roughly two million civilian employees. The conversation moves in two arcs: how a venture capital firm was built from three people into an institution of about seven hundred, and what Kupor is trying to change about how the federal government hires, rates, promotes, and retires its own people.

The founding nearly did not include him. Kupor had worked with Andreessen and Horowitz at Loudcloud and Opsware, which Hewlett-Packard bought in 2007, and he was living in North Carolina when the two began planning the firm in the summer of 2008, as Bear Stearns vanished and Lehman Brothers collapsed. He hesitated for months, weighing a stable job at HP against a startup fund in a financial crisis, and only committed around Christmas. The standing joke, he says, is that a faster yes might have made the firm Andreessen Kupor Horowitz. His official status stayed first employee rather than co-founder, and there was no job description: the founders would invest, and he would figure out everything else a firm needs.

The early days were that literal. His first act was a capital call, the request that investors wire money for an investment, and he sent it from his personal Gmail account because he did not yet have a firm email address. An investor wrote back demanding to know who he was, then called Andreessen and Horowitz to report that someone was impersonating them and shaking him down. Before that came office furniture and bank accounts; after it, hiring finance staff so the mistake would not repeat, and then hiring the operators who would become the firm's services arm. The firm grew from three people to roughly one hundred in its first year.

The model came from Hollywood. Michael Ovitz, who had been on the Loudcloud board, built CAA by disaggregating the agent's job: instead of one agent handling everything for an actor, a client who walked into CAA met a table of specialists, each expert in one domain. Andreessen and Horowitz applied the same idea to venture capital, which then ran on the one-to-one model of a connector like John Doerr, who personally knew every salesperson, chief financial officer, and political contact a founder might need. A firm, they argued, could institutionalize that: hire investors for their domain expertise and surround them with full-time professionals running talent networks, business development, government relations, and the rest. When founders pitched, the firm answered with a reverse pitch: not just a board member, but the whole phalanx.

Marketing was the other break with tradition. Venture firms did not market, Kupor says, because for decades capital was scarce and entrepreneurs came to them; the balance of power made marketing unnecessary. A new firm with no track record could not compete on the incumbents' terms, so it competed on a new category instead, capital plus services, and told that story loudly and before it was fully built. Rivals on Sand Hill Road complained that the newcomers talked a big game, which sounded to Kupor like a description of any entrepreneur creating a category: you have to narrate it, and then you have to deliver.

Delivery came fast. Within months of the first fund closing, the firm joined Silver Lake to buy Skype out of eBay, a buyout from a firm that had promised its investors early stage venture investing, and one shadowed by a dispute over the underlying intellectual property that had scared off other buyers. About eighteen months later Microsoft bought Skype for four times the purchase price, and the firm returned roughly half of its first fund to investors. Martin Casado's networking company Nicira also exited early in the first fund, and Casado later joined the firm as a partner. The wins landed while established firms were hunkered down over their portfolios in the crisis, and the second fund they enabled supplied the fee stream that paid for the services build-out. Andreessen and Horowitz took no salary at the start so the money could go to hiring.

Kupor's own role stayed operational for years, by his account an honest fit: early stage investing, he says, demands a granular feel for technology he does not have, and in a room of ten database pitches that depth is the whole job. About ten years in, the firm reversed its structure, decentralizing from one centralized organization, where nearly everything reported to him, into specialist funds, crypto under Chris Dixon, American Dynamism under David Ulevitch, artificial intelligence infrastructure under Casado, with the services teams feathered into each vertical and only legal, finance, compliance, and investor relations kept central. The reorganization shrank his domain, which stung, and also freed him to invest. From 2019 he helped build the firm's later stage venture fund with David George, leaning toward health care, where judgment is about operations and the road from one to infinity rather than zero to one. He left with about fifteen investments and ten board seats.

Policy, though, predates venture capital in his life. He did health care policy research in high school, worked at Stanford for Alan Garber, now the president of Harvard, on prostate cancer screening economics, and later for Jay Bhattacharya, who now runs the National Institutes of Health. With his children grown and Andreessen and Horowitz's blessing, he took the OPM job as a return to that first interest. His theory of the work is deliberately simple: an organization is a collection of people lined up around a set of objectives, and performance follows from having the right people and incentives that reward the behavior the objectives require. Government, he argues, fails mainly on the second term.

The incentive structure he describes is a compliance culture organized around avoiding blame. A senior report told him early on that their job was to keep him out of the news: no congressional hearings, no critical audit reports. The safe strategy that follows is to do nothing new, which is why obvious improvements sit untouched for decades. Kupor's counter is what he calls permissioned risk, risk deliberately sized to the stakes. Where failure can kill people, tolerance should be near zero; where the work is processing retirement applications and tax refunds, the upside of automation dwarfs the cost of an occasional error. His own license to push this, he told the president in his interview, is that he wants no other job when this one ends, so a bad hearing costs him nothing he is protecting.

Performance management is where the incentives show up in numbers. Federal employees are rated on a five point scale, and historically 99.7 percent have been rated three or better, meaning only 0.3 percent fall below expectations, a result he calls mathematically impossible in a workforce of two million. At the top, roughly ninety percent of the roughly seven thousand senior executives receive one of the two highest ratings, so bonuses are spread across nearly everyone and motivate no one, while weak performers face no consequences beside their high performing colleagues. His new rules cap the top rating at no more than forty percent of a workforce and reserve at least sixty percent of the compensation pool for that group. At the same time, his team is eliminating time in grade, the tenure requirement that blocks a promotion for at least a year regardless of performance, so advancement can follow merit instead.

The reason this matters now is demographic. Seven percent of the federal workforce is under thirty, against twenty three percent of the overall U.S. workforce, and forty four percent is over fifty, against about a third nationally; if nothing changes, half the workforce retires within five to ten years with no bench behind it. Kupor's recruiting pitch discards the old promise of lifetime employment, which he calls the worst pitch imaginable for anyone under thirty, and offers a two or three year tour instead: work on genuinely hard problems at NASA, the Energy Department, or elsewhere, build rare experience, and then stay or leave for the private sector with the government's help. The flagship is Tech Force, which aims to hire one thousand software engineers, artificial intelligence experts, and data scientists for two year terms at the GS-14 level, a senior individual contributor grade. Entrants take actual skills assessments, third party coding tests rather than the self-rated questionnaires federal hiring has relied on, and some forty private companies partner on training during the term and a job fair at the end. The model he cites is Israel's military cyber units, whose alumni every firm wants to hire.

His test case for skills based hiring is a nineteen year old software engineer who dropped out of college to join the DOGE effort and works now on the National Design Studio's redesign of federal websites. Under standard rules, lacking a degree and five years of experience, the government would never have hired him, or would have hired him only at the entry level GS-5 grade and a salary Silicon Valley would multiply by ten. Kupor's view is that such a person should enter at GS-14 or GS-15, the top grades, because demonstrated merit says he can perform there, and that job descriptions across government are being rewritten on that principle.

The headcount backdrop is the DOGE reduction, which took the civilian workforce from about 2.3 million when the administration began to about 2 million now, its smallest share of the total workforce since 1960. Kupor, who was nominated in December and planned with the DOGE team before his confirmation kept him sidelined, frames his own job as institutionalizing the change: something drastic was needed to turn a tanker of that size, and his task is the durable part, making agencies state what they are actually working on and accept that some of it should stop, since government only ever adds.

His Exhibit A is retirement. Until last year, a retiring federal employee's paperwork traveled by mail through their agency and a separate payroll office to an underground limestone mine in Boyers, Pennsylvania, where some eight hundred OPM staff read paper files, computed annuities by hand, and took anywhere from three to twelve months to produce a first check, mailing forms back for a missing signature at a month's delay each time. The mine still holds about four hundred million pages of records dating to the 1950s, fetched by runners whenever a retiree's circumstances change. The process is now fully electronic: paper intake ended on June 30, the first iteration has cut processing time by more than half, and the commitment is a complete retirement check within fifteen days of a clean electronic application. Room sized scanners are digitizing the entire archive, which should eventually let the storage mine close. In parallel, his office is consolidating the government's one hundred twenty separate human resources computer systems into one, which he says cuts the annual cost from about two billion dollars to about forty million, and gives the government, for the first time, a single continuous record of an employee's service.

The parts connect, in his telling, into one loop: tell a compelling story and talented people will come for a few years rather than a lifetime; give them a culture where ratings mean something, promotion follows merit, and a sized risk is permitted; and those people will modernize the systems everyone else experiences. The recruiting starts at earlycareers.gov and a tour of about forty college campuses, and his closing offer to a young listener is direct: send him a message, and they will find a way to make something work.

Open questions

  1. The a16z services machine was funded by management fees that early wins like Skype made possible, and it scaled because the firm could pay market salaries for operators. OPM has no equivalent engine: it must fund assessments, digitization, and a technologist corps out of appropriations. What sustains the build-out when the initial push meets a budget cycle or a change of administration?
  2. Capping top ratings at forty percent assumes managers will differentiate honestly once the rules change. The old system was also a set of rules, and managers gamed it into meaninglessness. What in the new design prevents ratings from quietly re-inflating, especially once Kupor, who says he wants nothing afterward, has left?
  3. Tech Force asks elite engineers to accept below market pay for two years in exchange for training, mission, and a partner run job fair at the end. If the program works as designed, its partner companies will hire away much of each cohort. Does the government then gain a durable alumni network and a recruiting brand, or does it mostly subsidize talent development for the firms it partners with?
