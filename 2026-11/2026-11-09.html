The Rest Is Science · Hannah Fry and Michael Stevens on parasites that control minds

Why Does Evolution Keep Inventing Mind Control?

On The Rest Is Science, “Why Does Evolution Keep Inventing Mind Control?” is an episode about parasites, hosted by Professor Hannah Fry and Michael Stevens. Fry opens with her new housemates: six thousand parasitic wasps, Trichogramma evanescens, which she has willingly released at home so they will lay their eggs inside the eggs of clothes moths. The wasps are half a millimeter long, golden yellow with bright red eyes, and effective, but in Fry's judgment unimaginative. They want the body of the moth egg, they eat it, and they leave. Other parasites do far more. They get inside another creature and stay there, and some of them take over its behavior.

Her second example arrived by accident. Her sister trapped a cricket in a glass in a bathroom in France, squished it, and out of the cricket emerged a horrific long worm, up to thirty centimeters of it, that had been living inside. Hairworms breed in water, pass through small water insects, and grow inside crickets that eat those insects. As the worm matures it changes the cricket's behavior, so the cricket makes a beeline for a pond or river and dives in, at which point the worm emerges and restarts its cycle. The cricket sometimes survives. The worm is not much concerned either way.

Parasites are nearly as old as complex life. The oldest confirmed evidence, from fossils described by researchers at Northwest University in China in 2020, is 512 million years old: a clam-like animal, Neobolus wulongqingensis, whose feeding current was exploited by tube-shaped organisms clamped to its shell, all facing the incoming food. Measurements settle the relationship: hosts carrying many tubes grew smaller and weaker, so the tubes were stealing, not sharing. Stevens offers his favorite definition of a parasite: a predator that kills its prey in units of less than one. The two of them also run through a scale of relationships, from commensalism, where one side benefits and the other is unaffected, like the mites living harmlessly in our eyebrows, through mutualism, where both benefit, like gut bacteria, to parasitism, where one benefits and the other is harmed.

Getting inside a body is so hard that it is remarkable it happens at all. A 2016 survey by Sara Weinstein and Armand Kuris found that parasitism has independently evolved at least 223 times across the animal kingdom. Each origin had to clear the same hurdles: find a host, gain access, survive its defenses, steal enough to reproduce without killing the home you live in, and get your offspring into the next host. Fail at any one and the experiment ends. The prize explains the effort. A host finds your food, digests it, builds your complicated molecules, and keeps you at the right temperature.

The entry routes are ingenious. Malaria lets the mosquito do the hard part, piercing the skin and depositing the parasite in tissue, from where it works its way into a blood vessel and rides to the liver. The botfly goes further, gluing its eggs onto a mosquito so the larvae arrive with the bite and burrow in. Many parasites simply use the door the host already has, the mouth, which is how the hairworm reaches the cricket, one insect inside another. Hookworms dispense with subtlety entirely. They penetrate the skin of a bare foot, ride the bloodstream to the heart, cross to the lungs, climb the windpipe, get swallowed, and arrive at the intestine. Stevens calls it changing trains on the Circle line.

Once inside, the deepest trick is behavioral. The hosts argue that every body comes out of the box with behavioral switches already fitted: flu makes us hot, tired and horizontal because our own bodies flip those switches, and a single molecule such as serotonin can change how a person feels and acts. A parasite only has to learn which molecules move which behavior. Rabies does it crudely, reaching the salivary glands and the brain so the infected animal becomes excitable and aggressive and bites, delivering the virus onward. The clearest mechanism they discuss belongs to a baculovirus that infects spongy moth caterpillars, which Germans call Wipfelkrankheit, treetop disease. Infected caterpillars climb to the treetops, die, and dissolve, raining virus onto the leaves below.

That climb has a molecular handle. Molting in the caterpillar is driven by a hormone, 20-hydroxyecdysone, and the virus carries a gene, EGT, whose product adds a sugar to the hormone and switches it off. In a 2011 experiment, Kelli Hoover and colleagues deleted EGT from the virus: infected caterpillars still died, but they did not climb. Put the gene back, and the climbing returned. Tube experiments then showed the trick is simpler than it looks. Infected caterpillars climb toward light, up or down depending on where the light is, so the virus is not steering them to a treetop, just turning up their drive toward brightness. The hairworm, they note, appears to do the same thing to the cricket's sensitivity to light, which is how a worm that cannot steer ends up at water.

Fry's favorite parasite closes the show: Toxoplasma gondii, a single-celled organism happiest in a cat's gut. It leaves in cat feces, gets eaten by a rat, and then needs the rat to be eaten by a cat. Infected rats lose their aversion to cat urine and make a beeline for it, and there is evidence, which Fry flags as somewhat controversial, that the parasite works through the same brain circuitry as sexual attraction, so the rat is in effect drawn to its predator. The human findings she cites come with a raised eyebrow: studies linking infection to men enjoying the smell of cat urine, and to roughly double the involvement in road traffic accidents, may be correlation rather than cause, though the mechanism is not impossible.

Their answer to the title's question runs through Richard Dawkins's idea of the extended phenotype: if a parasite's gene reliably changes another animal's behavior in ways that help the parasite reproduce, that behavior is part of the parasite's phenotype, and the boundary of the organism is not where we drew it. Mind control keeps being reinvented, they conclude, because once evolution puts one organism inside another, everything the host knows and does, its behavior included, becomes a resource the tenant can use. The conversation ends where that thought leads, with the two of them wondering how much of anyone's behavior is theirs at all, and whether the self is a fiction assembled from parts that mostly get along.

Open questions

  1. If bodies come fitted with behavioral switches that parasites merely learn to flip, what actually distinguishes a parasite flipping a switch from the ordinary chemistry of mood, appetite and illness?
  2. The human Toxoplasma findings rest on studies the hosts themselves call shaky. What kind of study could separate a parasite changing human behavior from correlation and confounding?
  3. If a host's behavior can be part of a parasite's extended phenotype, where should biology draw the boundary of an organism, and what changes in medicine or ecology if it draws that line in a different place?
