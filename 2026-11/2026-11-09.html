The Rest Is Science · Hannah Fry and Michael Stevens on parasites that control minds

Why Does Evolution Keep Inventing Mind Control?

On The Rest Is Science, “Why Does Evolution Keep Inventing Mind Control?” is an episode about parasites, hosted by Professor Hannah Fry and Michael Stevens. Fry opens with her new housemates: six thousand parasitic wasps, Trichogramma evanescens, which she has willingly released at home so they will lay their eggs inside the eggs of clothes moths. The wasps are half a millimeter long, golden yellow with bright red eyes, and effective, but in Fry's judgment unimaginative. They want the body of the moth egg, they eat it, and they leave. Other parasites do far more. They get inside another creature and stay there, and some of them take over its behavior.

Her second example arrived by accident. Her sister trapped a cricket in a glass in a bathroom in France, squished it, and out of the cricket emerged a horrific long worm, up to thirty centimeters of it, that had been living inside. Hairworms breed in water, pass through small water insects, and grow inside crickets that eat those insects. As the worm matures it changes the cricket's behavior, so the cricket makes a beeline for a pond or river and dives in, at which point the worm emerges and restarts its cycle. The cricket sometimes survives. The worm is not much concerned either way.

Parasites are nearly as old as complex life. The oldest confirmed evidence, from fossils described by researchers at Northwest University in China in 2020, is 512 million years old: a clam-like animal, Neobolus wulongqingensis, whose feeding current was exploited by tube-shaped organisms clamped to its shell, all facing the incoming food. Measurements settle the relationship: hosts carrying many tubes grew smaller and weaker, so the tubes were stealing, not sharing. Stevens offers his favorite definition of a parasite: a predator that kills its prey in units of less than one. The two of them also run through a scale of relationships, from commensalism, where one side benefits and the other is unaffected, like the mites living harmlessly in our eyebrows, through mutualism, where both benefit, like gut bacteria, to parasitism, where one benefits and the other is harmed.

Getting inside a body is so hard that it is remarkable it happens at all. A 2016 survey by Sara Weinstein and Armand Kuris found that parasitism has independently evolved at least 223 times across the animal kingdom. Each origin had to clear the same hurdles: find a host, gain access, survive its defenses, steal enough to reproduce without killing the home you live in, and get your offspring into the next host. Fail at any one and the experiment ends. The prize explains the effort. A host finds your food, digests it, builds your complicated molecules, and keeps you at the right temperature.

The entry routes are ingenious. Malaria lets the mosquito do the hard part, piercing the skin and depositing the parasite in tissue, from where it works its way into a blood vessel and rides to the liver. The botfly goes further, gluing its eggs onto a mosquito so the larvae arrive with the bite and burrow in. Many parasites simply use the door the host already has, the mouth, which is how the hairworm reaches the cricket, one insect inside another. Hookworms dispense with subtlety entirely. They penetrate the skin of a bare foot, ride the bloodstream to the heart, cross to the lungs, climb the windpipe, get swallowed, and arrive at the intestine. Stevens calls it changing trains on the Circle line.

Once inside, the deepest trick is behavioral. The hosts argue that every body comes out of the box with behavioral switches already fitted: flu makes us hot, tired and horizontal because our own bodies flip those switches, and a single molecule such as serotonin can change how a person feels and acts. A parasite only has to learn which molecules move which behavior. Rabies does it crudely, reaching the salivary glands and the brain so the infected animal becomes excitable and aggressive and bites, delivering the virus onward. The clearest mechanism they discuss belongs to a baculovirus that infects spongy moth caterpillars, which Germans call Wipfelkrankheit, treetop disease. Infected caterpillars climb to the treetops, die, and dissolve, raining virus onto the leaves below.

That climb has a molecular handle. Molting in the caterpillar is driven by a hormone, 20-hydroxyecdysone, and the virus carries a gene, EGT, whose product adds a sugar to the hormone and switches it off. In a 2011 experiment, Kelli Hoover and colleagues deleted EGT from the virus: infected caterpillars still died, but they did not climb. Put the gene back, and the climbing returned. Tube experiments then showed the trick is simpler than it looks. Infected caterpillars climb toward light, up or down depending on where the light is, so the virus is not steering them to a treetop, just turning up their drive toward brightness. The hairworm, they note, appears to do the same thing to the cricket's sensitivity to light, which is how a worm that cannot steer ends up at water.

Fry's favorite parasite closes the show: Toxoplasma gondii, a single-celled organism happiest in a cat's gut. It leaves in cat feces, gets eaten by a rat, and then needs the rat to be eaten by a cat. Infected rats lose their aversion to cat urine and make a beeline for it, and there is evidence, which Fry flags as somewhat controversial, that the parasite works through the same brain circuitry as sexual attraction, so the rat is in effect drawn to its predator. The human findings she cites come with a raised eyebrow: studies linking infection to men enjoying the smell of cat urine, and to roughly double the involvement in road traffic accidents, may be correlation rather than cause, though the mechanism is not impossible.

Their answer to the title's question runs through Richard Dawkins's idea of the extended phenotype: if a parasite's gene reliably changes another animal's behavior in ways that help the parasite reproduce, that behavior is part of the parasite's phenotype, and the boundary of the organism is not where we drew it. Mind control keeps being reinvented, they conclude, because once evolution puts one organism inside another, everything the host knows and does, its behavior included, becomes a resource the tenant can use. The conversation ends where that thought leads, with the two of them wondering how much of anyone's behavior is theirs at all, and whether the self is a fiction assembled from parts that mostly get along.

Open questions

  1. If bodies come fitted with behavioral switches that parasites merely learn to flip, what actually distinguishes a parasite flipping a switch from the ordinary chemistry of mood, appetite and illness?
  2. The human Toxoplasma findings rest on studies the hosts themselves call shaky. What kind of study could separate a parasite changing human behavior from correlation and confounding?
  3. If a host's behavior can be part of a parasite's extended phenotype, where should biology draw the boundary of an organism, and what changes in medicine or ecology if it draws that line in a different place?

Kleiner Perkins · Rivian leaders on building hardware in the age of physical AI

The New Rules of Building Hardware in the Age of Physical AI | Insights from Rivian’s Leaders

On Kleiner Perkins, “The New Rules of Building Hardware in the Age of Physical AI | Insights from Rivian’s Leaders” is an episode of Builders, the firm’s lunch conversation series, now in its second season. Hosts Josh Coyne and Leigh Marie Braswell sit down with RJ Scaringe, Rivian’s founder and chief executive, and Vidya Rajagopalan, who leads electrical hardware and silicon at Rivian, to sort out what is real in physical AI and what building hardware at scale actually demands.

The starting premise is that hardware does not forgive. Software can ship and be patched later; a vehicle has to be right the first time. Rajagopalan describes the mindset this produces: long planning cycles, because a chip sent to a fab takes months to come back, and constant vigilance about variation, because physical parts differ from one another and processes drift in ways written code never does. She borrows Andy Grove’s line for it: only the paranoid survive.

AI helps a great deal, but inside firm limits. Rivian uses AI tools to generate Verilog and test benches for its chips, to run chip bring-up scenarios that would take people far longer to enumerate, and to hunt for compatible alternate parts when supply chains seize up. What AI cannot shorten is the physics of making things. Design and validation cycles can tighten; the time it takes to manufacture something does not care.

The supply chain is the standing pressure underneath everything. A drinking glass has one or two makers behind it; a car has thousands of companies, hundreds of them supplying Rivian directly, and each of those with suppliers of its own. Rivian learned this launching its first product into the pandemic in 2021 and 2022, and its people still fly to suppliers, often in Mexico, to chase root causes two or three tiers down. Scarcity now reaches commodities nobody used to plan around: the e-glass in circuit boards, pulled by the data center boom, along with memory and even MLCC capacitors.

Rivian’s structural answer is vertical integration for anything strategic, which in practice means anything defined by software or controlling the vehicle’s critical behavior: motors, autonomy, and most strikingly silicon. About four years ago the company decided to build its own autonomy chips, a self-described billion-dollar decision taken with no in-house silicon expertise. The logic was cost structure. The expensive part of delivering higher autonomy is in-vehicle inference, more than the sensors that draw the attention, and a chip program cannot be half tried. The company was either doing it or not.

Rajagopalan’s case for owning silicon is that Rivian knows its own application. Merchant chip designers work to benchmarks and guess at real workloads; an integrated company designs around the system it actually ships, down to buffer depths and memory bandwidth. The discipline that keeps the bet honest is a business case: Rivian knew its vehicle cost targets, knew roughly what a chip of a given size would cost to make, and required the program to pay for itself against those numbers. The first generation is the hardest, she notes, because there is no field data from a previous chip to design against.

For the software founders now rushing into hardware, Scaringe’s advice is to hire scar tissue. He meets many robotics founders who are brilliant but have never built hardware before, and who underestimate design for reliability and for supply chains; he says he often feels he is meeting a younger version of himself. A founder who has not been through it needs someone on the team who has.

That conviction produced a second company. In November 2025 Scaringe formed Mind Robotics to build robots with human-like dexterity and reasoning for manufacturing. It has raised a little over a billion dollars, and Doug Field has joined as president: his career runs from the Segway through Apple’s MacBook line and the Tesla Model 3 program to Ford. The plan is concrete and near: the first robots go into a Rivian plant in the middle of next year.

On the state of physical AI, Scaringe sees an inflection, as better motors and batteries meet foundation model techniques and cheap local inference. By the mid-2030s, he expects robotics in places like manufacturing plants and mines to simply be assumed. The industrial business case already works: Rivian would happily spend tens of thousands of dollars per robot per year for work only humans can do today, while the home case is far less clear. He also expects purpose-built form factors to beat humanoid imitation. The winning lawn robot is a robot that is the lawnmower, not a humanoid walking behind one.

The modeling shift underneath this mirrors self-driving. Hand-written, rules-based planners are giving way to end-to-end neural networks trained the way people learn, and Mind builds what it calls world action models. Yet both guests downplay architecture as the contest. The scarce input is data, and robotics has no internet to scrape. Mind is therefore building a data collection operation of its own: wearable cameras capturing the worker’s point of view, fixed cameras watching the work, tactile gloves, all time-synchronized, alongside data it buys. The same inference platform Rivian built for its cars powers the robots.

In a plant, the launch robots take the simplest variable work first: pick and place, moving parts from racks that are never in quite the same spot into fixtures that always are, work Scaringe estimates can be half of a plant’s labor. Mind expects its robots to cover about 20 percent of plant tasks at launch and to grow toward 80 percent as the models improve. That is why the hardware is designed with headroom. Rivian’s cars gain point-to-point driving through an over-the-air update on unchanged hardware; a robot’s body is locked at build time while its brain keeps learning.

Scaringe also expects the industry to consolidate. Too many companies are building slices of the robotics stack, he argues, and the best funded will vertically integrate, leaving component suppliers to serve the second tier of companies that could not raise the capital to build in-house. It is the pattern that killed the integrator car startups of 2016 to 2018, which assembled compromised systems from underfunded suppliers. On organization, both guests favor flat and flexible: Mind ran remarkably flat through its first hundred people, Rivian’s technical domains report to Scaringe and are run by senior leaders as if they were their own businesses, and the structure is expected to keep changing as the companies scale.

Open questions

  1. Rivian’s silicon bet rested on knowing its own application and volumes, and on a business case that had to pay for itself. Below what volume does that math stop working, and how should a smaller hardware company judge whether it has crossed the line where merchant silicon is the better answer?
  2. If the best-funded robotics companies vertically integrate and suppliers are left serving the second tier, does the robotics supply base get stronger through focus or weaker through starved margins?
  3. Mind’s plan assumes models keep improving on fixed hardware, growing task coverage from roughly 20 percent toward 80 percent. What happens to the economics of the launch robots if progress on physical tasks plateaus before the coverage does?

Semiconductor Engineering · Frank Ferro on memory for physical AI

Memory In Physical AI

On Semiconductor Engineering, "Memory In Physical AI" is a whiteboard conversation with Frank Ferro, group director for product management at Cadence. The channel's editor in chief sits down with Ferro at Cadence to work through a simple problem: humanoid robots, autonomous vehicles, and drones can only run as long as their batteries last, and a humanoid's usefulness today may last only a few hours per charge. No single component fixes that, so the discussion stays on the basics: efficient processing, denser and faster memory, and better data movement between processors and memory.

The starting contrast is with the data center. There, Ferro says, resources were plentiful and cost was not as critical. A physical AI system is constrained on every axis at once: power, processing, how much memory can be fitted, and cost, which he calls a very big factor.

His first drawing shows the cloud side of the story. A CPU is ringed with LPDDR memory and its controllers, delivered on SOCAMM compression modules that the industry has begun using in place of conventional DIMM memory. LPDDR wins on two fronts: it is faster than regular DDR, and it draws less power, because it was designed for mobile devices. Each slim compression module carries about four DDR devices, so the modules stack up alongside the processor, and designers squeeze them in by keeping the memory beachfront at the edges of the die as small as possible, even on what Ferro calls radical size dies.

Mobile memory has had to grow up for this role. Moving from LPDDR5 to LPDDR6, the standard adds the RAS features, the reliability and security functions data centers demand, which is making LPDDR a much better fit there. The pressure runs the other way as well: models are trained in the data center, and then they have to be pushed out to the edge of the network or into physical devices with a much smaller footprint and tighter constraints.

The second drawing shows what survives that move. The die is far smaller, because the cost of the silicon now matters, and where the cloud CPU might carry stacks of memory on every side, the physical AI chip might fit only three memory controllers per side, maybe a fourth. Yet the chip is still running large language models. Inference models are smaller than training models, but they keep getting large and unwieldy, and the system has to react in real time: a car has to know that what it sees is a road sign and not a person.

So the real question is how the memory is partitioned. Ferro breaks the contents into pieces. Model weights are a very large amount of static memory. The KV cache stores previous state so the system does not have to recalculate everything, but it grows with batch size and can be big. Activations, where the matrix multiplies happen, grow with the batch too. Optimizer states remember history and can run to twice the size of the static weights, or bigger. In the cloud, this work can be split between a CPU and a GPU. In physical AI, all of it blends into a single SoC, and where each piece sits in the memory hierarchy becomes extremely critical.

The working method is prioritization. Some data has to be very fast, other data can be fetched when needed, and Ferro sorts memory into hot, warm, and cold tiers, a scheme he likens to the basis of prefetch. How those tiers are partitioned inside a physical system, he says, will look very different from the cloud, and it depends on the problem: a car, a robot, and a factory system will each partition differently.

The KV cache earns its keep by cutting both computation and memory traffic. If the system remembers its last token and its previous state, it only has to fetch the new information and reuse the rest, which saves processing and, because less data is fetched, power as well.

Ferro points to two further levers, which he flags as topics for a follow-up conversation. One is processing less data in the first place, pruning it down. The other is doing the processing next to memory or in memory itself: running activations and matrix multiplies inside the DRAM avoids moving data in and out, which he says saves an enormous amount of power and processing. That approach is being explored for LPDDR and even HBM, and he frames it as another way through the memory wall, since the speed of an AI model's response is governed less by processor power than by memory bandwidth.

The physical layer may be the hardest part. LPDDR5X runs at about 10 gigabits per second, LPDDR6 will reach about 14, and hyperscaler customers want to go faster still. Getting the signals from 20 or 30 memory PHYs out of the cheapest possible package, at those speeds, without crosstalk, takes careful work on materials, packaging, signal integrity, and power integrity, and Cadence works with customers on where each memory should sit. Capacity is moving too: LPDDR6's second generation features allow more memory to be attached. Ferro closes by reducing the whole problem to two moves: reduce the amount of data that needs processing, and speed up how fast the processing gets done.

Open questions

  1. If the KV cache and optimizer states keep growing with model size, what actually caps the capability of a battery-powered robot: the memory that fits on its SoC, or the energy budget for moving data through it?
  2. Ferro treats processing inside DRAM as the next phase. What has to change in the LPDDR and HBM standards, and in who designs what, before in-memory computing ships in physical AI products rather than research demos?
  3. As LPDDR absorbs data center reliability features and gains capacity, where does HBM still justify its cost at the edge, and where does it price itself out of physical AI entirely?

Sam Witteveen · NVIDIA secret 2-bit model and the 4-bit rule

NVIDIA's Secret 2 Bit Model

On Sam Witteveen, "NVIDIA's Secret 2 Bit Model" looks at the model Microsoft teased at its local AI launch: a supposedly secret 2-bit model built to run on the coming RTX Spark laptops, which carry just 24 gigabytes of memory. NVIDIA has now shipped it. The model is Nemotron 3.5 Bolt 74B-A9B, a mixture of experts design with 74 billion parameters in total and about 9 billion active per token, built on NVIDIA's hybrid Mamba and Transformer architecture. It takes images and video, offers up to 256K of context, and stores all of its experts at 2 bits, so the whole thing fits in 24 gigabytes. NVIDIA claims it beats 4-bit models with a similar memory footprint.

Witteveen's argument is that the recipe matters more than the model. If you understand how NVIDIA got from the Nemotron 3.5 Super, a 120 billion parameter model whose full precision file runs to 148 gigabytes, down to a file of roughly 20 gigabytes, you have a map of nearly every technique now used to shrink large models: pruning, distillation, post-training, and quantization, all planned together rather than bolted on at the end.

He starts with the usual approach. Most downloadable models are quantized after training: the model is trained at 16 bits, then someone rounds every weight down to fewer bits, usually with calibration data to soften the damage. At 8 bits that rounding is basically free, and at 4 bits it is close to free, which is why 4-bit became the local default. Below 4 bits, post-training quantization falls apart. The sharp deterioration sits between 3 bits and 2 bits: at 2 bits and under, the network's representations change so much that the model effectively has to relearn, and simply rounding it gives it no chance to adapt.

The research NVIDIA leans on is ParetoQ, a paper from Meta. Its key finding is that when a model is trained properly at low precision, a 2 or 3-bit model can beat a post-training quantized 4-bit model at the same memory budget: a bigger model with fewer bits per weight wins. Witteveen admits he is still suspicious of the claim, mostly because it is not something third parties can easily do. It really needs to come from whoever trained the model, and Bolt is the test of whether it works.

There are honest caveats. The 2-bit figure applies to the experts. The Mamba layers sit at 4 bits in NVIDIA's NVFP4 format, and the embeddings and output layer are at 8 bits, so this is a mixed precision model rather than 2 bits everywhere, something NVIDIA is upfront about. Speed is the other caveat. On an RTX Spark, NVIDIA's own table puts Bolt at around 84 tokens per second, behind the recent Qwen and Gemma models. Witteveen's summary: this is a smarter model per gigabyte, not a faster one.

The case for going to 2 bits anyway has three parts. First, speed at the scale local users actually run: with one request at a time, generation is limited by how fast weights stream through memory, not by compute, so fewer bytes per weight means more tokens per second. Second, headroom: an agent model shares the machine with its KV cache, perhaps an embedding model and a speech model, plus a browser and an IDE, so a model that leaves memory free makes the whole setup work better. Third, it is a design choice: at a fixed memory budget you can ship a mid-sized model at 4 bits or a much larger one at 2 bits, and NVIDIA is betting on the larger one.

The build itself has three stages. Stage one is pruning. Starting from the 120 billion parameter Super, NVIDIA ran a technique called Star Elastic, which Witteveen describes as an architecture search with a memory budget: a smaller router learns, layer by layer, how many experts to keep and how wide each expert block should be. The number of experts active per token was cut from 22 down to 9. The budget was set in advance: the model had to fit under 24 gigabytes at batch size one with a 128K context window, and the pruning already assumed the experts would end at 2 bits, while the Mamba state stayed at 16 bits. The pruned model was then improved by distilling from its parent over about 120 billion tokens, mostly at an 8K context for speed, with the final chunks at the full 128K so the long context ability came back.

Stage two is ordinary post-training, still at full 16-bit precision. The vision encoder, which had been separated during pruning, is reattached. The model goes through supervised fine-tuning, checkpoint merges, and several rounds of reinforcement learning aimed at agent tasks. Only then comes stage three, the quantization, and this is where NVIDIA departs from simple rounding. It uses quantization-aware training, where low-bit rounding is simulated on every forward pass during training while a full precision copy of the weights is kept for the updates, so the model learns to live on the quantization grid. NVIDIA's twist is QAD, quantization-aware distillation: the quantized model is the student, and it learns from the unquantized version of itself. That, Witteveen notes, lets NVIDIA rerun the pipeline with quantization switched on and distill at the end, without needing all of the original training data again.

Bolt is part of a wider pattern. Google has shipped quantization-aware trained checkpoints for the Gemma family, and in the smallest Gemma 4 models the token generation layers are pushed down to 2 bits while the core reasoning layers stay at higher precision, bringing the smallest model in at under a gigabyte. OpenAI used similar ideas on its GPT-OSS models, which is one reason the 120 billion parameter version fits on a single 80 gigabyte GPU, and Moonshot has done related work on Kimi. His reading: the big labs now plan for quantization during training instead of leaving it to the community afterwards, because good low-bit versions are what make models popular on consumer hardware.

The quality cost, by NVIDIA's numbers, is real but modest next to the size change. On PinchBench the score moves from about 90 for the full model to about 88 for Bolt, and on GPQA Diamond from about 89 to about 86, while the file shrinks from 148 gigabytes to about 22. He also flags two neighbouring techniques. Cerebras has a method called REAP for pruning whole experts out of a mixture of experts model, with the finding that cutting experts beats merging them, because merging breaks the router's ability to choose; which experts survive depends heavily on the calibration data, so a model pruned on code stays good at code and may quietly get worse at everything else. And Microsoft's BitNet trains models from the first step with weights of just minus one, zero, or one, a true 1.58-bit model, but current GPUs lack proper hardware support for it. His conclusion is that NVIDIA's path, train big then prune, distill, and train down to low bits, is the realistic one for now.

His own tests, run on a ZGX Fury workstation built around a GB300, underline who Bolt is for. There it generates at about 220 tokens per second, while the full Super model on the same machine runs at roughly 500 to 600, with far faster prefill. On the pelican on a bicycle SVG test, both models produce mediocre drawings, which he attributes to neither being reinforced for that kind of task. On agentic tool calling, Bolt scores ten out of ten on the basic suite. On the harder pro suite it occasionally misses the prompt injection resistance test, scoring seven of eight, but the full size Super fails the same test in the same way, so the weakness is inherited rather than caused by the low bit rate. The Peterman Pod · Venki Ramakrishnan on AI, drug discovery and biology

Nobel Prize Winner: AI’s Impact On Drug Discovery And Biology | Venki Ramakrishnan

On The Peterman Pod, “Nobel Prize Winner: AI’s Impact On Drug Discovery And Biology | Venki Ramakrishnan” is host Ryan Peterman's conversation with Venki Ramakrishnan, the structural biologist who shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for work on the ribosome and now sits on the scientific advisory board of Isomorphic Labs, the drug discovery company founded by Demis Hassabis. The first half covers what AI can and cannot do in drug discovery. The second half is about Ramakrishnan's own career: a hard pivot out of physics, decades on an unfashionable problem, and what he thinks science actually rewards.

Ramakrishnan starts from the one clear success. AI has been used to predict how proteins fold, and that has worked really well, even if it is not perfect. A drug, though, is usually a molecule that has to bind in a critical pocket of a target protein and block it from working. AI can propose an array of candidate molecules that it thinks will fit, and it can make the traditional search, screening thousands of molecules and iteratively improving the hits, much more efficient. The early results look promising, he says, and people in the field are optimistic.

What AI cannot compress is everything downstream. Some candidate molecules will have toxic side effects, and AI can predict toxicity only in some cases. Molecules have to be synthesized, tested, and carried through clinical trials, and that experimental part, he says, is always going to be the bottleneck. Even monoclonal antibodies, a well understood class of drugs, have put people into critical care in phase one trials because of off-target binding. Trials also have to be funded and supplied with patients, which is the real limiting factor. His verdict is deliberately patient: it will become clearer in a few years, once there is experimental proof that AI has actually brought a drug to market faster.

He also doubts biology will follow the trajectory of mathematics or computer science, where models are redrawing the frontier. Biology is intrinsically more complex and is not a defined, closed system. AI has had the most impact on highly computational problems, like protein folding, and DeepMind has since released a program that tries to predict what genes will do when mutated. But the more complex the biology gets, from molecules to cells to whole organisms, the less impact AI has had so far.

The deeper limit, in his view, is that AI does not choose the questions. Even in mathematics, AI has become very good at solving increasingly hard problems, but the problems themselves, the judgment about what is interesting and important, were defined by human beings. He does not see AI announcing what we really need to understand and then solving it. The best way to think of it, he says, is as an incredibly powerful tool for humans to use. He describes himself as cautious rather than wildly optimistic.

Peterman then turns to the pivot. Ramakrishnan took a PhD in theoretical condensed matter physics and abandoned the field, not out of confidence but because he saw no future in it for himself: his thesis has gathered maybe twenty citations in fifty years. Biology was the pull, because it was going through a revolution. As a graduate student in La Jolla he read a Scientific American article by Peter Moore and Don Engelman about the ribosome, the ancient machine that reads genetic information and builds proteins. He wrote to them, received a fellowship to Yale, started over as a biology graduate student in 1978, taking undergraduate courses in his first year, and spent the rest of his career on that one molecule.

His rule for choosing problems is a directed random walk. The big questions, the origin of life, development, how the brain works, cannot be answered overnight. You need a vision of where you want to get to, and then you carve out steps that are each feasible with the tools that exist. Work on something important whose time has not come and you waste your career. Do only small incremental things and you wander. The skill is combining importance with feasibility, accepting detours while keeping a bias toward the goal.

The ribosome itself was a test of that rule. After the boom of the 1950s and 1960s, figures like Francis Crick and Sydney Brenner declared the basic problem solved and moved on, leaving the mechanism, the structure of a machine made of half a million atoms, untouched because it seemed impossible. When Ramakrishnan entered, the field was unfashionable, and by the end of his fellowship it was so unfashionable that he struggled to get a job. His CV did not help: he never finished a biology PhD, and a physics doctorate from Ohio University that no biologist could parse landed him, as he puts it, in pile B. He started over at Oak Ridge and then Brookhaven, learned crystallography, and when the technology ripened, with Ada Yonath's crystals showing the way, he and others finally solved the whole structure.

His advice to young scientists follows from that story. Credentials matter less than environment: pick the place with the right mentors, stimulating colleagues, and the infrastructure to do top level science, which is not the same as the highest ranked school. In his case, finishing the safer credential might have kept him off the ribosome entirely, and there is no way to know whether the safer path would have been the better one.

On the difference between his two fields, he says biology's questions are settled by experiment, not theory, and its culture runs on control experiments: add the factor, then see what happens without it, a discipline that scales up into randomized clinical trials. Physicists moving into biology often want to predict everything from first principles, but every system has to be dealt with at its own level of complexity. Physicists still invade other fields successfully, he notes, because they are comfortable with quantitative thinking, mathematics, and complex instruments.

He closes with the human side of the profession. Skill, he says, is not genius: skill is being well trained and competent at the craft, while genius is seeing a few steps beyond what is obvious to everyone else. He admits to having caught pre-Nobelitis, the warping that sets in when everyone tells you your work might win the prize and you start quietly talking up your own contribution. The books that shaped him were Feynman's Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman! and, despite its faults, including its treatment of Rosalind Franklin, James Watson's The Double Helix, which first showed him biology as a detective story rather than rote memorization, and which Jennifer Doudna also credits with drawing her into the field. His final advice is simple: stay open-minded, and do not let fear of change keep you in a rut.

  1. If AI makes candidate molecules cheap while trials stay slow, expensive, and short of patients, does the bottleneck in drug discovery simply move to funding and running trials, and who pays to test the extra candidates?
  2. Will AI's impact on biology keep fading as systems get more complex, from molecules to cells to organisms, or is there a route by which it jumps levels the way protein folding prediction did?
  3. If choosing the problem is the part of science AI does not do, can that judgment be taught deliberately, the way Ramakrishnan's directed random walk suggests, or is it only learned by spending years inside a field?

The short version: progress there has been running at roughly twice its earlier rate since late 2025, and METR thinks Anthropic has probably crossed the acceleration warning line it set for itself.

Mahajan is careful about what kind of exercise this was. METR ran a pilot with unusually high access to Anthropic's internal evidence, then published what it could. The headline finding is a sustained doubling of the rate of progress: about two years of AI progress compressed into one year, visible since late 2025. METR believes the acceleration is currently driven mostly by AI itself, though Mahajan stresses repeatedly that the evidence is uncertain. Just as notable is what the report did not find: no observed bottlenecks and no obvious signs of strong diminishing returns that would suggest the acceleration is about to slow.

Most of the publishable evidence rests on an index of well specified tasks, the kind that can be scored cleanly. That is also its limit. Whether the speedup generalizes to the fuzzy, long horizon work that recursive self-improvement would actually require is an open question, and Mahajan concedes the public evidence is compatible with seeing much less acceleration on those harder to specify tasks. He does point to signs of life, though: AI systems producing autonomous algorithmic improvements and major gains in parts of the training pipeline are the sorts of things one would expect to matter for recursive self-improvement, even if the evidence is still unclear.

A related question is whether returns are accelerating, meaning each capability gain makes the next one easier to win. METR found no evidence of that kind of hyperbolic, faster than exponential growth. Harry presses him on Chinese open-weight models, which some guests call safe to download and run locally and others call an unverifiable backdoor risk. Mensch's answer is that a model is a compression of knowledge, and if you do not control the data supply chain behind it, you cannot guarantee anything about its output. A planted behavior in some narrow context cannot be excluded and cannot be measured, only monitored. Mistral serves third party open models itself, because customers want choice and choice saturates its infrastructure, but roughly 80 percent of its token traffic is its own models, where it can vouch for the full training pipeline.

The reluctance is uneven. Defense customers in Europe would never use Chinese technology, finance is reluctant because it is critical infrastructure, and retail and parts of manufacturing are more relaxed. The concern Mensch hears more often is security of supply: frontier models are now powerful enough to run cyber attacks, the US began restricting their export in May, and China is looking at export limits of its own. Models are being treated as defense systems, so customers need guaranteed updates and supply on the model IP side, not just the hardware side.

That leads to the warning in the episode's title. Centralized providers, Mensch argues, hold immense leverage in a world with no sovereign alternative. The end game, which he says is pretty clearly what is being priced in today, is that an oligopoly of two or three providers uses the data produced by enterprise systems to build better systems and then goes after the business of its own customers. Mistral's purpose is to make sure that world does not arrive. His comfort is that building models is not impossibly hard: it takes capital and the right team, the open-weight frontier is catching up with the closed one, and in many domains it is already ahead.

On energy, the subject of the title the video carried when it was commissioned, Mensch is deliberately deflationary. Energy is around 10 percent of the total cost of ownership of compute. What dominates the bill is the cost of chips and the cost of capital. Europe has excess energy today, in France and the Nordics especially, and energy only becomes the bottleneck around 2029 and 2030, as multiple gigawatts of new capacity get deployed each year. Until then the challenge is building fast enough on the energy that already exists. After that, production has to ramp: France's EPR nuclear program, solar that can be built faster, and states that plan ahead, speed up permitting, align on dedicated sites, and offer guarantees on long data center leases to help companies with small balance sheets build ahead of demand.

Building a cloud, he explains, turned out to be an industrial process with nothing to do with software: supply chain latency across GPUs, CPUs, storage and electrical parts, demand forecasting across enterprise and AI native customers, and project financing. If Mistral were resource unconstrained, it would simply build much more capacity, because customers keep discovering that a use case they thought needed eight GPUs needs a thousand. Europe, in his view, is plainly short of compute, whatever may be true of the US buildout.

Sovereignty also has a granularity limit. Not every country can have its own frontier lab, because relevant models need a large market. Mistral operates across Europe, the US and Asia, with about 60 percent of revenue from Europe. Its enterprise work concentrates on four verticals: manufacturing, where it helps ASML build better machines and Airbus build better planes, financial services, defense, and the public sector, bringing reasoning models alongside physics and quantitative models that understand the physical world.

On vertical integration, he draws a line. There is no Mistral chip planned. The company has already moved from training models to serving them on infrastructure it owns, a shift that took about 18 months, and its tokens are generated purely in Europe in a way that escapes US extraterritorial law, which matters a great deal to its customers. At the application layer it builds custom business applications with its own forward deployed engineers working on common primitives, and it is now scaling that motion through system integrators such as TCS, since it cannot hire tens of thousands of engineers itself.

The business runs on two revenue lines that reinforce each other: platform license revenue for building business applications, and consumption revenue from the infrastructure. Customers arrive for the license and then discover they need GPUs or token services. Looking ahead, he expects the runtime side to grow fastest, the sandboxes, CPUs and storage that agents need when they execute tasks, call tools and run code in the background, work that is far more CPU intensive than answering conversations.

On inference, he describes a market behaving like a commodity market with high margins today: an on-demand side where price dominates, and a premium side where mature customers pay for reserved physical capacity and service level guarantees on latency and generation speed. On the crowded field of new labs, Stebbings counts more than a hundred neolab providers created, and Mensch says the survivors will combine research strength with a customer-centric enterprise motion and, critically, access to compute, which is the bottleneck strangling many young labs. Mistral reserves some of its own capacity for neolabs it wants to grow with.

He is skeptical of the hundred billion dollar training run arriving soon. Making a model strong in a domain like chip design or industrial engineering takes training environments, quality data and taste, none of which scale just by stacking compute. Asked why Nebius and CoreWeave are down sharply if everyone is compute starved, he says the market reaction is unclear to him, but notes Mistral finances its compute against revenue commitments from customers, stays anchored on their value creation, and has not leveraged the company heavily, and that in any industrial buildout leverage eventually breaks.

Focus, he says, is what he is most grateful for. Mistral skipped the consumer market, which is packed and ultimately an advertising business, and skipped chip design. The same discipline applies to self-improvement: AI can climb a well-defined problem, but the real friction in enterprises is ambiguous problems with humans in the loop, which is exactly why the forward deployed engineers exist, to define the problem properly. On growth, his test is physical. GDP rises from more production, not more intelligence: better factories, better ASML machines, and planes that fly for 40 years on less fuel. AI has to have a physical effect.

In the quick fire round, he repeats that the market is fragmenting and sovereign deployment will be the category that matters, predicts that within a few years every engineer, not just software engineers, will operate AI agents as a normal part of the job, and says a European IPO is entirely possible now that investor appetite for European listings has changed. What excites him most is AI enabling foundational research, running simulations at unprecedented scale and letting agents operate them, in physics and other fields where understanding of the world is still thin.

Open questions

  1. Mensch argues centralized providers will use enterprise data to build systems that compete with their own customers. What would count as evidence, early enough to act on, that this end game is actually underway?
  2. If energy only binds around 2029 to 2030 while chips and capital dominate today, which constraint actually bites first in Europe if permitting, grid connections and nuclear timelines slip?
  3. Mistral's sovereign pitch depends on the open-weight frontier staying close to the closed frontier. If that gap reopens, does the sovereign model survive, or do enterprises drift back to the centralized providers?

Sourcery with Molly O'Shea · Will Marshall on the Planet Labs factory

Planet Labs (NYSE: $PL) Factory Tour

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, "Planet Labs (NYSE: $PL) Factory Tour" is a walk through Planet's San Francisco satellite factory with Will Marshall, the company's co-founder and chief executive. There is no sit-down interview until the end. Most of the episode is Marshall moving from bench to bench, picking up hardware, and explaining how a company that started in a garage now builds satellites the way other companies build phones.

The scale claim comes first. Planet has built about 700 satellites in 15 years, more than any company on Earth except SpaceX, and Marshall says all 700 together cost less than a typical single NASA satellite. The reduction is roughly a thousandfold, and he credits miniaturization: satellites that once filled a room and cost a billion dollars or more have been shrunk until they can be launched in batches, four or five times a year, on more than 40 rockets so far.

The founding story is preserved on a whiteboard from the team's garage, where the first satellite was assembled on nights and weekends while the founders were still partly at NASA. The original experiment put a consumer phone on a suborbital rocket. The launch failed, the phone's electronics survived, and the data came back anyway. Marshall traces the idea to a question someone at NASA kept asking: why spend a billion dollars on a satellite when a 500 dollar phone already has most of what a satellite has? Planet's answer was to ride the consumer electronics industry, whose research budgets at Apple and Google dwarf anything space agencies spend, instead of inventing every component itself. Launch a few more satellites than needed, accept that one or two might fail, and the economics change completely. Marshall compares it to the move from mainframes to desktop computers.

What a working satellite actually does is easy to forget. A telescope roughly the size of a shoebox images the ground from 500 kilometers up, far enough to see individual trees and houses. Star cameras read constellations to fix the satellite's pointing, reaction wheels turn it, and radios beam images down at two gigabits a second over a range of more than a thousand kilometers. The first Planet satellites managed about a megabit a second, so each satellite now returns roughly a thousand times more data than the originals.

Holding focus is the quiet engineering problem. A telescope's optics cannot shift by more than a fraction of a wavelength, about a thousandth of a millimeter, or the image blurs. The satellite has to survive 15 g of shaking and 200 g shock loads at stage separation, then a thermal swing from minus 40 to plus 20 degrees Celsius every 45 minutes in orbit, without that focus moving. Much of the factory exists to prove this in advance: a shock table that slams a hammer down, a vibration table that simulates launch, thermal vacuum testing, and an RF chamber, a room lined with radio-absorbing material, where each satellite's radios are checked without reflections confusing the measurement.

The factory floor is organized by bird. Planet names its fleets after birds, a space tradition Marshall happily continues: Doves for the daily scan, Tanagers for hyperspectral work, Pelicans for high resolution, and Owls next. More than 650 Doves have been built in the lab, at a rate that can reach 40 a week, and each Dove takes about eight hours to assemble. Marshall contrasts that with his NASA years, when the equivalent figure was eight years. About 200 Doves are in orbit now, imaging the entire Earth every day, the only system that does so: about 4 million images a day, on the order of 100 terapixels, in eight spectral bands. A new facility in Berlin will roughly double manufacturing space and build up to 60 of the larger Pelicans a year.

The division of labor is simple to state. At the chip level, Planet buys almost everything. At the system level, it builds almost everything: telescopes integrated from purchased blanks, radios built from other people's chips into custom circuits and antennas, and 48 ground stations of its own at high-latitude sites in Alaska, northern Canada, northern Norway, and the southern tips of New Zealand and Australia, placed near the poles because the fleet flies in polar orbits. The Pelican shown in the clean room, Pelican 10, carries a xenon ion engine, about ten times more efficient in space than a chemical rocket engine, and flies unusually low, which is how the bird got the name: pelicans skim the water for extra lift, and the satellites skim the top of the atmosphere for sharper images.

The strategy point Marshall keeps returning to is that fleet size is not the metric. Planet already flies the largest Earth imaging fleet, about ten times larger than anyone else's, collecting roughly a hundred times more area than any other commercial actor. The next fleet, Owl, keeps the same daily coverage and about the same number of satellites, possibly slightly fewer, but moves from three-meter to one-meter resolution, which means about ten times more data per unit area, and adds optical links so the data comes back about ten times faster, within minutes. The owls are named for bigger eyes and, Marshall says, wisdom: they carry a large amount of compute on board.

The Tanager fleet shows what the data is for. Its hyperspectral instruments can spot a methane plume from orbit, and when Planet tells a gas company about a leak, switching that one leak off is, in pollution terms, like taking a couple of hundred thousand cars off the road. Against that, Marshall is candid about the industry's real hazard: not the roughly 10,000 operating satellites, but the roughly 100 million pieces of space debris, fragments of satellites that broke apart, crashed, or were destroyed by anti-satellite weapons, each one capable of shattering another satellite into tens of thousands more.

His reliability boast comes with a twist. Of the 700 satellites launched, every one that reached orbit turned on and worked, with only minor faults along the way: an out-of-focus image here, a solar panel that did not deploy, a reaction wheel that stopped early. Marshall says he actually regards the perfect record as a problem, because it suggests the team is not pushing the envelope hard enough on sensors and capability.

The episode ends upstairs with the product all of this feeds: an AI interface over Planet's archive, some 3,000 layers of imagery covering every day for ten years. A large language model sits on the back end, so instead of training analysts to query satellite data, a farmer can ask about their own field, or a journalist about a flood or fire, and the system searches the entire Earth in under a second and shows change over time. For the demo, O'Shea asks about Area 51, and the interface compares the site in 2021 with 2026, structural changes and aircraft movements visible. Marshall's framing is that imagery used to be usable only by the likes of NASA and intelligence agencies; tying it to a language model opens it to anyone who can ask a question, in any language. The interface is in a beta launch now, with a full launch planned soon.

Open questions

  1. Owl promises one-meter daily imagery and about ten times more data from roughly the same number of satellites, delivered about ten times faster over optical links. If the links or the onboard compute lag the sensors, does the daily whole-Earth promise survive at the new resolution?
  2. Planet's cost revolution rested on cheap consumer electronics and a willingness to lose a satellite or two. As the fleet shifts toward larger, costlier Pelicans and Owls built at rates of dozens a year rather than hundreds, how much of that economics still holds?
  3. The AI interface is in beta and headed for a general launch, putting a decade of daily imagery of everywhere, including sites like Area 51, behind plain-language questions. What limits, if any, will Planet place on who can ask what about whom?
