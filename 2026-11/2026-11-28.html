Will Phillips · Inside Runway with Alejandro Matamala Ortiz on world models and robotics

24h Inside a $5.3B AI Research Lab Building World Models for the Robotics Industry

On Will Phillips, "24h Inside a $5.3B AI Research Lab Building World Models for the Robotics Industry" spends the day inside Runway, the New York company best known for AI video generation, as it pushes that research into interactive characters and robotics. The host, Will Phillips, has used Runway's models since Gen 1 in 2023, and he frames the visit around one question: whether a company built on video can turn that head start into a lasting advantage in the physical world, where far richer rivals such as Google are chasing the same prize.

Runway's scale is the first thing he establishes. The company recently raised a $315 million Series E led by General Atlantic, at a reported valuation of $5.3 billion, and its New York office fills two floors in the Flatiron district, one of them designed by a co-founder. The office keeps its own origin story on display: the first AI generated video the team made has been turned into a flip book, a piece of physical media commemorating a digital first.

Alejandro Matamala Ortiz, a co-founder and the company's chief innovation officer, describes Runway as a full stack research company that builds foundation models for image, video and audio and turns them into products for the creative world. His own starting point was one of the earliest text to image models, which produced tiny, pixelated 256 by 256 pictures and still astonished him, because the loop of offering an idea to a system and getting something back felt like a new creative process.

The funding story runs against the usual script. Runway began with three students at art school building a tool they wanted for themselves, and when word of mouth carried the project to venture capitalists, the founders at first simply did not answer the emails, because they did not think of themselves as building a startup. They started replying once they understood they would need resources and people to scale, and raised a seed round backed by Lux Capital, Amplify and Compound.

The original ambition, Matamala Ortiz says, was to let anyone in the world create a fully generated two hour feature film. The turn toward robotics came from a discovery along the way: the models were very good at simulating the world, at capturing how objects move and interact. He sorts the future of these world models into three buckets. The first is media and entertainment, where Runway started. The second is real time immersive applications such as video games and virtual reality. The third is physical AI, where generated video stops being something people watch and becomes training material from which robots learn how to behave in the physical world.

The newest product on the media side is Characters, which turns a single image into a talking AI character you can hold a conversation with. Phillips tests one, a tennis coach, which fields his question about improving his backhand with tips on preparation and footwork. The engineering difficulty, a team member explains, is latency: the model is a diffusion model, generation takes time, and a conversational character has to start answering within about two seconds of the user stopping talking, or the illusion breaks. The team's claimed edge is simplicity, one photo uploaded and a working session within a few minutes.

The robotics push partly arrived by acquisition. The founder of Kinetix, a company that joined Runway with its team, explains the logic in blunt market terms: there are two types of winners now, foundation model companies and companies with a moat on distribution, and everything in the middle is dying. Asked why he joined rather than compete, he says his team looked for the best lab in the market and the answer was clearly Runway.

Downstairs, the robotics floor runs on demonstrations and evaluations. At one evaluation station, a robot operator named Jill helps run a basic task that sanity checks a model before it graduates to harder ones. The head of robotics, Andy, points to a quiet advantage of starting from video: a model pretrained on video has seen plenty of sponges and foam, so it already understands contact dynamics that a robot only model would have to learn from scratch. His central claim is about scaling laws. Just as ChatGPT emerged after a slow burn with transformers, he argues, adding more data and more compute to these models will eventually produce what the team calls a ChatGPT moment for robotics, and he is hopeful something arrives in the next year or two.

A second station, a machine the team calls Brunello, collects the demonstrations those models learn from, using leader and follower arms: a human moves the leader arms and the follower arms mirror them. Phillips takes a turn at the controls and finds it harder than it looks. Then comes the payoff demo. A robot running one of the team's policy models is told, completely zero shot, to pick up the red die from a cluttered surface full of distractors. It finds the die and lifts it, a task the team describes as right at the edge of the robot's current capabilities.

Phillips closes with a prediction of his own: the day is coming when spinning up a working robotics company, one that can deploy real hardware, will be as easy as spinning up an online AI tool, and it is not far away. Matamala Ortiz gets the last word, on the value of an ambitious north star. In 2018, promising a fully generated two hour feature film drew many noes from people who said it would never be possible, and working backwards from that goal kept the team from being distracted. Today, he says, that same promise is no longer ambitious at all. The goal now is to simulate the world entirely.

Open questions

  1. Runway's robotics bet rests on video pretraining transferring to physical work, down to contact dynamics learned from watching sponges and foam. What would count as decisive evidence, beyond staged demos, that world models learned from video can carry a robot through messy, contact rich tasks in the open world?
  2. Andy expects a ChatGPT moment for robotics within the next year or two, driven by scaling data and compute. If it arrives, does the advantage belong to video first labs like Runway, or to whoever controls the largest fleet of robots collecting real demonstrations?
  3. The Kinetix founder argues the middle is dying, squeezed between foundation model labs and distribution moats. Can a lab of Runway's size hold a frontier position against Google scale budgets on research alone, or does it eventually need the distribution moat he describes?
