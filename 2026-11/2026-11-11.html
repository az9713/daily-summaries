Stripe · John Collison and Sam Gerstenzang on building companies with AI

Fireside | John Collison and Sam Gerstenzang on building companies with AI

On Stripe, “Fireside | John Collison and Sam Gerstenzang on building companies with AI” is the closing fireside from Stripe's SaaS Platform Summit 2026, recorded live, in which Stripe co-founder John Collison interviews Sam Gerstenzang, who used to work at Stripe and left to co-found Boulton & Watt. Collison jokes that Stripe aspires to be the world's fastest company while Gerstenzang runs the world's slowest startup incubator: Boulton & Watt starts one company every few years, very deliberately, puts all of its focus into that one company, runs it until it is well past product-market fit, and only then finds a chief executive to replace the founders.

The studio looks for industries that are overlooked, regulated, complex, or taboo, and it works backwards from the profit and loss statement rather than starting with a product. Its first company, Moxie, helps nurses and doctors open their own med spas. Its second does the same for funeral homes, and its third works in freight, where millions of companies ship goods across the United States and some 500,000 trucking companies, most with between one and ten trucks, need a broker sitting in the middle. In each case the aim is to run everything the business needs except the core craft itself, so that in the ideal end state an operator has only three expenses: the platform, taxes, and real estate.

Moxie shows how far that goes. Opening a med spa is regulatory complicated, and a nurse might otherwise spend about 20,000 dollars on a lawyer in each state before serving a single customer, so Moxie built the equivalent of a Stripe Atlas product to be there before the business even exists. Then it took over the marketing that grows the practice, then the software that takes payments, built on Stripe payments, Treasury, and invoicing, and then the purchasing: med spas spend about 30 percent of their revenue on pharmaceuticals such as Botox, so Moxie became one of the largest buyers of Botox in the country and passes the savings along. The freight company works the same way from the other side, giving an experienced broker the backing to leave a big firm, take the risk himself, and connect shippers with trucks.

What unites med spas, funeral homes, and freight brokers, Gerstenzang says, is that they are local businesses with something close to a geographic monopoly, preoccupied with finding customers within a radius and with not running out of cash each month. Their owners are good at the job they signed up for, not at the suffering of running a business. Boulton & Watt is also, in effect, taking the other side of the private equity roll-up bet: when a chain buys the local owner out, the number two or three employee is often left thinking that should have been them, and the studio helps that person compete against the chains with better buying power than the chains themselves have.

The largest change AI has brought is that the studio now builds custom software for individual customers, something Gerstenzang says would have been crazy five years ago. One of its freight brokers works out of a customer's office in Louisiana, where three brokers compete for every job through the customer's own portal, and the studio spent two hours building software that automates that portal work for him. The pattern is a common back end and a fixed, opinionated set of APIs, with cheap new front ends built on top for each customer's way of working. The older companies in the portfolio show the cost of getting there: the first company has needed two big re-architectures, one so that AI systems can understand the code as well as the engineer who named everything, and one that forced the whole team to question its assumptions. Everything that ships to customers is still reviewed by a software engineer, with different standards for internal tools, and the teams themselves have been reshaped into pairs, one product manager matched with one engineer as domain experts, while the total number of engineers and the total software spend have stayed roughly flat.

Asked where value goes in software over the next five to ten years, Gerstenzang's answer is risk. His studio goes looking for risk because customers will pay to offload it: taking full responsibility for a job, sharing credit risk, carrying compliance risk. No nurse is going to vibe code her own medical records system, he says, because she cares too much about HIPAA rules and about her license. On the freight side, the company takes the risk that a shipper actually pays and that a truck does not drive off with the load. Software, in this view, is the enabling layer and the conduit, not the point. The systems of record, the Salesforces and Workdays, stay powerful because so many different users and workflows meet in them, but agents change where the interaction happens: if a customer would rather work through a new interface sitting on top of Workday's data, Workday has to reach deeper into solving other problems, and take on more risk, to survive. Pure software businesses, he argues, now need scale and buying power, or a risk they can take on that customers will pay for, because the variance in what money spent on software development buys has collapsed.

Collison presses him on whether Boulton & Watt is really a chain in disguise, something like the Chick-fil-A or Domino's model, given the training centers, the shared playbook, and the bulk buying. Gerstenzang says the software approach creates a different margin profile and lets the studio go deeper into the actual work, and that being the vertical specialist is itself the moat: the med spa company hosts its nurses on shoe-shopping trips, is opening a training center in Los Angeles where influencers with large TikTok followings teach injection classes, and can credibly call itself the med spa people in a way no horizontal player can. The sales motion has matured with the model. The original painkiller was simply getting started, the licensing and incorporation problem, sold to a nurse leaving an existing practice. Having followed those customers from one site to many, the studio now sells to existing businesses doing five to ten million dollars at a single location by showing a before and after profit and loss: you will pay 13 percent, he tells them, but it will feel like it cost negative 2 percent.

The conversation is equally specific about where AI has not worked. It cannot come up with the new business idea, and it cannot separate user segments and exercise the judgment of how to sequence features, though it is good at summarizing and at resurfacing notes from two years ago. A pre-AI human team that handled front desk calls for a hundred med spas was replaced with a voice AI that was, by their own measurement, better than the humans, and customers rejected it anyway: they could understand why a human made a mistake, so the voice version had to be perfect to be trusted, the same fault tolerance problem as self-driving cars. Adoption inside the older companies has also taken far longer than Gerstenzang expected, because of data integration work and change management. Nobody has been fired, and legal expenses have not fallen either; the firms simply consume more legal services, checking documents they once skipped, while important contracts still go to a lawyer. On talent, he hires for high agency, high curiosity, and high judgment, testing for agency by looking for a pattern of leaps a person made happen themselves.

For the fourth company, the studio looks for a tailwind that will happen without them and a glitch in the universe that suddenly makes a startup possible. Restoration, the insurance-paid work that follows burst pipes and climate damage, is one area under study, along with fire safety, and he mentions, only half joking, animal hospice over Zoom. The session closes on an experiment not yet tried: letting end users prompt changes to their own custom software through a text box in the corner. Gerstenzang is curious and wary in equal measure, since that is where the maintenance problems of endlessly morphing software would begin, and Collison urges him to try it with one customer and report back.

Open questions

  1. If the variance in software outcomes keeps collapsing, how many pure software companies can actually make the shift to taking on risk, and what happens to the ones that cannot?
  2. Will customers eventually be allowed to prompt changes to their own custom software, and who carries the maintenance burden when software starts morphing under its users?
  3. Does the one-to-one pairing of product manager and engineer survive inside larger and more complex companies, or is it an advantage that belongs to small teams and fades with scale?

The Knowledge Project Podcast · Jon McNeill on the impossible speed of Elon Musk

The Impossible Speed of Elon Musk - Ex-Tesla President

On The Knowledge Project Podcast, “The Impossible Speed of Elon Musk - Ex-Tesla President” is host Shane Parrish in conversation with Jon McNeill, who served as president of Tesla and went on to become chief operating officer of Lyft and a co-founder of DVx Ventures. McNeill spent years inside Musk's companies, and the episode is his account of how they actually run day to day: the speed, the communication rules, and the decision habits behind Tesla's growth. He says he scaled Tesla roughly tenfold in sales while he was there.

His first meeting with Musk was at SpaceX, where the two of them walked the Model X line for the better part of two hours working a real problem: workers bolting on the Falcon Wing doors had to do it blind, reaching underneath the chassis, and McNeill suggested a jig so they could follow a pattern. They built the jig overnight. Musk, he says, was assessing how he processes problems, and told him success would look like the two of them reaching the same answer from the same inputs. Speed of every kind, decision, cash through the business, response time, is Musk's core belief, because competitors are probably moving slower.

Before he had even formally joined, McNeill overstepped. Musk named demand as his biggest problem: about 12,000 cars promised for the quarter, only about 3,000 sold with half the quarter gone. McNeill test drove at eight stores under eight different email addresses and never got a single call back. Sales operations found roughly 10,000 test drivers in the previous 30 days who had never been called. Staff were incented to give test drives but, with no commissions at Tesla, had no incentive to ask for the sale. McNeill had new test drive leads shut off worldwide until staff called their earlier drivers back, orders poured in, and then he called Musk to apologize for acting without checking in. After a long silence, Musk told him it was a great decision and that he would fit in fine, because what mattered was speed of decision guided by first principles, not hierarchy.

The culture he describes routes around hierarchy entirely. In most companies a question travels up and back down the chain like an EKG trace, and Musk wanted communication to look like a flat line instead: go straight to the person who has the answer. Two communication rules followed. Emails were to run to three sentences, the problem, the proposed solution, and the next step with any ask for resources or permission. And bad news had to travel as fast as it was known, because bad news only gets worse with time. Borrowing from Sheryl Sandberg's practice with Mark Zuckerberg, the two also held a weekly meeting that opened by clearing the air about anything frustrating before turning to business. McNeill says he arrived at Tesla as a conflict avoider and left direct, and that he learned to pair hard feedback with explicit respect for the person receiving it.

On incentives, he cites Charlie Munger's dictum to follow the incentives. Musk refused commissions because he did not want Tesla stores to feel like car dealerships, so the team built a non-monetary substitute: a leaderboard in every store ranking sellers by sales that month, with a line drawn near the middle that nobody explained. People worked hard to stay above the line. The currency became asking for the order.

Risk, in Musk's framing, is capital allocation, and capital allocation is a bet to be sized by asymmetry. McNeill adopted the poker version of the same idea: if the asymmetry sits on the downside, do not make the bet unless the downside can be removed; if it sits on the upside, allocate and run. Musk told him he wanted a fellow entrepreneur rather than a big company hire precisely because entrepreneurs learn to assess risk. The same logic explains Autopilot's weekly, sometimes near daily, updates. A disengagement a driver reports is replayed through simulation, a corrective action is trained and proven in simulation, and once the team is highly confident it ships, starting a feedback loop instead of surrendering months of learning to caution. Cars, McNeill says, can appreciate through software rather than depreciate like every other car.

His decision cheat sheet starts with the end: define the outcome you want, then ask what would need to be true for it, then what investment that requires, and finally weigh impact against effort alongside everything else competing for the same capital and time. The no list, a lesson from Sandberg, should be far longer than the yes list. On imperfect information he is blunt: action creates information, information creates certainty, and waiting hands the learning curve to a competitor. Musk's own variant is an ability to shut off failure; after a string of exploded SpaceX rockets in 2017 and 2018, he focused only on the couple of things that had to be true for the next launch to succeed.

Product meetings opened with Musk turning to McNeill and asking what the market wants. When the answer was that mothers wanted a bench seat in the Model X instead of the two captain's chairs, Musk went straight to a whiteboard with his engineering and supply chain leads and began designing in real time. Slides were banned for product work because they mask problems; the product itself had to be in the room. Leaders also ate their own cooking: executives took a random car off the line each night, drove it home, and had notes waiting in engineers' inboxes by 8 the next morning, while McNeill read Reddit and the forums daily and Musk took the feedback on X. Great products, in his telling, are simple to the point of elegance, and a great product sells itself. He repeats the story of Musk cornering Steve Jobs for his design philosophy and getting the answer that a perfect product sells itself, and of Jobs's team spending a week locked on two iPod requirements, a thousand songs and finding any song in four seconds or less, then refusing every feature, down to an FM radio, that served neither.

The marketing budget at Tesla was, in Musk's words, precisely zero, against a mandate to double the company every six to eight months. The substitute was Musk himself: his following was built deliberately, first through space fans and then car content, every post was product news, and the resulting press was repurposed as marketing. Scaling a car company tenfold also meant reinventing the physical model. Mobile service replaced many service centers once the team found most repairs could be done in a driveway, and Doug Field's casting breakthrough, pitched by rolling a Hot Wheels car across the table, replaced a welded skeleton of hundreds of parts with castings, eliminating half the factory and, McNeill says, buying Tesla a lead of about eight years that rivals are only now closing.

Neuralink began in the back of a Model S in Hong Kong, when Musk observed that typing with thumbs runs at about 18 words a minute against 60 or more on a keyboard, and asked what it would mean to think into the machine instead. Within a week he was meeting leading brain computer interface researchers. The episode also lays out the five step algorithm from McNeill's book, born of postmortems on big mistakes: question every requirement, delete as many process steps as you can, optimize what remains, use speed as a revealer of failure points, and automate only last. He gives a current example from DVx: thematic exchange traded funds weighted by a product's bill of materials rather than by market capitalization, so an AI infrastructure fund holds the companies that actually supply a data center. Company processes, he says, are gardens nobody weeds, and Musk's habit of asking whether Tesla really needed Salesforce, a tax on growth, was the weed whacker applied weekly.

Leaving Tesla was personal. His family staged an intervention over the Christmas holidays, telling him they did not like who he was becoming: moody and grumpy, with the stress of trying to support Musk through a rough period for his mental health leaking out at home despite his efforts to compartmentalize it. He told Musk he could not help with those problems and needed to go. It did not feel like failure, he says, so much as leaving a job and people he loved, and many of those teammates work with him still. He resists the idea that a leader has to imitate Musk or Jobs; Walter Isaacson's answer to his question about whether success requires being a jerk was that it requires being authentic, and Satya Nadella is his example of effectiveness without the abrasion.

The back half ranges widely. There is Calvin McDonald's fire yourself exercise, asking what a successor would obviously do in the first weeks and then doing it now. There is hiring: McNeill spent about 40 percent of his time at Tesla interviewing, looks for candidates whose detailed grasp of their own work proves they did it rather than managed it, and treats the final interview as cultural imprinting, a chance to hand a new hire the decoder ring for how success works. There are mentors, vertical ones who have seen the movie before, like Scott Cook, whose Follow Me Home exercise of having customers vote on fixes and wishes he later ran with Lyft drivers, and horizontal ones, peers who see your problem from the side. There is his deepest professional regret, a startup where he and his co-founder never reconciled organic growth against growth by acquisition, and the staff lived inside that tension for years. And there is Lyft, where he arrived during the delete Uber moment, doubled market share and revenue, and took the company public, but concluded that ride share's original sin was competing on price: once two interchangeable products compete on price, the business becomes a commodity and margins collapse.

DVx, his venture firm, now applies a five point test before building a company: a big addressable market confirmed by customer interviews, gross margins above 50 percent, an unsolved hard technical problem that could become a moat, a competitive field the firm is not crowding into, and liquidity in the space. The firm has launched 17 companies this way. His own definition of success is simpler: kids and a wife who want to be around him, time outdoors, and time to keep creating companies, because after his first exit he found the lasting satisfaction was not the money but the jobs, the receptionist who bought a house and the colleague whose kids' college was paid for.

Open questions

  1. McNeill's system depends on leaders who can judge asymmetry, imprint culture in final interviews, and read frontline feedback daily, so how much of it survives in a company whose founder lacks Musk's appetite for that work?
  2. Tesla's zero marketing model was built when Musk's audience and the novelty of the product did the work, so does the model still hold now that Tesla does advertise and the car market has caught up on electric vehicles?
  3. He argues that competing on price destroyed ride share economics, yet his own funds and venture studio compete in crowded financial markets, so where does he draw the line between healthy competition and the commodity trap?

The Francis Crick Institute · Brian Cox and panel on beating the superbugs

Can we beat the superbugs? - A Question of Science with Brian Cox

On The Francis Crick Institute, “Can we beat the superbugs? - A Question of Science with Brian Cox” is a live panel recorded at the Crick in London, in which host Brian Cox puts audience questions to four researchers who work on drug-resistant microbes: Adam Roberts of the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine, Sabrina Moyo of the University of Bergen and the Liverpool School, Max Gutierrez, a cell biologist and group leader at the Crick, and Martha Clokie, professor of microbiology at the University of Leicester. The series is a BBC Studios production for the institute, and the question running through the session is whether antibiotics could stop working on a global scale, and what science can do about it.

The panel starts with the scale of the problem. Drug-resistant infections already kill about 1.3 million people a year, and forecasts suggest resistance could kill more people than cancer by 2050. Moyo warns that even the current figure is probably an underestimate, because in many low-income countries there is no documentation of who died of what and no diagnosis, so those deaths never enter the data. Antibiotics sit at the heart of medicine as we know it, Clokie says, supporting everything from routine infection treatment to surgery, cancer care, and chronic disease management, so a post-antibiotic era would make every branch of medicine more dangerous. The exposure is wider than hospitals, too: pets and farm animals are treated with antibiotics, the agricultural and fishing industries depend on them, and in Florida an estimated 20,000 tons of streptomycin are injected into citrus trees, so a collapse in effectiveness would reach food production as well as clinics.

The biology works against us. Bacteria challenged with a drug explore their evolutionary options at extraordinary speed: a petri dish seeded with ten or twenty E. coli holds tens of millions within a day, enough that every base pair has likely had a chance to mutate, and a rare advantageous mutation, perhaps one in ten million, is all resistance needs. Resistance is also ancient. Microbes have been fighting each other for billions of years, most of our antibiotics are products of that microbial warfare in the soil, and bacteria revived from ice age samples already carry resistance. Gutierrez adds that microbes have many tricks, from sharing resistance genes on plasmids to growing shells that block a drug's entry, and that resistant bacteria typically appear somewhere in the world soon after any new antibiotic arrives.

Asked what is driving pathogens to evolve faster than medicine can keep up, Moyo points first to misuse: the wrong antibiotic, given to the wrong patient, at the wrong dose or for the wrong duration. Environment is the second front, as hospital and manufacturing wastewater carries antibiotics and resistance genes into places where they mix and spread, and livestock farming adds more pressure, including reports of HIV antiretroviral drugs being used to speed growth in poultry and pigs. Gutierrez adds a quieter driver: substandard medicines. In countries with weak regulation, an antibiotic may contain less active drug than its label claims, and for a disease like tuberculosis, which needs at least three antibiotics for at least six months, under-dosed treatment manufactures resistance directly.

The threat is not limited to bacteria. Fungi may be worse off, the panel says, because there are only about four main classes of antifungal drugs against roughly twenty-five classes of antibacterials, the drugs penetrate fungi poorly, and fungi resemble human cells closely enough that treatments risk toxicity. Candida auris, a fungus that has emerged independently in several parts of the world, already shows resistance in about forty percent of isolates. Viruses and parasites also evolve resistance, but bacteria account for more than ninety percent of the mortality because they grow and spread so fast.

The most developed alternative the panel discusses is phage therapy: using bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria, as medicines. Clokie explains, with a soft toy phage produced from her pocket, that a phage recognises its target bacterium by its tail fibres, injects its DNA, and turns the cell into a factory that releases more phages until the infection is cleared, a self-replicating medicine that multiplies at the site of infection. Phages pair with antibiotics in several ways. They can kill bacteria that antibiotics no longer touch, their enzymes can chew through the biofilms antibiotics cannot penetrate, and some phages bind to the very pumps bacteria use to eject antibiotics, so bacteria that evolve to block the phage lose the pump and become sensitive to the antibiotic again. Gutierrez describes complementary strategies: combining antibiotics with treatments that boost the innate immune response, which targets the host rather than the microbe and so invites almost no resistance, and exploiting collateral sensitivity, where resistance to one antibiotic makes a bacterium more sensitive to another, so drugs can be sequenced intelligently.

Asked how close phage therapy is to mainstream use in the UK, Clokie is blunt: it is almost never used, reached only by a small number of patients whose doctors persisted when nothing else was left. The UK has fallen into a vicious circle identified by a House of Commons inquiry three years ago: phages must be manufactured to a certified standard before they can be trialled, but nobody will fund that manufacturing without trial evidence, and there can be no trial evidence without manufactured phages. The regulator, the MHRA, has now issued guidance, so use is technically possible, but the infrastructure is missing. Georgia offers the counterexample: the Eliava Institute, founded in 1923, has practised phage therapy for a century, and Belgium has made the most progress in Europe, helped by Georgian specialists on its team.

Stewardship of the antibiotics we already have needs both culture change and better tools, Moyo argues. The public needs to absorb that antibiotics work only on bacterial infections, and clinicians need rapid diagnostics: a doctor facing a child with a possible bloodstream infection cannot wait, so without a fast answer on whether the infection is viral or bacterial, and which drug will work, the safe default is a broad-spectrum antibiotic, and that default is exactly what breeds resistance.

New antibiotics are scarce for economic rather than scientific reasons. Roberts explains that a new antibiotic can cost upwards of 1.5 billion dollars and twenty years to develop, after which clinicians are asked to lock it away and use it as little as possible, so most large pharmaceutical companies have left the field and discovery has fallen to academics and small firms. His own answer at Liverpool is a citizen science project: with no funding for expeditions, the public posts in swabs of anything, from fridge grime to pets, and the team has built a library of more than 80,000 bacterial isolates, many never studied for antibiotic production, and is finding new compounds that kill resistant pathogens. Clokie's phage hunting works the same way: seventy percent of known phages were found on just twenty bacterial species, her project has found 2,000 new phages that kill E. coli, and the best donors are mundane places like wastewater and zoos. Gutierrez's group works the other end of the problem, using imaging to track whether antibiotics actually reach and enter the bacteria they are meant to kill, work that bears on why tuberculosis needs three or four pills a day for six months. Prevention also counts: Moyo's study in Tanzania gave healthy newborns probiotics for one month and reduced their gut carriage of multidrug-resistant ESBL bacteria, the kind that make later infections hard to treat.

The final audience question is political: microbes ignore borders, while the structures fighting them are fiercely national. Moyo argues the pitch to wealthy countries should not be charity but global health security, since strengthening health systems in the global South slows the emergence and spread of resistance everywhere. Cox floats funding antibiotics as a public good, with taxpayers paying for discovery, production, and distribution, and suggests one government moving first could bring others along; he also calls it short-sighted to raise defence spending while cutting the planet's biosecurity. Martha Clokie describes the charity Phages for Global Health, which trains doctors, vets, and microbiologists in the global South to run their own phage programmes rather than importing finished treatments.

The panel closes on qualified optimism. Clokie calls phages an unopened treasure chest: every major pathogen on the WHO priority list has phages that attack it. Gutierrez points to new tools such as artificial intelligence for simulating how resistance evolves. Roberts is hopeful because the chemical building blocks for new drugs plainly exist and only need looking for, and Moyo takes optimism from the shift beyond new antibiotics alone, towards vaccines, probiotics, phages, and prevention: with those, she says, we can be faster than the bacteria themselves.

Open questions

  1. Who should pay to break the phage therapy deadlock, funding certified manufacturing before trial evidence exists, and is the taxpayer-funded public good model Cox suggests politically achievable in even one country?
  2. Can rapid diagnostics reach frontline clinics, including in low-income countries, fast enough to change prescribing defaults before broad-spectrum overuse does more damage?
  3. Will combination strategies such as collateral sensitivity sequencing and immune boosting hold up in routine clinical practice, or will they stay research findings while resistance keeps spreading?

YC Root Access · Chaz Englander on Model ML and the AI operating system for finance

How Model ML Is Building the AI Operating System For Finance

On YC Root Access, "How Model ML Is Building the AI Operating System For Finance" is a YC x London conversation in which YC partner Gustaf Alströmer interviews Chaz Englander, the co-founder and chief executive of Model ML. Model ML, from Y Combinator's Winter 2024 batch, is building what Englander calls the AI operating system for financial services, used by large banks, asset managers, and consulting firms. It is his third YC company with his younger brother Arie: they founded Fat Llama in 2017 and a second company in 2020, and sold both.

The intensity of this company, Englander says, is unlike anything from the first two. For its first year the team worked nine in the morning to nine at night, seven days a week. Model ML came out of stealth in February of last year with five to ten people and now has about 200 across eight offices in the major financial hubs, adding roughly 20 people a week, and it has raised over 150 million dollars. He keeps reminding the team that agent harnesses could not interact with Excel until February of this year, so in his view almost everything built before that was useless for finance, and with customer procurement cycles running six to twelve months, the current moment is close to winner takes all.

The backstory starts with two English brothers who wanted to build a consumer software business. Englander studied accounting and finance, taught himself to code, and was obsessed with startups and with YC's own podcast; he still remembers shaking through the 2017 interview trip and the Skype call that told them they were in. Friends were mostly confused, and one, in front of a group, mocked him for still doing full-time entrepreneurship. He traces the drive partly to a hyper-competitive family, a mother who ran ultramarathons professionally, a father who played professional rugby, and four boys for whom everything was a contest, and partly to a love of learning: people joining from elite banks and universities, he says, learn more in their first two weeks at the company than in their whole prior education.

Two lessons from the earlier companies carry straight into this one. The first is the YC batch itself: founders who go through the misery together become lifelong friends who protect each other, which is a large part of why the brothers did YC a third time when they did not have to. The second is emotional resilience, the ability to stay unflustered when something is on fire, plus a tactical rule for fast scaling: when a company goes from 10 people to 200 in nine months, the veterans have been there four weeks, so communication lines about where questions and information go have to be made completely explicit.

Model ML itself began as a tool the brothers built for their own lives. After selling their second company they ran a small family office, investing their own money and experimenting with applying large language models to their own work, including the due diligence grind they had suffered through twice as sellers: banks of questions, answered by typing. For about three years Englander pitched Arie new ideas, and the one Arie picked was the system they were already using themselves. It was, Englander says, staring them in the face.

What the product has become is broader than the point solutions of a year ago. Instead of a tool a banker visits for one task, it behaves more like a digital twin of the end user, which is why, he says, about 60 percent of users open it every day. His example is a managing director monitoring thousands of companies for take-private signals across qualitative and quantitative metrics: where juniors once updated columns in a huge spreadsheet, the agent now watches for a trigger such as a management change, does the leveraged buyout modelling, builds the 55-page pitch deck, and brings the finished pitch to the banker. Work that took a team of about ten people is now automatic. The human side of that, he argues, is juniors no longer ordering logos in slides at 2 a.m., and moving to strategic work instead; in finance, for now, the more AI a firm uses, the more money it makes.

The timing argument runs through the talk. Agent harnesses could not work properly with numbers or with Excel until February or March of this year, so the company is riding model improvement rather than fighting it, and Englander urges founders to bet on models getting better. He also separates the open source and closed source worlds: running capable open source models on your own hardware unlocks work that cost previously ruled out. Among the closed models, performance in his world has spread from a heavy concentration in Anthropic to a fairly even split across Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google. That is why no bank can bet on a single model: best means accuracy, latency, and cost at once, and the only safe route is consuming AI through a model-agnostic agent harness like theirs.

On fundraising, his view is that investors are betting on the person far longer than founders assume, possibly until a raise of half a billion, and what they are really testing is whether this founder will work as hard as it takes and how high the ambition runs. A billion dollar company is not ambitious enough now, he says; founders should be aiming at something far larger, and the most ambitious people he knows look almost deluded to everyone else. Everyone gets nos: 19 out of 20 is the normal ratio, and a no is not a verdict on the company. The first round of investor feedback is usually polite fiction, because investors want to stay friendly and keep the next round open, so he has learned to pin people down until they say the real reason, on the logic that the faster the true feedback arrives, the faster the company can adapt.

Asked why this could not be built inside a bank, Englander gives two reasons. Startups have the luxury of learning by failing and can iterate inside regulated limits far faster, and culture cannot be retrofitted: you cannot turn a nine to five organisation into a nine to nine, seven day one, even in a subgroup, which is why acquirers buy AI teams and keep them separate. The same logic makes failed YC founders a hot commodity, with companies like Rippling and Stripe actively trying to acquire them. His pitch to anyone weighing a safe corporate offer is that joining or starting a startup is a reversible decision that teaches more in months than a career otherwise would, and even a failed founder comes back more employable than they left.

His advice to the London audience is simply to be more ambitious: Europe has the talent, he says, but its version of great is smaller than America's, as if founders feel they do not have the right to go for everything. Looking five years out, he expects humans to do less of the doing: judgment, relationships, and the value of a firm's internal data will rise sharply, while agents listen to email, Slack, and calls, build the to-do list, draft what they can, and handle exchanges like due diligence questions end to end instead of today's download, open, run, re-upload shuffle.

The audience questions fill in the operating detail. On safety, Model ML often deploys inside the customer's own infrastructure, so the customer controls what the system can connect to, and an agent harness logs every step from A to B, which he argues makes the work more traceable than a human's. On hiring, he says to optimise for culture over background in the early days, quoting a former HSBC chief executive's line that the highest performing teams operate like school and university friends. On quitting, his rule is to persevere but not blindly: if you honestly believe people want what you are building in a large market, persevere at all costs, and he calls perseverance the single top attribute of a great founder, noting his first company took two and a half years to find product market fit. Gustaf Alströmer's answer on coping with pressure is to stay off social media, and Englander agrees competition should be framed as proof a market exists and as fuel, like a sports team, rather than an obsession.

Open questions

  1. If performance keeps rotating among Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and open source models, what durable advantage does a model-agnostic harness company keep once every bank can switch models at will?
  2. Englander says more AI use currently means more money for his customers, at least for now: what happens to Model ML's pricing and to junior banking careers when that arithmetic breaks and the industry restructures?
  3. Can a culture built on nine to nine, seven day urgency and a healthy dose of fear survive at 200 people across eight offices, and what replaces fear as the motivator once the existential phase passes?
