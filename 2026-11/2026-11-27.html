Big Think · Brian Greene on black holes, string theory, and the limits of the human mind

Physics is approaching the limits of the human mind | Brian Greene: Full Interview

On Big Think, "Physics is approaching the limits of the human mind | Brian Greene: Full Interview" is a two hour solo talk by Brian Greene, the Columbia University physicist and co-founder of the World Science Festival, whose books include The Elegant Universe and Until the End of Time. Speaking alone to the camera, he works through five subjects in turn: the mysteries of black holes, whether string theory is dead, the possibility of extra dimensions of space, free will and consciousness, and whether the laws of physics are invented or discovered. Running underneath all five is the question in the title, whether human minds are nearing the edge of what they can understand about the universe.

He opens with black holes, and with a correction. The popular image of a black hole as a monster that reaches out and pulls in everything around it is wrong, he says: a black hole and a planet of the same mass exert exactly the same gravitational pull at the same distance. What makes a black hole different is that its mass is concentrated in a far smaller region, so you can get much closer to its center, and past the boundary called the event horizon nothing, not even light, can escape. The idea was already hiding in Einstein's equations in 1915. Karl Schwarzschild, serving at the Russian front in the First World War, solved those equations before Einstein himself had, and his solution showed that a star crushed small enough would warp space so deeply that anything falling in could never climb back out. Einstein did not believe it, and wrote papers in the 1930s and 1940s arguing that black holes could never exist in the real world. Direct observation has since settled the matter: they are part of our universe.

The first deep mystery he takes up is entropy, the measure of disorder that the second law of thermodynamics says always increases. In the 1970s John Wheeler posed a puzzle to his graduate student Jacob Bekenstein: if you throw a hot cup of tea, full of disorder, into a black hole, the disorder seems to vanish from the universe forever, and the second law appears broken. Bekenstein's answer was that the entropy is not gone. It stays in the black hole, whose event horizon grows slightly larger as it absorbs the tea, and he showed that a black hole's entropy is nothing but the surface area of that horizon, in the right units. That had a strange consequence: anything with entropy must have a temperature, and anything with a temperature glows, so Bekenstein was in effect claiming that black holes glow, a claim he himself hedged on.

Stephen Hawking took the claim seriously, initially hoping to prove Bekenstein wrong. Instead, after bringing quantum physics into conversation with general relativity, he showed that black holes really do radiate. The picture Greene gives is of particle pairs appearing in the restless quantum fluctuations just outside the horizon: the black hole can tear a pair apart, one partner falling in while the other escapes to space, so energy slowly leaks away. A black hole with the mass of our sun would take some 10 to the 67th power years to evaporate this way, but given long enough it shrinks to nothing.

That evaporation creates what Greene calls a real problem. Hawking radiation, on Hawking's own account, carries no information: gather all of it after the black hole is gone and you could never reconstruct what fell in. Yet the laws of physics insist that information is never destroyed, that knowing the present should in principle let you retrodict the past. One proposed rescue is holography, the idea that as an object crosses the horizon its information is smeared out on the surface, the way a hologram stores a three dimensional image on a thin sheet. If so, the outgoing radiation can carry that information away, and someone who collected every particle the black hole ever emitted could in principle extract even the complete works of Shakespeare from it. Greene is candid that no observation we can imagine will test this directly, because the timescales are trillions of trillions of years, but he argues the question matters anyway: black holes are the heaviest sledgehammer physicists can swing at their own laws, and whether the laws survive the pounding decides whether our understanding is on the right track.

The second chapter asks whether string theory is dead, and Greene begins by refusing the role of its promoter. He is sometimes held up as the poster boy for the theory, he says, but what he cares about is marching toward truth, and if string theory failed he would drop it in an instant. Its claim on his loyalty is that it resolves a genuine scandal: general relativity, which describes the big things, and quantum mechanics, which describes the small things, are mutually incompatible, and putting their equations together yields infinity as the answer to almost any question. String theory posits that the fundamental ingredients of the world are not pointlike dots but tiny vibrating filaments, open or closed, whose different vibrations are the different particles. Study the quantum mechanics of such a string and, remarkably, general relativity falls out of the mathematics: the theory contains a quantum description of gravity, which is exactly what is needed to understand the center of a black hole or the moment of the big bang. The strings themselves would live at the Planck scale, about 10 to the minus 33 centimeters. Magnify a single atom to the size of the observable universe, Greene says, and a string under the same magnification would be roughly the size of an average tree.

The experimental column, he admits, is empty where it matters most. As a graduate student in the 1980s he hoped that by now string theory would have made predictions that experiment could confirm or refute. Many pinned their hopes on supersymmetry, the idea that every known particle has a heavier partner: a selectron for the electron, squarks for the quarks. The Large Hadron Collider has found none of them. That is disappointing, Greene says, but it does not rule the theory out, because the mathematics nowhere predicts how massive the partner particles must be; they may simply be heavier than the collider can create. He would be thrilled, he insists, if the theory were ruled out, since that too would be a step toward truth.

The column of successes, though, is long. Beyond uniting gravity and quantum mechanics in one coherent formalism, string theory let Andrew Strominger and Cumrun Vafa count the microscopic arrangements behind a black hole's entropy and land exactly on the value Bekenstein and Hawking had conjectured. The unified picture also turns out to contain, in broad brush strokes, the ingredients particle physicists had won over decades, gauge symmetry, the Higgs mechanism, anomaly cancellation, the generation structure of particles, none of which had to be there. And in pure mathematics, mirror symmetry, an idea Greene named in a 1989 paper with Ronen Plesser, uses string theory to trade a hard question about one shape for an answerable question about a completely different looking shape, giving mathematicians a tool for problems that had stumped them. Holography, born in black hole physics and developed inside string theory by Juan Maldacena and others, is now offering insight into what space itself may be made of.

So why do critics call the theory dead? Greene says the charge always reduces to one point, that string theory has yet to make a definitive testable prediction confirmed by experiment, and he concedes he has said the same himself in his books and interviews. His difference with the critics is over the standard of judgment. He does not judge an idea only by confirmed predictions: he also weighs whether it advances understanding of deep questions, and on that score string theory has delivered for forty or fifty years. That the field's best minds, Edward Witten, Leonard Susskind, Steven Shenker, Tom Banks and many others, remain captivated by its richness after all that time is itself, he suggests, evidence of how deep the structure runs.

The third chapter turns to extra dimensions. String theory's mathematics does not merely allow them; it requires six, or depending on the formulation seven, dimensions beyond the three of everyday experience. The oldest explanation for why we do not see them is that dimensions come in two flavors, large ones like ours and ones curled up so small they escape detection. Greene's image is a garden hose seen from far away: it looks like a one dimensional line, but up close its surface has a second, circular dimension wrapped around it. The curled up dimensions would be everywhere, attached to every point of the familiar three, just far too tiny for even our best instruments. A second possibility is that we live on a three dimensional slice, a brane, and that light and nearly all particles are trapped on our slice while other slices sit unseen nearby; only the graviton, the particle of gravity, might travel off it. The idea has a long pedigree: in the early 1900s Kaluza and Klein found that one extra dimension seemed to unify Einstein's gravity with Maxwell's electromagnetism, though the details failed against observation, and Einstein himself explored it. String theory revived the idea by requiring the dimensions rather than positing them, and Greene's own thesis worked out how one such set of dimensions might curl up.

Greene also draws out two of the stranger consequences his own work has touched. If our universe is a brane moving through an extra circular dimension, a signal sent off the brane and around that dimension could, thanks to relativity, reach a distant alien civilization before a light signal sent directly, and a reply could come back in virtually no time, making a real time conversation across the galaxy conceivable, under two very large ifs. And where Einstein's theory forbids the fabric of space from ripping, Greene and his colleagues found that in string theory space can tear and repair itself, its basic shape changing in a topology changing process, with the strings wrapping around the rip and protecting the rest of the universe from catastrophe, all perfectly smooth from the standpoint of physics. He also pauses on why Einstein never found string theory: Einstein was no great fan of quantum mechanics and wanted to move past it rather than marry it to gravity, and in any case new ideas tend to come from young people unburdened by an earlier age's intuitions.

The fourth chapter takes up free will and consciousness, which Greene treats as one question. If consciousness is nothing but the laws of physics emerging through neurological processes, then the feeling of choosing is not an independent force in the world. When he lifts his arm, he says, fond of thinking he chose to, the motion is nothing but electrical signals and contracting muscle fibers, particles obeying physical law at every step, and none of us has the power to intercede in that lawful unfolding. Beneath that sits the hard problem of consciousness: the particles in our heads, electrons, quarks, protons, neutrons, are not believed to have any inner world at all, so how can a large aggregate of them light up with thoughts, sensations and emotions? One proposal, from the philosopher David Chalmers, bites the bullet and suggests that particles may carry a tiny proto-consciousness of their own, which melds together in large aggregates into full experience. Greene's own guess is more conservative: consciousness is likely an emergent byproduct of a certain kind of information processing, the way temperature emerges from the average motion of particles, and on that view an artificial system that processed information the way the brain does could in principle have an inner world too. Full disclosure, he adds: he does not think any current AI system is conscious, but he allows the possibility over ten years or a thousand.

If consciousness is physical, he argues, free will is an illusion, and quantum mechanics offers no escape hatch. Quantum randomness loosens determinism, but we have no power to choose which of the possible outcomes occurs; a coin flip that decides tomorrow is not something your will controls. Greene still lives as if he had free will, and calls it a useful fiction, but he draws practical consequences from letting it go. Regret softens: when something goes wrong he tells himself it is unfortunate that his particles chose left instead of right, since he could not have done otherwise. Credit softens the same way when an equation finally yields. And responsibility survives, properly understood: a person whose particles were part of the causal chain that produced a bad outcome is responsible in that sense, and society should still respond, but to shape future behavior rather than to exact retribution, the way a robot vacuum updates its navigation after bumping the sofa, without anyone supposing the vacuum has free will. He allows that his whole picture could be wrong, that consciousness might be a fundamental field our brains tap into, but he sees no evidence for that, and he prefers to stick with what can be tested, mathematized and confirmed.

The final chapter asks whether the laws of physics are invented or discovered, and Greene, who as a young physicist assumed physicists were tapping into deep truths of reality, has changed his mind: we invent the laws, and mathematics with them, as a language for summarizing the patterns reality shows us. There is no Platonic realm of perfect triangles somewhere out there, he argues; the universe does have reliable regularities, and the laws we write are our best articulation of them. Those laws have curious qualities. They do not care what we want, they seem to operate universally without exception, and they make no distinction between past and future, treating time symmetrically even though human experience is anything but. The arrow of time, on the best current answer, comes not from the laws but from the universe's initial condition: it began in a highly ordered state and has been running down ever since, entropy rising, which is why eggs splatter and never unsplatter. History counsels humility about our current laws: Newton's laws were superb for the everyday world and failed at the very small and the very big, and Greene considers it incredibly unlikely that the cutting edge laws of the twenty first century are the final ones. By the twenty fourth or twenty fifth century, he expects, physicists will not be relying on today's understanding.

Mathematics itself, he argues, bears our fingerprints. Counting plausibly began with tallying members of a group on the African savannah, and base ten arithmetic reflects the ten fingers we happen to have. His deeper view is that mathematics grows out of the human propensity for metaphor: symbols like x, y and z are marks on paper that stand for points in the world, and the whole discipline is a systematic metaphorical system we invented, which makes its success at describing the moon's orbit and a baseball's flight all the more remarkable, and all the more a pale approximation, perhaps, of whatever the true deep understanding would be. Could AI become the tool that goes beyond the limits of an embodied being? Possibly, he says, but today's systems are themselves embodied in a sense, trained on the corpus of human information; only if they shed that reliance might they launch somewhere humans never could, an outcome he calls possible, exciting and frightening.

That brings the talk back to its title. Greene thinks it quite possible that human intelligence has hard limits, the way his own intelligent dog, which can open doors with lever handles, will in all likelihood never understand general relativity. We may work on some problems for five years, a hundred years, a thousand years, and make no progress, and that would simply be a limitation of our biological makeup. Some mysteries already push us into strange places: the quantum measurement problem, in which a particle can be here and there in the mathematics yet is only ever found here or there when measured, led Hugh Everett III in 1957 to take the equations at face value and conclude that both outcomes happen, in different universes, an idea that sounds wild in English but is, Greene says, the most straightforward reading of the mathematics. Other mysteries may have no deeper answer at all: nobody knows why the fundamental particles have the particular masses they do, and perhaps that is just how it is. He closes by resisting the phrase theory of everything. Such a theory would unify the fundamental particles and forces in a single master equation, but it would not explain how the human brain works or what you will have for dinner next Tuesday; the macroscopic world must be described in its own languages, neuroscience for the mind, everyday terms for a baseball in flight. And if we ever meet aliens, their understanding may be radically different from ours, shaped by a different evolutionary history and different senses, so different that no meeting of the minds may be possible. We are, he concludes, a remarkable pattern of particles, sculpted by evolution, made of the same stuff as the planets and the stars, and he finds that connection humbling and gratifying rather than diminishing.

Open questions

  1. Greene argues that holography could in principle preserve information through a black hole's evaporation, yet concedes no imaginable observation could test it on any human timescale. If a claim is in principle checkable but in practice sealed off for trillions of years, what distinguishes it from a claim that is not checkable at all?
  2. He judges string theory by its successes in unifying ideas and generating mathematics, not only by confirmed predictions, while also insisting he would drop it the moment it failed. What would failure actually look like for a framework whose defenders can always say its particles are too heavy to see and its development is unfinished?
  3. Greene holds both that free will is an illusion produced by particles obeying physical law and that people should still be held responsible, with punishment aimed at shaping behavior. Does that forward looking account of responsibility quietly smuggle back the agency his physics argument denies, or is it genuinely consistent?

AI News & Strategy Daily | Nate B Jones · Nate B. Jones on getting faster at the wrong job, and four steps to a better use for AI

Is AI Just Making You Faster At A Job You Never Wanted? 4 Steps To Find A Better Use For It.

On AI News & Strategy Daily | Nate B Jones, "Is AI Just Making You Faster At A Job You Never Wanted? 4 Steps To Find A Better Use For It." is a solo talk by Nate B. Jones aimed at a pattern he sees everywhere: people are using AI to get very fast at work they never wanted in the first place, producing faster emails, faster decks and more small projects inside the same job. Against the common advice to simply be more ambitious, he asks, ambitious about what, and lays out four steps for finding a better use for the technology: find people whose work opens a door for you, try a piece of that work yourself, find people to learn alongside, and read what the experience tells you so the learning loop stays alive.

The first step starts with Riley Brown, a creator who makes videos about building with AI. Brown began in Discord groups where people were experimenting with AI images and early language models, and making videos gave him a reason to keep exploring. That started a feedback loop: he began building apps despite never having been an engineer, collaborated with people he met on X, and over months of experimenting in public, a flywheel of collaboration took hold. Jones pulls two lessons from the story. Brown could see other people experimenting and find a way to connect with those experiments, and his own experiments were public enough, even apart from his channel, that others could see where to help him. The practical version for viewers is to look one step beyond any video that catches their attention: ask who made the thing being demoed, who is involved in the story, and who is asking questions in the replies that draw good answers. He cites the writer swyx on following the people behind useful work, and the people they learn from, until you reach the discussions where the work is still evolving.

The second step is to try a piece of the work, starting from whatever already holds your attention. Jones builds a deliberately made up example: an operations worker who uses AI for email and spreadsheets and who, outside work, is the relative who listens to older family members tell stories and wonders over old photographs. He gave that description to an AI and asked for three activities that would build real skills without demanding a career change, and the answer offered recording a relative's memories in their own words, investigating when and where old photos were taken, and reconstructing the history of a place that mattered to the family. Each option is a different kind of work, he notes, and each produces something tangible. Underneath, the hobby trains the same meta skills as serious work: reasoning, visual information, voice, pattern finding, and search and retrieval across the internet.

The heart of that example is learning to push back. Jones used AI to find outside resources, including StoryCorps guidance for recording conversations and guides to old maps, then used those principles to prepare his own question list instead of accepting the AI's line of inquiry. He calls this refusing to cognitively surrender: the AI tends to stick to the middle of the road, offering a fine generic question such as what a normal day looked like when you were 25, while the questions he actually wanted were about a particular time and place. That skill, insisting on specifics, transfers directly back to operations, product and customer work. He also warns against stopping after one attempt: transcribe the conversation, find the passages worth digging into, and let curiosity carry the project forward, the same way a single shared example, his own Open Brain system for capturing and refinding information, seeded a wave of viewers adapting it to their own problems and improving it together.

The third step is finding people to learn with, and the example is swyx himself, whose real name is Sean Wang. In 2018 Wang watched a livestream talk by Dan Abramov, a developer on the React interface tool, worked through the demonstration, and published his walkthrough the next day, which pulled people in and built his connections in the community. The detail Jones underlines is freshness: choose something someone has just put out, a new demonstration or a first version, because it is top of mind and far more likely to start conversations than work that has been settled for a decade. In AI, he argues, nothing is ten or fifteen years old, so a beginner who has genuinely tried a small piece of the work and followed the thread to where experienced people are wrestling with it, his example is the live public debate over formal verification of software, can have an opinion that matters. He also recommends making one small, useful resource, a guide, an example or a comparison your group keeps needing, and keeping it honest about what you tried and did not know, so it keeps working for the next person without you having to announce yourself again and again.

The fourth step is reading the experience for what it says about your next move, and Jones insists that whether the project succeeded is the wrong marker. A project can work and still teach you mostly what you do not want, or fail and hand you a skill or a person who carries you forward. He tells the story of Simone Giertz, the inventor known for her deliberately terrible robots, who discovered at advertising school that she loved building and programming, yet found that building alone was not enough until she changed her setting and everything clicked: she had to learn both an activity and the context she wanted to do it in. Part of this step, he says, cannot be delegated to AI at all. AI can prepare examples, but only you can learn whether you like storytelling, or writing, or making something come alive for another person, by checking with your gut. Other people's responses are data too: Ben Tossell built roughly fifty products with no-code tools that nobody bought, but people kept asking how he built them, and that pull led him to teaching and then to building MakerPad.

He closes with practical counsel. Pick one piece of work you actually want to investigate, at work or at home, try something you understand well, bring the AI elements in on purpose, keep track of what happens, and grow it deliberately while connecting with others. When one direction starts to sing, lean in and get good enough at it that it becomes a real option, but keep your existing income as long as you can until the new thing can pay. AI, in his framing, is a choice enabler that rarely looks like one, because the blank text box seems to demand something of us instead. His closing ask is small: pick one thing to try this week, not ten, and report back what you found.

Open questions

  1. Jones argues the pushback skill, refusing the AI's generic middle of the road answer and insisting on specifics, is the core meta skill of the AI era. Can that skill actually be taught directly, or does it only develop as a byproduct of caring about a particular piece of work?
  2. The method leans heavily on public, fast moving communities, above all X, for finding people and live debates. What is the equivalent path for people whose fields have no such public square, or who cannot build in public because their work is confidential?
  3. He advises keeping your existing income until the new direction can pay, yet also says a direction only becomes a real option once you lean in seriously. How do you tell the difference between prudent patience and never quite committing?

MTS · Scott Kominers on the human-AI centaur and the Riemann hypothesis claim

The Riemann Hypothesis Was Solved by a Human-AI Centaur | Scott Kominers

On MTS, "The Riemann Hypothesis Was Solved by a Human-AI Centaur | Scott Kominers" is a conversation with Scott Kominers, a Harvard Business School professor and a research partner at a16z Frontier who works on AI economics and AI in mathematics, and who has also spent more than twenty years solving and designing enigmatic puzzles. The conversation runs from what generative AI has done to puzzle design to the reported proof of the Riemann hypothesis, and one idea ties the two halves together: the interesting unit is neither the human nor the model alone, but the two working as a single system.

An enigmatic puzzle, as Kominers describes it, gives you none of the framework a crossword or a Sudoku gives you. You receive some information, perhaps numbers, perhaps a thousand page PDF that looks blank, and a big part of the puzzle is figuring out what you are even supposed to do with it. He compares it to an escape room whose walls are invisible: you discover the logic by exploring the boundaries, and the designer's craft lies in building invisible guard rails that keep solvers from wandering too far down rabbit holes.

Generative AI has shifted that craft from both directions. Models have trained on major competitions in this style, such as the MIT Mystery Hunt, whose puzzles sit online, and they are good at the recursive logic solving requires. Designers can now build grunt work into a solution, a long computer program or even a mathematical proof in the middle of a puzzle, because a solver who figures out what is being asked can hand the labor to a tool. That expands the design space enormously, he says, while also forcing designers to keep their puzzles resting on ingenuity rather than on brute force recursion that a model can simply grind through.

Where humans still beat models, in his account, is the sideways leap. A model tends to get obsessed with the micro details in front of it and to miss a hidden logic that sits outside its natural scope of view, such as a puzzle that has to wrap physically around a sculpture before its text makes sense. Standard tropes are no longer safe either: hide letters inside an image and a model may spot the steganography at once and announce that the answer is sandwich. He also points to sequential discovery puzzles, physical boxes where a key turns out to be magnetic and has to be slid around, and to the kind of context a life supplies. Humans, he says, are very good at looking at what they have been given and guessing that they are supposed to do something with it.

The same tools have sped up the designing as much as the solving. His example is a puzzle he built a year ago in which an image encoded an entire HTML file in its color structure, so that chopping the image up in the right way produced a working web page, and the encoded text had to fit an exact number of characters to cover the grid of pixels. That would once have taken a week to prototype. He woke up with the idea at two in the morning and had a working version in about an hour.

Mathematics, he argues, shows the mirror image of the puzzle problem. Models are unbelievably good at spotting the tool you had not thought to throw at a problem, because they search in embedding space without the boundaries of any one person's training: a model might point out that theta functions can be modular forms even if you never learned that. Math is a well behaved topology for this, since things that sit near each other in embedding space often really are useful to each other. Puzzle design is built from the opposite kind of move, a broad lateral jump to another logic entirely, and so far humans remain better at those.

The second half turns to the Riemann hypothesis, and to the claim, reported days after an a16z Frontier video on AI doing math, that the mathematician Levent Alpoge has proved it with AI assistance. Kominers offers two clarifications. First, it was not solved by AI: Levent plus an AI solved it, a human sitting in a mobile suit like a Gundam pilot, whose mathematical ingenuity the AI expanded, letting him make an incredibly complicated walk around all of number theory and arithmetic geometry. Second, in his words, it is not done: there are many more layers to what the Riemann hypothesis is, and mathematicians are only starting to understand the structure of the reported proof.

The surprise, for the experts he knows, is the shape of that structure. The proof was widely expected to require completely novel types of math, things not yet even imagined, as if the final boss of a game sat two hundred dungeons away and the field had cleared seven. What is emerging instead, he says, looks like a very complicated crosswalk, or hopscotch: the proof moves in and out of different branches of math, assembling tools from dungeons one through seven, where by seven he really means seven hundred, in an intricate array, with genuinely new pieces built along the way. One description he repeats is that it is proved by everything, using nearly every piece of math along any path related to it, in a carefully orchestrated sequence, like a frame perfect tool assisted speed run. He sees the same pattern in other recent AI assisted results: existing pieces combined in ways nobody had contemplated, so the ideas turn out to be more grounded in existing mathematics than anyone realized.

Could basically anyone with this level of model have done it? Kominers says no. He has known Alpoge for years, and singles out his extraordinary talent for seeing big abstract connections between entire areas of math. The models accelerated and expanded that vision beyond what anyone had imagined trying, but it still took the human's abstract sense of how the pieces could fit together, and his probing of the models, to make the assemblage. The hosts call it a true centaur discovery, and Kominers agrees, while noting that results now span a range: some are centaurs, some are model only, and some are human only with the model serving as a computational tool.

Open questions

  1. If the reported proof really assembles existing tools rather than inventing wholly new mathematics, should mathematicians expect the other great open problems to fall the same way, or is the Riemann hypothesis special because so many branches of math touch it?
  2. Kominers says models excel where nearness in embedding space is a good hint and lag where progress needs a lateral leap. How would anyone tell whether that gap is genuinely closing, rather than the models simply getting faster at adjacent moves?
  3. If the decisive skill in centaur mathematics is the human's abstract vision for how distant pieces fit together, how should mathematicians train for it, and how would a field evaluate a skill like that?

AI Engineer · Michelle Chen on how Cloudflare became a neolab

Why Cloudflare Became a Neolab — Michelle Chen, Cloudflare

On AI Engineer, "Why Cloudflare Became a Neolab — Michelle Chen, Cloudflare" is a Code Summit talk by Michelle Chen, a group product manager at Cloudflare who works on products including Workers AI, AI Search, and AI Gateway. She frames the talk with a line from Kierkegaard, that life can only be understood backwards but must be lived forwards, and uses it to trace how a sixteen-year-old content delivery company ended up training its own AI models.

Looking backwards, Chen starts with the network itself. Cloudflare began in 2010 as a CDN, and instead of centralizing compute in a couple of big regions the way traditional hyperscalers do, it built data centers all around the world, as close to users as possible. Once those machines existed, the company asked what else people could build on them. Workers arrived in 2017 as serverless functions that start in milliseconds and vanish when the code finishes, followed by the more stateful Durable Objects and, in 2025, containers for workloads that need something closer to a virtual machine.

Storage came next, with KV, the R2 object store, and the D1 database, so developers could build full, stateful applications entirely on Cloudflare. Then 2023 changed the picture. As ChatGPT took off, Cloudflare launched Workers AI and added GPUs across its network, again at the edge rather than in central regions. The bet behind it was that models would get smaller over time, which would make network latency the thing that mattered most.

The opposite happened. Models grew from the seven-billion-parameter scale that once counted as frontier to trillion-parameter systems, and latency mattered less and less. Chen is candid that the original hypothesis was wrong, and Cloudflare pivoted: in March 2026 it began serving Kimi, followed by GLM and DeepSeek, offering the giant models through a simple API call. With infrastructure, compute, storage, and large models in place, the company settled into an agent cloud story, with agents deployed, powered, and fed with data on Cloudflare. But, as Chen tells it, the story never felt quite right. One piece was still missing.

The missing piece arrived in September 2026, when a model called Jev introduced what Chen calls system one models. Where a large language model generates tokens autoregressively and can take minutes to reason through a decision, a system one model takes a single forward pass over its inputs and produces an output a program can act on. The two kinds of model are meant to work in tandem, with the small, fast models taking routine work off the large ones. Suddenly Cloudflare's original Workers AI hypothesis looked right after all, just early: small, fast models make network latency matter again.

There was one problem. Jev was closed source, so Cloudflare could not host it. The company built its own version instead. Experiments began on September 18, three days after Jev launched, and after a week of deliberation the team committed on a Friday evening, trained the model over a single weekend, and launched it the following week. The model, called Clef, gives agents a way to make decisions programmatically, without waiting on minutes of language-model reasoning and without waking a human whose sleep schedule would otherwise gate a round-the-clock agent.

Three weeks later came Cleric, a second new model type that Cloudflare calls an extraction model. Cleric takes unstructured input, an email, a message, a web page, even an image, and returns clean, typed values that software can use directly. In all, Cloudflare shipped four models in three weeks. Chen's point is that becoming a neolab removed a dependency: when a capability the platform needs is closed, unavailable, or risky to rely on, Cloudflare can now build it.

To show what this looks like in practice, Chen demos a coding agent triaging a failed CI build. The conventional setup sends the agent's forty available tools back and forth to a large model across repeated calls, dragging the same context along each time. In the system one version, Clef Flash first prunes the tool list from forty down to ten, Cleric extracts the pull request number and then pulls the exact error out of hundreds of lines of logs, and the large model is called once, at the end, to act. In her measured run away from conference Wi-Fi, the workflow finished in 9.2 seconds against 17.2 for the model-only version, cost 72 percent less, and used about 94 percent fewer large-model tokens, one call instead of five.

Other builders are already using the pair for routing decisions, spam and phishing calls, and extraction from web pages and support tickets, and a fuller Cleric launch is on the way. Chen closes by returning to the forward-looking half of her opening frame: the industry changes too fast for any company to simply defend its first hypothesis, so the real skill is reacting well when the wind shifts. She leaves the room with a Tolstoy line about the present being the only time anyone has power, and a question: what are you going to build today?

Open questions

  1. If system one models take over routine decisions and extraction, how much of a typical agent workload is actually left for large reasoning models, and does that change what the big labs should optimize for?
  2. Cloudflare trained Clef in a single weekend out of necessity. Can a model built that quickly hold up in production decision-making, and how would anyone verify that it does?
  3. Chen presents building in-house models as protection against closed-source and regulatory risk. At what point does that insurance become a distraction from the platform business it is meant to protect?

Semi Doped · Nir Tasher on NOR flash memory and embedded security

Why NOR Flash Memory Is the Attacker’s Holy Grail

On Semi Doped, "Why NOR Flash Memory Is the Attacker’s Holy Grail" is a sponsored conversation between host Austin Lyons and Nir Tasher, the CTO of Winbond Israel. Winbond is the world's largest NOR flash vendor by shipments, and the episode is paid for by the company, so its claims are a vendor's case. That case is still a striking one: the small memory chip that holds a device's boot code and credentials is, in Tasher's telling, the single most attractive target in modern hardware, because code planted there survives power cycles and is almost impossible to detect or remove.

Flash, he explains, is the nonvolatile memory inside nearly every connected device, from earbuds and refrigerators to cars and data center servers. Unlike RAM, which empties when the power goes off, flash keeps the initial code a system runs when it starts, and often the credentials it needs, such as Wi-Fi passwords and serial numbers. Everything a device does begins from that code, so the whole system depends on trusting it. Winbond sells billions of these devices a year.

That persistence is exactly what an attacker wants. Tasher says the holy grail for most attackers is a dormant and resilient attack, one that stays in the system through power cycles and is nearly impossible to remove. Real attacks are almost never the movie version, someone slipping a USB stick into a machine. They are mounted remotely, from halfway across the world, and after all that effort an attacker does not want a reboot to wipe the work away. The payload placed in flash is usually small, often just something that opens a port and leaves a door ajar for whoever knows to look. Antivirus software does not run on televisions, refrigerators, routers or street lights, so such an implant is hard to detect and harder to remove: a compromised fleet of street lamps or servers could mean sending a technician to reflash every single unit by hand.

AI has sharpened the threat, in his account. Writing malicious code used to demand rare expertise; LLMs and agentic coding tools have lowered that barrier, and AI based attacks find creative ways around software protections, because no piece of software is perfect. He points to a report from Google the previous week describing a fully autonomous AI based cyber attack, designed and carried out by a machine without human supervision. Billions of devices already deployed carry old code in their flash and cannot be retrofitted, so the defense he proposes is aimed at future devices.

Winbond's answer is to move the protection into the flash silicon itself. Its secure flash carries security logic built into the same single die as the storage, rather than a separate controller bundled into the package. Any attempt to modify the flash contents must come as an authenticated command, signed with a strong cryptographic key; without the key, the content cannot be changed, even remotely. Failed attempts are reported to the host system, repeated failures lock the device, and if the contents are ever found altered or corrupted, the company's philosophy is to stop the system and call for human intervention rather than let it run on possibly compromised code.

Firmware updates get the same treatment. The flash checks that update code is genuinely signed by the owner of the code and that it is newer than what is installed, which blocks downgrade attacks. Tasher ties the urgency to regulation: the European Union's Cyber Resilience Act becomes a mandatory requirement starting in 2027, and it obliges systems that process data to be updatable when vulnerabilities surface.

He gives two reasons the protection has to be hardware, and monolithic hardware at that. Protecting software with more software, he argues, is fighting fire with fire, since the protective code would carry the same human fallibility as the code it guards, whereas hardware can only do what it was designed to do and its behavior can be checked across nearly every combination. A multi-die package, by contrast, can be opened up: an attacker can melt the plastic, probe the signals between the dies, and extract secrets, then reuse what they learn against every similar system. A single die raises that physical attack to something closer to a state actor's budget. The same reuse logic runs through his whole argument: the goal is to make attacking each individual device its own hard problem, so that one success does not unlock a million identical devices.

Supply chain attacks worry him most of all. Security research groups, he says, rank them the top threat, ahead of AI based attacks, because no amount of supervision fully controls a chain of human beings. He recalls reports from the mid-2010s of an extra, unauthorized chip found on server boards from a major maker, sitting between the flash and the processor, and notes that a flash chip is among the easiest components to counterfeit: it is far simpler than cloning a CPU or GPU, the packaging and markings can be copied, and a swapped shipment can be arranged with a small bribe somewhere along the route. Winbond's countermeasure is that each secure flash device can authenticate itself cryptographically at any point in its life, proving it is a genuine Winbond part with a known serial number and that the code inside it has not been modified, so a substituted device can be identified immediately.

Quantum computing, he says, does not undo any of this, because the company deliberately avoided classical algorithms such as RSA and elliptic curve cryptography, whose hardness rests on factoring problems that quantum machines are expected to solve quickly. The signing scheme used for updates and device authentication is LMS, a hash based signature method in the Leighton-Micali family, which does not rely on those problems and is considered resistant to quantum attacks.

His closing pitch is practical. The secure flash is a drop-in replacement for standard NOR flash, so a maker of EV chargers, appliances or IoT devices can move from no hardware security to nearly full hardware security by swapping one component, with only modest software changes to use the features. Makers facing Cyber Resilience Act certification, he suggests, should get security consultancy, ideally from someone unbiased, and he is candid that Winbond itself is biased toward its own devices.

Open questions

  1. Tasher argues that hardware protection can be verified across nearly all combinations while software never can. How far does that confidence extend once the surrounding system, its update servers and its signing keys are counted as part of the attack surface?
  2. If supply chain substitution is the top threat and a bribed driver can defeat physical supervision, how much of the protection rests on customers actually interrogating devices for authenticity at every stage, and what happens where they do not?
  3. Secure flash protects future devices but cannot be retrofitted into the billions of vulnerable devices already deployed. What, if anything, defends that installed base as autonomous AI attacks mature?
