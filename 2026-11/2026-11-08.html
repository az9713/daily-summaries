Lightspeed India and Southeast Asia · Benedict Evans on how big AI will become

Benedict Evans on Why Nobody Knows How Big AI Will Become | Masters by Lightspeed

On Lightspeed India and Southeast Asia, the first episode of Masters by Lightspeed, "Benedict Evans on Why Nobody Knows How Big AI Will Become", is a conversation between Hemant Mohapatra, a partner at Lightspeed, and Benedict Evans, the independent analyst who has spent twenty five years studying how technology platforms shift, from the PC to the internet to mobile. The question running through it is what those earlier shifts can tell us about AI. Evans's answer, in short, is that they can tell us what the early phase feels like, and almost nothing about how it ends.

Every platform shift, he says, passes through a period where the technology does not quite work but visibly will. The Homebrew hobbyists went from the Apple 1 circuit board to millions of Apple 2s while ordinary users still crawled under desks to reboot frozen PCs and lost work to corrupted floppy disks. The early web ran on narrowband connections and browsers that could not do obvious things. His own attempt to browse the web through a WAP transcoding system, a Palm Pilot and an infrared link to a Nokia phone ran at perhaps twenty kilobits a second, and getting one page to load felt like the future arriving. AI today, he argues, sits in that same phase: agents that clean up an inbox by deleting it, elaborate prompting rituals, and token bill shock, which he compares to the mobile data bill shock of around 2010, when flat rate plans met usage twenty times higher than expected and carriers had to scramble. Most of what normal people will need has not been built yet, and nobody knows what the market structure will be.

What we do know, he says, is that the eventual winners are invisible at this stage. While the browser wars raged, Mark Zuckerberg was in junior high, Larry Page and Sergey Brin were graduate students, and Jeff Bezos was a man in a baggy sweater saying "I sell books". Everyone in 1998 knew people would shop online. Nobody had any conception of Facebook. People complain that Evans the analyst will not make a call, and his reply is that the call is that there are twenty possibilities, and the outcome will be one nobody is currently thinking about.

The adoption numbers make the same point from the other side. ChatGPT has some nine hundred million weekly users, but most of them use it every few days or less, and for that majority the models are interchangeable. Swap ChatGPT for Gemini and a casual user would not notice any difference, the way people in street interviews could not tell last year's iPhone from the new one. The valley habit of living inside the tools, laptops held half open so an agent keeps running, is the modern equivalent of sitting on bulletin boards with a 9.6k modem in 1994: thrilling, real, and not how everyone else lives.

That interchangeability is why Evans expects the model layer to commoditize. The models leapfrog each other across a jagged frontier, good at different things in different ways, and nothing yet gives one of them a durable lead. There is no equivalent of Google being two years ahead of Bing and always two years ahead, and no network effect holding a leader in place. Ask what would make one model sustainably and defensibly twenty five percent better than all the others, he says, and there is no answer. Models, in that respect, are like wheat or oil: you cannot tell one supplier's from another's. Mohapatra adds that commoditization may be necessary for value to move up the stack, with a few large players staking out territory and charging rent, and pricing following the path of cloud storage, where launches became price drops.

The deepest difference from every earlier shift, Evans argues, is that this time nobody knows the science. In 1995 you knew what a PC cost, how many existed, and what it would cost to pass a home with fiber, so you knew mass adoption could not happen next year. With AI, we do not know the physical limits: not how much better the models will get, nor how much cheaper. A paper next week might deliver today's results for a tenth of the compute, or a new use case might demand ten times more. The only certainty is that the current configuration, the pricing and the multiples, cannot be stable. Some problems have a terminal point, like dictation, which can simply work and then get cheaper forever. Others, like image recognition or cancer detection, are fractal and keep expanding. And "best" itself is undefined: it might mean far cleverer than a human, or half as clever and vastly faster.

He is equally blunt about the product. A chatbot, he says, is a terrible product and a terrible interface, and it would be a bad interface even if a superhuman intelligence sat behind it, indeed even if an actual person did. Booking a family holiday through a conversation, with a human agent throwing options onto a screen in reaction to your words, would be worse than a booking site whose interface embodies thirty years of thought about how people choose. Graphical interfaces exist for good reasons: they are scaffolding that helps you work out what you actually want, which is hard even with a brilliant intern. You cannot one shot a description of the task.

Mohapatra asks why a single model company would not simply absorb the whole stack, pointing to SpaceX building both the railroad to space and Starlink, and to Jio building fiber and consumer apps in India. Evans pushes back: platform owners always feel the pressure to move up the stack, and mobile operators and Microsoft both tried to provide everything. They succeed only when they can turn the new thing into what they already are. His test for Google was always whether it could turn an activity into a commodity SKU it could warehouse alongside all the others, almost literally an internet paperclip maximizer; when a business needed a custom supply chain, custom purchasing and physical stores, Google would not do it, grocery being the exception that proves the scale rule. Companies that step outside their nature fail at it, as Apple has with services and Google has with delightful interfaces. That, he says, is what the disruption literature was always about.

The same logic defuses the so called SaaS apocalypse and the idea that consultancies are finished. Writing the software, Evans says, was never the hard part: no venture capitalist in a pitch ever thought "but couldn't somebody else write all of this code?" The hard part is noticing that a problem exists at all, convincing an industry it has the problem, working out the right solution, and then the whole go to market grind of adoption, budgets and changed habits, in categories where ten predecessors may already have failed. Consulting has the same structure. Claude cannot really make the slides: it makes slides that look like Bain, BCG or McKinsey slides to someone who does not know what those are. The consultants themselves use Claude to make slides precisely because they know what the slides should look like. And the deck is not the product anyway. You hire Bain to work out that the problem is this and not that, and to ask the questions nobody inside the company can ask.

From there the conversation turns to jobs, and to his distinction between tasks and jobs. Occasionally a whole job is a button: the elevator attendant drove a lever driven car to your floor, a serious and dangerous job, and automation first reduced it to pressing a button and then removed the person entirely. Accounting is the counterexample. Accountants in the 1950s spent their days adding numbers, and the industry then spent a century automating accounting through punch cards, mainframes, databases and ERP, yet the number of accountants rose in a straight line the whole time. Partly that is the Jevons paradox: cheaper work can mean more of it. But mostly, he argues, the tasks migrated while the job and the function endured, and the census categories that seem to show tidy substitutions are misleading: a career can run from stock clerk to office machine operator to, in effect, Oracle operator, a title no census ever counted. He is scathing about confident forecasts from machine learning researchers, noting that Geoffrey Hinton's advice a decade ago to stop training radiologists was completely wrong, and that expertise in models confers no authority on economics. The hopeful sign he and Mohapatra note is younger workers concluding that AI lets them do a senior designer's or senior coder's job now, and ramping up fast.

Asked what the new things will be, Evans warns against forcing the old thing to fit the new tool. More code is not the endpoint; the endpoint is something only possible with the new thing. His consumer ladder runs from identifying a coat in a photograph, which machine learning could do before large language models, through being given twenty comparable coats at different prices and talked through the choice, to a system that studies your Instagram and proposes a coat to freshen your look. The enterprise version climbs from finding a piece of data mentioned on a call, to listening to six months of recorded calls and extracting the main customer concerns, to the top of the stack question: synthesize telemetry, Salesforce data, calls, pricing and billing, and suggest how to reprice to reduce churn. Whether a model sits at the bottom of the stack as a feature inside Zoom or Salesforce, or at the top asking questions that could not be asked before, is the open design question, and the probabilistic nature of the tool is the point rather than a defect. He compares the skeptics fixed on error rates to someone looking at an Apple 2 and objecting that no bank could use a machine without mainframe uptime. The recorded music industry's revenue curve makes his closing point: it fell by half when tracks no longer cost fifteen dollars each, then recovered to about three quarters of its peak when fifteen dollars a month bought all the music in the world. Streaming was not a cheaper CD. It was a different proposition.

Two or three years out, Evans hopes for real product market fit beyond software development, more consumer breakouts, and more abstractions built on top of the chatbot. The constraint he names is marginal cost: Instagram could reach fifty million users with no revenue, but tokens cost money, so AI products cannot grow free first and monetize later, and advertising only works once token prices fall far enough. He also expects the actual uses to keep surprising outsiders: Martin Sorrell argues AI's importance to advertising is in back office paperwork, billing and media planning rather than creative, and consultants are finding huge demand for AI run primary research, surveying twenty thousand customers quickly.

On the bubble question he refuses a date. Market timing is impossible, he says, citing the fund manager Tony Dye, who called the market overvalued from 1994 and was right only eventually, and Keynes's line that the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent. The comparison with 1995 to 1997 is a way of saying it is early and unclear, nothing more precise. Transformative shifts do tend to produce bubbles: electricity fed the boom before 1929, and smartphones arguably had theirs in advance, in the 3G bubble that coincided with the dot com one. The euphoric psychology is visible now, in the feeling he ventriloquizes: this is amazing, stop asking questions, let go, can't you feel the AGI. That feeling also accompanied PCs, the internet and mobile, and the internet did turn out to be kind of a big deal. The honest problem is that the bull case cannot be analyzed either way: nobody has a theory of why these models work, or of what human intelligence is, so percentages for doom or transcendence are vibes, his word, dressed as forecasts.

The end state, then, is a range wide enough to be almost useless, and he says so. Somewhere between three and ten companies might end up spending between two hundred billion and two trillion dollars a year. Those are large numbers but not impossible ones: oil and gas spends roughly a trillion dollars a year on capital expenditure, global telecoms about three hundred billion, mobile about two hundred billion, and what matters is whether a reasonable return can be attached. At one pole sit hundreds of models, many open, running on premises and on devices at no marginal cost to developers. At the other sit a few giant central systems carrying everything. Evans's own inclination is toward the former. His core claim is only that, unlike PCs, the internet and mobile, where the basic parameters of the possible were known, this time nobody knows how big AI will become.

Open questions

  1. If the model layer commoditizes the way Evans expects, where does durable value actually settle: with the few model platforms charging rent, with whoever owns distribution, or with the unglamorous problem finding and go to market work he says was always the hard part?
  2. Evans argues marginal cost is what blocks the free growth consumer playbook that built Instagram. If token prices fall far enough for advertising supported AI, does the consumer breakout follow, or does the chatbot interface itself remain the binding constraint?
  3. He says the range of end states, from a few central systems spending trillions to hundreds of cheap open models, cannot be narrowed because the underlying science is not understood. What evidence, in the next two or three years, would actually narrow it?

David Senra · Nikesh Arora on winning deals and growing Palo Alto Networks

How to Win Deals & Grow a $250B Business | Nikesh Arora, Palo Alto Networks

On David Senra, the episode "How to Win Deals & Grow a $250B Business | Nikesh Arora, Palo Alto Networks" is a long conversation with Nikesh Arora, the chairman and chief executive of Palo Alto Networks. Arora spent ten years at Google, where he ran Google Europe and then served as chief business officer while the company grew from about 2,000 to 60,000 employees. After two years as president of SoftBank, he took over Palo Alto Networks in 2018, when it was an $18 billion company built around a single firewall product. It now sells more than 70 products, and most of the conversation is about the acquisition system behind that expansion, and the temperament behind the system.

The episode opens with an angel investing circle Arora shared with Silver Lake's Egon Durban and Marc Andreessen, a group that later became a technology advisory board for William Morris Endeavor after Silver Lake acquired it. It also produced a famous near miss. Andreessen pointed Arora to the dinner speaker nobody had heard of, Palmer Luckey, and Arora and Durban agreed to invest in Oculus alongside the founders Brendan Iribe and Luckey. The paperwork never arrived. Arora woke up one morning to the news that Facebook had bought the company.

Arora has held each of his jobs for a long time by modern standards, and he argues that staying put is not the same as standing still. Google changed completely around him across his decade there. What matters, he says, is whether the platform in front of you keeps handing you new problems. At Palo Alto Networks, the problem he set himself on arrival was simple to state: how does an $18 billion company become a $100 billion one, and beyond.

His answer started with enterprise economics rather than security. Product development runs at 15 to 18 percent of revenue at most enterprise companies, and administration at 6 to 10 percent, so nearly the whole gap between gross margin and operating margin is sales, marketing, and support. The most expensive thing in enterprise software is acquiring a customer. A company with one product knocks on twenty doors and hears no fifteen times, while a company with a truck full of products can sell something to almost every customer it reaches. Palo Alto Networks had one thing to sell. Arora's insight was that it needed many, sold to the same top customers, and each one had to be best in class, because nobody wants the second best security product.

Building those products himself was too slow. A good technology product takes four to seven years to build, whoever you are, and he had arrived to find no pipeline. So he would buy. But the history of acquisitions is mostly failure, so he studied failed deals for their patterns and designed rules to control the variables he could. About 35 acquisitions later, the rules are the heart of the episode.

The first rule concerns who runs what he buys. The standard failure, he says, is to hand an acquired company to the internal manager who had been competing with it and losing. That person had every resource and still lost to a founder working out of a warehouse. So Arora inverts it: the acquired founder becomes the head of that category, and the internal team works for them. Some of those people leave. He accepts that, because rewarding the losing side with the prize asset risks destroying the asset.

The second rule is to buy only the number one or number two company in a category. The top two take a disproportionate share of a market's profits, and buying number three to polish it up never works, in his telling, because number one still exists and a large acquirer inevitably slows whatever it touches. He is candid that one and two are judgment calls, sometimes separated more by the founder's will than by revenue, and that Palo Alto has bought what it thought was number one and watched number two pass it through its own execution mistakes.

The third rule is the one other executives in the episode say they have never heard of. Arora negotiates the price himself, directly with the founder, with no bankers between them, on the grounds that the two of them have to work together for the next four years. The moment they agree on a deal, the paperwork goes to the finance and legal teams, and the founder's job becomes a 30 day sprint with Arora and his product people to design a joint product roadmap. He will not sign the definitive agreement until the founder signs that roadmap. Founders, he says, are dramatically more willing to align before they have the proceeds than after. Twice, Palo Alto walked away at that stage, once sensing the founders were only playing along to close, and once finding the technology thinner than diligence had suggested.

Founders who do sign are revested over three to four years, because what is being bought is a team and its continuity, not a legal entity. He puts it bluntly: you are not buying companies, you are buying teams, their point of view, their strategy, and their execution. Acquired founders also have to change how they operate. The will that wins as an underdog can wreck a company from inside a large one, and the founder's job becomes motivating 6,000 field staff to amplify the product rather than treating every colleague as an obstacle. His own negotiating style follows the same logic: the people across the table are about to become colleagues, so they are not treated as conquered enemies.

On price, his arithmetic is deliberately loose at the top. If an asset is worth $200 million, it can be worth $800 million, because his job is to turn it into a $5 billion to $10 billion asset, and nobody remembers the difference. He credits the same line to Durban, who told the WME team buying IMG that they would never remember the extra $400 million, only whether they got the asset. Durban once returned the favor by telephone, talking the value-minded Arora through a 29 round auction for the London Spirit cricket team the two men now co-own, with the advice that this was sport and he should not stop bidding.

The system has misses. One early acquisition was a bet on IP based segmentation that the market never adopted, and he cut it quickly, treating acquisitions as a portfolio the way drug companies treat compounds in trials. Senra raises Henry Singleton, who built Teledyne through more than a hundred acquisitions and then stopped for fifteen years, and asks whether Palo Alto Networks might stop too. Arora says it might. In his framing, M&A is not a strategy. It is a mechanism for executing one, used when a category matters and his own team is not where it needs to be.

On artificial intelligence, he argues the technology makes security more valuable, not less. Connectivity created the attack surface, and AI is expanding it: new models can find vulnerabilities in hours that used to take days, and attackers can rent that capability cheaply. Palo Alto's response includes what he calls virtual patching, writing signatures that bandage the perimeter until customers can patch the systems underneath, and he says business has risen since the newest vulnerability finding models appeared. His structural defense is cost. His company inspects the traffic on a laptop for about $25 a year at near zero marginal cost, while an AI system charged per token would cost orders of magnitude more to do the same watching. Attackers who get inside still have to be found and ejected, and that takes data, infrastructure, and expertise that a model alone does not supply.

The last third of the episode is about temperament. Arora describes a Hindu fatalism he says he only found in the last five years: do your best, leave no stone unturned, and let fate handle the rest. The most anxious moment of his life, he says, was giving a speech at his daughter's wedding in India in February. The daughter, Aisha, moved from marketing at Brex to venture capital and is now starting her own fund, with her father as an adviser she calls once or twice a day.

His own start was the opposite of cushioned. He arrived in America with $200 because that was what his father could give him, and his father later sent $2,000 toward tuition. As a parent, he refuses to pressure his children or to keep reminding them that he started poor, which he calls a distinction parents invent. Children are born into their circumstances, he says. The parent's job is to protect their drive and their happiness, and to let them watch and choose which parts of a parent's example they want.

Two earlier chapters round out the portrait. Selling Google advertising in Europe, he could tell customers neither the price nor the volume, because bids set the price and searches set the volume, so his job was teaching customers the return on investment math behind a maximum bid. Rainy days in France were good for revenue, because people stayed home and searched. And he keeps learning the way he advises founders to: four or five hours a week with AI founders, five to fifteen security startups a month, and a new habit of posting on X, which he started after Harry Stebbings teased him for letting podcasters build brands on his answers. He writes the posts himself. One early post was polished by an AI tool, and the grief he got for it settled the matter.

Open questions

  1. Arora's acquisition rules were designed for a world where a good product takes four to seven years to build. If AI keeps compressing that cycle, does buying the number one or number two company still beat building, and which of his rules breaks first?
  2. His defense against AI disruption rests on near zero marginal cost at the endpoint, roughly $25 per laptop per year against per token AI pricing. How far would token prices have to fall before that moat needs a different argument?
  3. He says Palo Alto Networks could stop acquiring altogether, as Henry Singleton did at Teledyne. What would the company have to become internally, in product development and in founder retention, for building to win on the same terms?

Bloomberg Originals · Francine Lacqua with Sunil Bharti Mittal on transforming India’s telecom market

The Billionaire Who Transformed India’s Telecom Market | Leaders with Francine Lacqua

On Bloomberg Originals, “The Billionaire Who Transformed India’s Telecom Market” is an episode of Leaders with Francine Lacqua, and the premise is stated in its first exchange: Sunil Bharti Mittal’s journey tracks the Indian economy almost exactly. Mittal built Bharti Airtel from a Delhi mobile license into a business touching more than 650 million customers, and he tells the story as a series of bets placed just ahead of a country that kept changing the rules on him.

He started at 18 in Ludhiana, an industrial town he calls the Manchester of India, selling bicycle parts with a $1,500 loan from his father because he could not afford to make a whole bicycle. He concluded he would always be a sub-optimal small-scale manufacturer if he stayed, moved to Bombay, and imported whatever would sell: zip fasteners, copper scrap, aluminum, stainless steel plates. The jackpot was Suzuki generators from Japan, in a country where he had grown up with eight to ten hours of power cuts a day. He became India’s largest generator importer, and then the government banned the imports and the business vanished overnight.

That was life under the license raj, the system of permits and controls the episode’s narration sketches, where a private company needed approval to import machinery or expand a factory. Mittal says his edge was speed and positioning: he did things big business had not done before, in places where incumbents were absent, and he learned policy well enough to stay a step ahead of it.

Weeks after the generator ban, in Taipei, he saw a push-button telephone for the first time. He tied up with Kingtel of Taiwan, broke the phones down in Hong Kong, shipped the pieces into India and assembled them there. When the government then required phones to be manufactured domestically, he won one of 42 manufacturing licenses, spent a year and a half building a factory, and never looked back.

The 1991 liberalization opened the next act. On holiday in Goa on January 2, 1992, Mittal saw an advertisement inviting bids for mobile licenses. After a two to three year saga he was awarded the Delhi license in 1994 and launched services in 1995, the year Bharti Airtel was born. The government then opened the whole country, handset prices fell, prepaid billing and calling-party-pays spread, and mobile adoption ran through India’s vast network of small shops and cash transactions.

Telecom, he says, has been a relentless battleground. The first great shock was Reliance’s entry in 2002, when the petrochemical giant launched cheap calling and bundled handsets at a scale Airtel could not match. Mittal calls it an existential crisis. His strategy was to lie on the ground and let the storm pass, which took about a year and a half; remarkably, only two of his people were poached. The company survived, and the Ambani split later put Reliance Communications in Anil Ambani’s hands while Mukesh Ambani regrouped.

The bigger storm came in 2016, when Mukesh Ambani returned with Reliance Jio, a network built for 4G data from the start, offering free calls and cheap data to anyone. Mittal estimates about $100 billion of standing investment across the industry was rendered useless, and many companies disappeared. Airtel bought spectrum and smaller rivals, its stock fell to a fifth of its value, and Mittal raised $18 billion in 34 months, diluting his own stake from 51 percent to 25 percent. He says the dilution did not matter; what mattered was being the last man standing. The market shrank from roughly ten private players to three, and India remains one of the cheapest telecom markets in the world, which is why Mittal publicly argues for tariff rises to keep carriers financially healthy.

Expansion took him to Africa, where buying Zain’s operations made Airtel a multinational overnight, with 15 countries strapped on. He now calls the move crazy and says he would not do it today. The India playbook misfired: there was no comparable middle class, and when Airtel cut prices, customers saved the money instead of spending more on phones. Eventually the operation worked, and Airtel Africa is today listed in London and, in his telling, a great success story.

On risk, Mittal draws a line between founders and managers. In his first ten to twelve years he bet the house many times; later the job becomes managing risk without sliding into risk aversion, the trap he sees in large companies with no significant sponsor. Entrepreneurs, he argues, are constitutionally in the risk category, while managers are trained to protect. He also notes that India, and Asia generally, is still unforgiving of failure, and needs to learn that a failed founder should be acceptable in the marketplace.

On geopolitics, he says the world he expected, one big global market, has receded: tariffs are rising, every government claims the right to protect its own market, and bilateral deals are the norm. India, he argues, is playing its cards extremely well, trading its 1.5 billion consumers for free trade agreements with the US, the EU, the UK, the UAE, New Zealand and Australia, while staying neutral enough to keep doing business with China and Russia. He points to Apple’s production shifting to India as evidence the country is becoming a manufacturing nation in a way he could not have imagined in his lifetime.

Asked whether he would enter telecom today, he says only outside the US, and that plain telecom is no longer tomorrow’s business. Airtel is adding financial services, cloud and sovereign cloud, and data centers on top of connectivity. The same connectivity logic took Bharti into space: in 2020 Bharti Global partnered with the UK government to invest $1 billion rescuing the bankrupt satellite operator OneWeb, added $500 million more, and OneWeb later merged with Eutelsat. Mittal’s pitch is sovereignty. Eutelsat OneWeb, European, British and Indian led, is the one low Earth orbit constellation that is neither American nor Chinese, and he argues every bloc will want an alternative to Starlink. The barriers are severe: a constellation costs about $10 billion, takes seven to eight years, and its satellites need replacing every five to six years, so the world has room for perhaps three or four, no more.

He closes on what the phone has done to countries like India and Africa. Where hard infrastructure would take decades, digital arrived first: India transacts billions of UPI payments daily, and government subsidies land directly in villagers’ phones. And on his own career, his summary is simple. He has had the luxury of doing what he loves, rather than loving what he does, and he thinks that is what made the difference.

Open questions

  1. Airtel survived Jio by consolidating and raising capital, but Indian tariffs are still among the world’s lowest and Mittal wants them higher. Will regulators and consumers let the surviving three carriers rebuild margins, or does the market stay structurally cheap?
  2. Mittal argues India can keep trading with the US, China and Russia at once while signing bilateral deals with all of them. If the US and China rivalry hardens into forced choices, how long can that neutrality hold?
  3. He says the satellite business has room for only three or four constellations. Will Eutelsat OneWeb’s sovereignty argument be enough to win it a durable seat against Starlink’s head start and Amazon’s spending?

Lenny's Podcast · Tulsee Doshi on why PMs matter more at the frontier

Why Google wants to win the frontier conversation | Tulsee Doshi (Head of Product for Gemini)

On Lenny's Podcast, the episode "Why Google wants to win the frontier conversation" is a conversation between Lenny Rachitsky and Tulsee Doshi, Google's head of product for its frontier models. Her portfolio covers Gemini, the generative media models behind Nano Banana, Veo and Lyria, the Gemma open models, and a set of audio models for dialogue, translation and text to speech that she says gets talked about far less than it should.

Her most quoted idea is that model development is not like ordinary product management. A PM can set goals, pick capabilities and decide which evals to hit, but the model itself is not predictable. She compares it to Snape's potions class: you choose the ingredients carefully, and then some magic happens that surprises you. So the job has two halves. One is shaping what goes into training. The other is studying what comes out, finding where the model unexpectedly shines, and turning that into a product. That is also why she keeps returning to the word vibes: benchmarks can say a capability improved, but only hands-on use tells you how the model feels, and how that feeling will land with users.

Rachitsky puts the common outside view to her directly: many people feel Google lags the top two labs, OpenAI and Anthropic, with bursts of being best followed by slowdowns. Doshi does not accept the framing. Google, she says, does want to win the frontier conversation, and the forthcoming Gemini 4, which she names Argon, is meant to prove it. But she argues Google's strategy is full stack in a way the other labs are not: hardware at the bottom, models in the middle, and, by her count, fourteen products with more than a billion users at the top. The goal is not one flagship chatbot but a family of models spread across the cost and quality curve, serving a search user, an engineer, or a cyber defender at the point each of them needs.

On safety she speaks from an unusual seat: before this job she led product for Google's responsible AI team for six years. Her optimism, she says, runs through intentionality rather than around it. She points to mitigations built into deployment, including monitors that watch a model's chain of thought for misalignment so the team can intervene, and she argues the safety debate underweights what AI does for defense. With Gemini 4, Google is partnering with cyber defenders, including Wiz, now a Google company, whose scanning program found and helped patch a vulnerability in a hospital system. Stories about fixing vulnerabilities, she says, are the stories Google likes to be in.

A thread running through the middle of the conversation is that the model's behavior is itself a user experience. How transparent the model is, how it formats answers, what it asks back: that is UX, and PMs shape it. Her team works with researchers on a spec for what good looks like for Gemini, translates that spec into rubrics, evaluates the model against them, and feeds the results into reward modeling. The PM, in other words, is no longer just the person who writes requirements for other people's work.

Her framework for building on shifting ground is the 60-20-20 rule. Sixty percent of a product should work today. Twenty percent should work well enough, in ways you can see the models reaching soon. Y Combinator · Paper Club on whether AI can cure cancer

Is AI Now Good Enough To Cure Cancer? | YC Paper Club

On Y Combinator, "Is AI Now Good Enough To Cure Cancer? | YC Paper Club" records the tenth YC Paper Club, held as a YC AI+Bio Club night in front of a live audience. Four speakers, Alfredo Gonzalez, Yusuf Roohani, Garyk Brixi and Divya Nori, take up one question from four directions: biology now produces far more data than anyone can mine by hand, so what happens when AI does the mining?

The host opens with a live experiment. Still arguing with a friend from Arc AGI about whether the Turing test is solved, he vibe coded a chat app in the previous twelve hours, paired half the audience with each other and half with AI agents given a persona and the room's context, and let everyone interrogate for two minutes. The models ranked in a clear order, but a third of the humans were mistaken for AI, and his verdict was that the test is not passed yet, though it is close.

His case for the evening is that the raw material has changed. Sequencing costs keep falling through the floor, Evo 2 was trained on 9 trillion base pairs, and, as Demis Hassabis remarked at YC a couple of months earlier, AlphaFold is now used at some stage in every drug brought to market. His deeper claim is procedural: for any task that can be verified, in a computer or in the physical world, the field now has a recipe for improving a model against that check, so foundation models can propose drugs, experiments can test them, and the loop can repeat. It will be expensive and slow in the physical world, he concedes, but the procedure is known.

Alfredo Gonzalez, a co-founder of AMA, argues that cancer has quietly become a data problem. A decade ago the Cancer Genome Atlas could sequence a few hundred patients per tumor type, so researchers were forced to average across patients and hunt for common mutations. Sequencing is now cheap enough, and usually covered by insurance for advanced tumors, that a single patient's tumor can be treated as its own dataset of some 20,000 genes, plus copy numbers, RNA expression, splicing and cell states. The bottleneck is no longer generating the data. It is mining it.

AMA's answer is a personalized research lab for each patient. Work that once needed a thirty person team of algorithm experts, cluster engineers, variant callers and clinicians can now be agenticized, he says, so the pipeline mines one patient's data in real time and stays connected to new findings as they appear. He describes a rare brain tumor that clustered closer to mesenchymal tumors than to other brain tumors once its pathways were compared across datasets, which pointed to therapies the literature had not suggested, and another patient whose last three lines of chemotherapy the data showed to be wrong for their tumor's biology, allowing a course correction.

Yusuf Roohani, who started the machine learning team at the Arc Institute and is now head of science at Entology, works one level up, on virtual cells. Molecular models such as AlphaFold and ESM can predict structures from sequence, but predicting how a whole cell behaves is harder, and poor prediction of what works in a human is, he argues, the main reason drugs fail in the clinic. His universal cell embedding model, published in Nature earlier this year, learned a shared representation of cell state from some 35 million cells through self-supervised learning alone, and the structure that emerged matched known cell biology.

Scaling that approach up taught a harder lesson. His team used agents to reprocess the NIH repository of raw transcriptomic data and reached more than 500 million cells, only to find the data overwhelmingly skewed to immune cells, with T cells overrepresented by an order of magnitude. Mining all public data buys volume, not diversity. His conclusion is to scale AI guided experimentation instead of data generation: run discovery as a loop, and borrow the machinery of autonomous AI research to decide which experiments are worth running.

That borrowing is literal in his current work. Entology's autonomous research system, Locus, led a public benchmark for autonomously post-training language models, beating common coding agents because it is optimized for search rather than coding. Applied to molecular design, the same system improved predictions of molecular binding efficiency as compute scaled, handled the noisy, tiny validation sets typical of science, and searched the latent space of foundation models more efficiently than existing tools. The results are in silico so far, but they suggest the primitives of AI research automation transfer to biological discovery.

Garyk Brixi, who led the Evo 2 team and is a PhD student at Stanford, asks how much a model can learn from DNA itself. Evo 2 treats DNA as a language and trains with next base pair prediction across all life, using a hybrid of attention and convolution to reach context lengths of a million base pairs, at 7 billion and 40 billion parameters. The data curation mattered as much as the architecture: keeping one genome per species and weighting training toward genic and conserved regions was, he says, the difference between state of the art and the bottom of the pack on mutation effect benchmarks.

Because the model learns what DNA is likely, an unlikely mutation is a signal of disease or lost function, and Evo 2 matches or beats supervised, human specific models at spotting such mutations despite having seen only one human reference genome. Its internal features are legible too: sparse autoencoders find neurons that fire on prophage related sequence across very different contexts, including prophages that other methods had not annotated. A newer bidirectional model from the team predicts how distant parts of a genome interact, and has already surfaced hundreds of unannotated non-coding RNAs and a CRISPR-like reverse transcriptase system that the lab then validated.

Divya Nori closes with the surprise that Evo 2 can be prompted. In-context learning, the emergent ability behind few-shot prompting in language models, appears in a model trained only to predict nucleotides, never trained for it. Prompted with the fourteen known examples of a rare class of lanmodulin proteins, it generates plausible new members of the family. Prompted with four CRISPR-Cas loci, it designs complete Cas9 systems. Prompted with examples, it generates whole phage genomes with the intended architecture, at a fraction of the cost of fine-tuning.

Her team then post-trained the model for one design task, using reinforcement learning with a sequence to function model as the reward, so it uses its in-context examples better. The result was a novel enhancer, a DNA element that switches genes on in specific cell types, which a colleague tested in the mouse brain and found active and specific where it was designed to be. Her larger point is that the whole inference time toolbox developed for language models, prompting, harnesses and lab in the loop iteration, may now apply to biological design.

  2. When a per-patient analysis says the standard lines of therapy were wrong for that patient's tumor, who carries responsibility for acting on it, and what evidence standard should an agentic lab have to meet before a clinician overrides the usual sequence?

On AI News & Strategy Daily | Nate B Jones, “You Use AI Every Week. Voters Just Said Not In My Backyard.” is Nate B Jones’s attempt to think past election night. His starting point is blunt: the AI data center build-out had a terrible night on November 3, 2026, and AI itself is not popular. In his telling, most Americans have come to feel that AI is set against the American dream rather than for it, and Silicon Valley has mostly itself to blame for messaging that left room for that framing.

The paradox sits in two numbers he cites. A May survey from Edison Research and SSRS found that 65 percent of American adults had used an AI chatbot in the previous week, up from 52 percent in February. An October poll from ABC News, the Washington Post and Ipsos found that 72 percent of Americans opposed a new data center in their community. Heavy use and heavy opposition live in the same population, and on November 3 the opposition voted: communities across the country blocked or restricted data center development, and in Edgewater, Florida, unofficial results showed more than three quarters of voters backing a prohibition on new data centers, margins Jones says were typical.

Demand, meanwhile, is not waiting for permission. His summary of the voter position is that Americans seem to expect AI to arrive without data centers, as if it came out of the air. It has to come from somewhere, and the rest of the piece follows what happens when the somewhere gets harder to build.

The danger, he says, is running out of money before you can serve demand that is still very much there.

That failure mode produces what he calls the almost backward consequence. A failed project does not disappear, because demand keeps rising and someone buys it. His conclusion is that the backlash may ironically strengthen Big Tech: the industry takes a very public political defeat and comes out more concentrated, with smaller AI companies renting more of their compute from the large players they are trying to compete against.

He gives the counterargument real weight, crediting Derek Thompson with making it after the election. If every developer builds against the same growth forecast, each project looks reasonable on its own while the collective result is far more capacity than the market can use when it arrives. Slowing construction while demand keeps growing gives customers time to catch up with supply, which moderates the overbuilding risk that had worried him for 2027. A cash-strapped developer can still go to the wall, he says, while the net effect across the economy is a supply of tokens moderated enough to avoid a bubble outcome entirely.

Displaced demand then goes looking for a home. A company whose preferred project is delayed does not wait for the next legislative session; it signs with a different provider or a different community, and suddenly a more expensive location with deliverable power, available water and a supportive community looks attractive, because opening on time is worth more than cheap land. That shifts bargaining power to communities, which now hold something developers increasingly need, a credible place to build, and can negotiate over infrastructure costs, union jobs and local investment. Jones argues the old map of friendly and unfriendly states stops being useful, because what matters is the specific site, its power agreements and its local support, and if every displaced project piles into the same few welcoming spots, those places will feel the pressure too.

Some of the search leaves the United States altogether. Europe is campaigning with its gigafactory initiative and the UK has set out what it expects from developers, but Jones hears more developer interest outside the EU than inside it, on the view that building in Europe is not much faster than building in the US even after November 3. The places that stand to gain, he argues, are those that can set their own rules with stable land, stable power and a friendly relationship with the US: Canada and Mexico on geography, plus India, parts of Southeast Asia and parts of South America. Jobs follow the investment, and his sharpest line on the subject is that keeping a data center out of a community does not keep the technology out. It means the community ends up renting the technology instead of owning a piece of it.

The most speculative consequence is orbital. Jones notes that xAI has said publicly it is going after orbital data centers, that Elon Musk and Jensen Huang have both talked about them, and that Google already has a Project Suncatcher prototype in orbit. The obstacles are real engineering: radiation, thermal management, depreciation and maintaining hardware you cannot walk up to. But orbit is a much looser regulatory environment, and the two players who can reach it, Google and xAI in his account, now have a stronger incentive to lean in. His own bet moves from ten years to six to eight for finding out whether orbital compute works at scale, not because the technology got easier, but because uncertainty about building on the ground in the US makes the option worth more.

His closing advice is to watch behavior rather than statements: what companies do with the next project they were planning, whether they wait, move, pay more for a site they can deliver on, or buy capacity from someone else. Capital allocation on the balance sheet, he argues, tells the truth in a way press releases do not, and construction headlines are a poor forecast for adoption, since employers will keep buying AI services from somewhere regardless. Underneath the analysis sits the concern he ends on. A friend of his who works at an AI company told him after the midterms that he feels left out of the AI race and worries about his job. If people inside the industry feel that way, Jones argues, the empowerment story is failing, and fixing how AI is talked about, as a tool that helps individuals get where they want to go rather than a force set against them, is the actual project of the next two years.

Big Think · Paul Davies on the origin mysteries physics cannot explain

The laws of the universe are unable to explain these origin mysteries | Paul Davies: Full Interview

On Big Think, “The laws of the universe are unable to explain these origin mysteries” is a full interview with Paul Davies, the theoretical physicist and cosmologist at Arizona State University, where he directs the Beyond Center for Fundamental Concepts in Science. Davies, the author of The Demon in the Machine and What’s Eating the Universe?, organizes the conversation around three origin mysteries: the origin of the universe, the origin of life, and the origin of consciousness. The universe, he says, we understand pretty well. Life is a tough problem, but science is on the case. Consciousness is the deepest of the three, because we do not yet know how to frame the questions.

His working definition of life is chemistry plus information. Break a human being down into raw materials and the components would fetch a few cents on the open market, so what makes life special is not the hardware but the behavior, the software. Physics has spent most of its effort on the hardware side of that pairing, and Davies argues the software side, how living things store, deploy and manage information, is where the neglected half of the explanation sits.

Information itself needs care. In the technical sense it is a reduction in uncertainty, measurable in bits, but the information inside a cell does something bits alone do not capture. Scramble a bacterium’s DNA and the amount of information is unchanged, yet the bacterium is dead, the way a book whose words have been shuffled still contains the same words and means nothing. Living information is meaningful, contextual, embedded in the whole organism, and a good deal of it lives outside the genome, in gene networks and in the folded structure of DNA itself. Davies calls this the information management that supervises the organization of the whole system, something local, force-by-force physics is not built to describe.

The historical thread runs from Aristotle, for whom even a falling stone was heading toward its natural place, through the vitalists and their élan vital, to Darwin, who flatly refused the origin question, writing that it was mere rubbish to talk of the origin of life, as one might as well talk about the origin of matter. The chemical pathway began with Miller and Urey in the early 1950s, sparking gases in a flask and producing amino acids. Davies’s verdict is that this addressed the hardware of life and that prebiotic chemistry has not traveled much further down the road since.

Erwin Schrödinger saw the other half coming. His book What Is Life? imagined heredity stored in an aperiodic crystal, a stable structure with an enormous number of possible arrangements, which turned out to be DNA. Schrödinger also predicted that explaining life would take a new kind of physical law, not one more law added to the existing list but a different kind of law altogether. Davies says he has taken that prediction seriously throughout his career.

For Davies, the crux of the origin is a single step: how did chemistry learn to write its own code? There may have been simpler precursor codes, with fewer amino acids or pairs instead of triplets, but the moment an abstract, mathematical code appeared inside molecules and began directing their behavior is the moment he would call non-life becoming life. Nobody knows how that happened, and he regards it as the deepest part of the mystery.

The epigenetic material makes the same point from the other end. Michael Levin’s laboratory at Tufts works with planarian worms that can be cut into pieces, each regrowing a complete worm. Worms chemically induced to grow two heads produce two-headed descendants when cut again, generation after generation, with identical DNA. The instructions for the body plan are stored somewhere else, possibly electrically, and inherited without being written in the genes. Whatever the mechanism, a genes-only account of biological information is missing part of the picture.

The second chapter takes up entropy. Life does not defy the second law of thermodynamics: organisms are open systems that export their disorder into the environment, as a refrigerator moves heat the wrong way by paying an electricity bill. Maxwell’s demon sharpens the idea. A creature that sorts fast molecules from slow ones turns information into a temperature difference, which makes information a kind of fuel, and nature got there long ago. Our bodies are full of molecular machines doing the demon’s work, operating remarkably close to the limit the second law permits. Life, in his phrase, plays the margins of the second law.

What is missing, he argues, is a framework that joins the two levels: the mathematical laws of physics operating locally at the bottom, and laws of organization that describe the whole system at the top. Ideas about top-down causation exist, but nothing yet amounts to a general, fundamental account of how the global context of an organism governs its local chemistry. Until that new kind of law arrives, he expects the origin of life to stay unexplained, and that gap is what the interview’s title points at.

The third chapter asks whether we are alone, and starts from an embarrassment: every conclusion rests on a single sample, Earth life, which may even have started on Mars before flourishing here. Opinion has swung from Francis Crick’s view of life as a near-miraculous fluke to claims that the universe teems with life, mostly because extremophiles survive in far harsher conditions than once thought possible. Davies is unmoved. Widening the places life could persist by a factor of ten does nothing against odds he quotes at one in a trillion trillion. What would settle it is a second sample of life, and one microbe on Earth that is life, but not as we know it, would do.

We would struggle to recognize that sample. The plumes of Enceladus carry organic molecules, but organic chemistry is not life, and we have no instrument that could deliver a verdict, no life meter, and not even a death meter that could separate the dormant from the dead, since frozen bacteria are more like a stopped clock than a broken one. Titan has been running prebiotic chemistry for four and a half billion years, but a reading of ninety-some percent of the way to life would be meaningless when we cannot define almost life, nor say whether the road from chemistry to life is a gradual climb, in Richard Dawkins’s image, up Mount Improbable, or a sudden jump like a gas bursting into flame.

His candidate for the second sample is close to home: a shadow biosphere. Life may have arisen on Earth many times, and descendants of a separate genesis could still be intermingled with known microbes, a different tree of life rather than another branch of ours, unnoticed because nobody has seriously looked. He is candid about the counterarguments. The first life to get going tends to monopolize resources and remake the planet, as oxygen, a corrosive pollutant when it first appeared, shows, and Earth’s interlocking biological, geological and atmospheric cycles may be rare enough to make this a rare Earth after all.

If anything out there is waiting to be contacted, he doubts it will be flesh and blood. A technological civilization quickly hands its heavy intellectual lifting to designed intelligence, which can redesign itself far faster than Darwinian intelligence evolves, and systems already being built blur the line further by incorporating living tissue. Combine that trajectory with quantum computing and you get entities able to explore every quantum alternative at once, which would appear god-like and quite possibly unintelligible to us. That, rather than Hollywood aliens, is what he thinks a first encounter would involve.

The final chapter turns to fine-tuning, the Goldilocks enigma. Adjust the constants of physics slightly, heavier electrons, a weaker weak force, stronger gravity, and the mathematics produces universes of neutrons only, or of hydrogen with no carbon for life to build on. Three explanations are on the table: it is a fluke, a designer set the values, or there is a multiverse in which observers can only ever find themselves in one of the rare universes where life is possible. At present, he says, choosing among the three is a matter of personal taste.

Davies offers a fourth way of his own. The laws may not be fixed at all. Against the traditional picture of laws stamped on the universe at the Big Bang like a maker’s mark, he floats the idea of flexible laws and a cosmic feedback loop, in which the emergence of life and mind connects back to the laws that made them possible, much as life and geology shape each other on Earth. The loophole is quantum uncertainty, which runs backward in time as well as forward, so there is no single fixed history from the Big Bang to the present. In that looseness, he suggests, the universe and its laws may have come into being together.

  1. If the origin of life is the moment chemistry wrote its own code, what observation or experiment could ever catch that step, rather than inferring it long after the fact?
  2. Davies wants a new kind of physical law that meshes bottom-level physics with top-level organization. What would such a law look like in practice, and how could it be tested?
  3. If a shadow biosphere were discovered on Earth, how would scientists confirm it was a genuinely separate genesis rather than an exotic branch of known life, and what would a second sample change about the search for life elsewhere?

[One Million AI Engineer Special] How Stanford Teaches AI-Native Engineering — Mihail Eric, Monaco

On Latent Space, “[One Million AI Engineer Special] How Stanford Teaches AI-Native Engineering — Mihail Eric, Monaco” is a special episode recorded to mark one million subscribers on the AI Engineer YouTube channel. Host swyx sits down with Mihail Eric, an adjunct lecturer at Stanford who teaches CS146S, The Modern Software Developer, and who leads AI at Monaco, a Series B startup building an AI-native sales platform. The conversation walks through Eric's ten-week syllabus almost week by week, and it keeps returning to one question: what should a junior engineer actually learn now?

Eric conceived the course in November 2024 and first taught it in fall 2025. It is now in its second run with a major refresh. Last year's version was a broad survey of agents across the software development life cycle. This year's version has solidified around principles instead: careful context engineering, and design and architecture for complex tasks. It is a practitioner's course, not a replacement for computer science fundamentals. Algorithms and data structures remain prerequisites, because implementation detail is increasingly the part students can hand to an agent, while judgment about systems is not.

His answer for juniors starts with the bar. This cohort is the first generation of the shift, and a well-trained AI-native engineer is, in his words, one of the most useful assets a company can have right now. But the floor is much higher than it used to be, close to senior-level expectations from day one. Every student he surveyed already uses coding agents; nobody writes code by hand. The university arranged tool credits so that nobody is bottlenecked by a twenty-dollar monthly plan.

The change Eric is proudest of is the open-source assignment. Last year's nine assignments were pedagogical and, frankly, one-shottable. This year students contribute to real AI-native open-source projects instead. They have to understand an unfamiliar repository, propose a change, and be reviewed by the project's actual maintainers. Companies including Warp, Factory and Resolve built partner exercises for the class. His rationale is mentorship: real review on real code is how the senior and junior gap has always closed, and some students will be hired off these contributions.

The early weeks build the machine before using it. Week one is harness internals: students build a Claude Code clone, learn that an agent loop is roughly two hundred lines, then intercept real Claude Code traffic with a man-in-the-middle proxy and read its system, plan and compaction prompts. Week two covers context engineering at scale, plus tool design and MCP, which Eric is firmly in favor of. Week three is skills, and week four is agent customization: configuration files, hooks and subagents.

Context engineering gets the sharpest treatment. Most practitioners, Eric says, never get past vanilla prompting, asking the model to just do the thing, and that fails in a million-line codebase. He teaches a framework he calls RAPID, which resembles Dex Horthy's research-plan-implement approach: a research phase, then a proposal offering a few alternative approaches with the human choosing, then a written plan or spec, then implementation and testing. Subagent context is still unsettled art, including whether subagents should inherit context or start isolated, how to fan work out and consolidate it, and the failure mode Dex calls the dumb zone. He also notes how much keeps moving underneath the course: Claude Code's system prompt once ran near twenty thousand tokens, was cut by roughly eighty percent, and grew again, because learnings keep getting baked into the model weights themselves.

Skills, in week three, are procedures you codify once so the agent runs them the same way every time. Two examples stand out. Matt Pocock's "grill me" skill interrogates the user before building, and Geoffrey Litt's explain-the-diff skill produces a visual summary of a change and then quizzes you on it, which Eric holds up as the antidote to switching your brain off. The failure mode is gardening: skills pile up, trigger at the wrong moments because their trigger text is sloppy, and need pruning. His advice is not to overcomplicate it. If you do something repeatedly, have the agent turn the last few runs into a skill and iterate in chat.

The middle of the episode detours into Monaco itself. The company sells an all-in-one, AI-native sales platform aimed at startups from seed to Series A, growing with them upmarket rather than trying to rip enterprise customers away from Salesforce. Its own agent harness powers go-to-market workflows end to end: scoring markets and ideal customer profiles, tracking pipeline, and handling the meeting summaries salespeople hate. Data is a core asset; the team built a continuously enriched database of companies and contacts, uses Parallel and Exa for enrichment, and times outreach to signals such as a prospect changing jobs. The company also bought the monaco.com domain, was sued by the country of Monaco over it, and won. For San Francisco listeners, its best-known marketing is a banner plane over the city, an experiment that stuck because it starts conversations.

Back at Stanford, week five covers agent-ready codebases, which Eric treats as the foundation everything later depends on: clear service boundaries, good documentation, and above all verifiability, so an agent's work can be checked. He endorses Factory's agent-readiness reports, which codify those principles. He is equally blunt that many larger companies are not blocked by agents at all. They are blocked by merge queues and slow, basic DevOps, and one more bot fixes none of that.

Week six is agentic code review, the new bottleneck. Once generation gets fast, a team can put up thirty or more pull requests a day, and a human doing line-by-line review becomes the choke point that wastes the speed. The middle path he teaches: design for a class of changes you can auto-approve with confidence, keep humans on the rest, and treat the pull request description as a living spec, with file-level pointers to what changed. Week seven is security, where the headline lessons are dependency discipline, since models happily install whatever the supply chain offers, and vocabulary: engineers who cannot distinguish authentication from authorization, or who have never looked at their cloud identity settings, cannot prompt for what they cannot name. Auth, data storage and billing are not areas to vibe code.

Weeks eight and nine scale the picture up. Background agents, including Stripe's Minions, Ramp's Inspect and Spotify's system, close the loop from production signals: an on-call agent with access to logs and the repository can open the first fix before a human wakes up. Team infrastructure follows: LLM gateways and MCP portals, which swyx argues are converging into a single layer handling governance, security, auditing and cost, and which Eric largely concedes, with the caveat that inside real companies those layers stay separate because separate teams own them. The role that owns the mission-critical layer, both agree, is the platform engineer.

Week ten is the software factory, dark or half-lit, and the question of what work is left. Eric's answer: two human choke points survive, intent at the top and verification at the bottom. Everything between them can, in principle, be automated, and he points to OpenAI's harness engineering write-up, an internal tool built over five months with no manually written code, as an early sketch. For students, his closing advice is about ambition and altitude. Think in whole systems, not single features. Go through the phase of over-automating everything, then come out of it accountable to real products. And his freebie for week eleven: make benchmarks and evals, and write rigorous engineering blog posts, because SWE-bench itself began as an undergraduate project at Princeton, and that kind of work still opens every frontier lab's door.

McKinsey & Company · Driving agentic transformation in large-scale change

Driving Agentic Transformation: How AI Is Rewriting the Playbook on Large-Scale Change

On McKinsey & Company, "Driving Agentic Transformation: How AI Is Rewriting the Playbook on Large-Scale Change" is an episode of Inside the Strategy Room, the firm's strategy podcast. Host Sean Brown talks with partners Chase Covington and Tomer Slaney about the argument of a recent McKinsey article on the agentic transformation office, which Covington co-authored. Their starting observation has an irony to it: the transformation office, the small team charged with driving large-scale change across a company, has itself been one of the slowest functions to adopt AI.

One reason is simple workload. Transformation offices are, in Covington's words, just busy, really busy, with the day-to-day. Slaney describes one four-person team the firm studied that spent about 80 percent of its time on reporting, creating materials, preparing for meetings, and follow-ups: necessary work, because trust in a transformation depends on the materials being correct and on time, but repeatable, predictable, and manual.

The weekly rhythm makes it worse. Transformations run on a cadence of data pulls and executive meetings, and the production work, the grunt work, gets compressed into exactly the window between the data arriving and the meeting starting. That is also the only window in which the team could be studying the data for insight, so the guests describe a structural ceiling: the insight work spills into the following days, by which time the data has moved on and the findings risk going stale.

Their answer is to hand the repeatable work to AI agents. Agents draft the weekly decks from the data, run the recurring checks and updates, verify that the right people are invited to the right meetings, and perform consistency audits no human team has time for: reading every slide and appendix to flag where this week's figures contradict last week's, or where a change to one initiative failed to trigger the follow-on changes its dependent initiatives needed. One team's nightly quality-control agent hands the office a prioritized list of what changed each day, and catching problems early, before anyone has to explain a variance, has built real goodwill between the office and the workstreams it serves.

What the freed time is for matters more to both guests than the savings themselves. It goes to coaching initiative owners, building alignment, and pushing for more value, the human work that makes change stick. Covington puts the boundary plainly: do not use AI bots to talk to the business. Agentifying the office also lowers the human load needed to stand one up, and lets a small office reach more employees and embed change more deeply, which McKinsey's research links to better odds of success. The four-person team built four agents and ended up smaller, yet freed roughly a full person's worth of time for higher-value work. Both guests also expect the label itself to fade: an agentic transformation is becoming just a transformation, the way using the internet stopped being a notable feature of a project years ago.

The risks they name are mostly about attention. One is cognitive surrender, accepting an agent's polished output without real review, only to find on inspection that it is slop. The other is attention surrender, illustrated by an action-tracking bot that read sarcasm in a meeting and dutifully logged an action item for the CEO to call every employee in the company and tell them to do their jobs. Their countermeasures are deliberate human-in-the-loop review moments, making people pitch the materials they bring so understanding is tested, and one firm rule: if you put something on a page, it is yours, whatever tool drafted it.

Data security gets the same treatment, because a transformation office holds some of the most sensitive information in a company. The guests warn against feeding ad hoc extracts into home-built agents, and favor working inside governed platforms, in McKinsey's case its Wave system, where the data stays in place and visibility can be matched to the audience. Model choice, by contrast, barely matters to them: most of the work runs fine on open-source models, the fit of the workflow matters far more, and swapping a tuned workflow onto a newer model can actually set the output back.

The closing note is about the jobs themselves. From the most junior person to the chief transformation officer, the work changes rather than disappears: less time making decks and chasing data irregularities, more time coaching the people who own the initiatives. The tools are different and the role is different, but the underlying job, changing humans, is not.

  3. They counsel keeping the same model behind a tuned workflow because upgrades can change the output for the worse. How long can an office hold that line as models improve, and who should decide when a switch is worth the disruption?
