Peter Yang · Charles Lamanna of Microsoft on what engineers do when AI writes the code

What Should Engineers Do When AI Writes All the Code | Charles Lamanna (EVP Microsoft)

On Peter Yang, "What Should Engineers Do When AI Writes All the Code | Charles Lamanna (EVP Microsoft)" is a conversation recorded at Microsoft Ignite with Charles Lamanna, Microsoft's executive vice president for Copilot, agents, and platform. Yang opens with the question in the title: if agents do more and more of the actual work, what is left for the humans? Lamanna's answer, developed across the interview, is that the work moves up a level, toward goals, guardrails, verification, and alignment, and that the engineers living it tell him they have never had to think harder.

Lamanna thinks it is probably inevitable that agents become the main client of a lot of software. So much of his own work already runs through agents: code generated, apps tested, and debugging done by an agent rather than by hand, with him still reviewing and verifying what it does and setting it off on the right tasks. Leading a large engineering team at Microsoft, he uses Copilot's cowork agents for long running jobs such as reviewing GPU capacity, doing budget planning, and organizing the next quarter's roadmap, some through API connections and some through computer use that launches an app or a browser and clicks through it. The picture is not agents on their own, he says, but agents in very large numbers working at the direction of a person, a force multiplier that will put far more load on systems. He cites a report of a fourteenfold increase in the load on GitHub over a year, driven not by more human developers but by agents submitting pull requests on behalf of people.

Those agents, he warns, are very harsh customers. They look for the best performance, the best atomic units, and programmability, and what an agent prefers is not always what a person would prefer, so the design pressure shifts toward efficiency, price, performance, and reliability. His favorite example is search: a person wants ten links with small snippets, because nobody reads a thousand results, but an agent does not care and can take a thousand results with a bigger chunk of text on each. As agents show up in more places, APIs and user experiences will evolve modes that support the agent client better.

Asked whether Copilot replaces Office, Lamanna describes three moves instead. First, Microsoft brought Office into Copilot: inside the Copilot app you can now open a Word document, a PowerPoint deck, or an Excel spreadsheet in a side by side experience, the conversation on one side and the full fidelity app on the other, because for fine work the mouse still wins. Nobody wants to type a prompt asking to move a title eight pixels to the left when they can click and drag. Second, Microsoft brought Copilot into the Office apps, where most users meet AI in the context of the document they are already working in, moving between keyboard, mouse, and prompt box as each suits the task. Third, the Office family keeps expanding, as it did when Outlook, Teams, and OneNote arrived: the Office team built Pages, an HTML forward format with Office security, sharing, and storage behind it, and Workspace, a newer Copilot construct. Journeys will increasingly start in a prompt box, he says, but that does not make the apps less important, and people tend to overestimate how quickly things change.

The multiplayer question runs through the middle of the conversation. AI tooling, Lamanna argues, has been overfit to how software engineers work, and coding is a somewhat lonely profession: a meeting, a backlog, a spec, then days alone on a branch before a pull request comes back for review. Most knowledge work is not like that. Sales, customer service, financial processes, and operations slice constantly between individual work, collaboration, and communication, and a sales rep who spends days in single player mode not talking to another person is probably not a very good salesperson. So Microsoft is stretching Copilot to support several people and several agents working together: the Office documents inside Copilot support real time co-authoring by people and Copilot at once, and the new Workspace concept gathers the people on a project, Copilot, and autopilots around one goal, whether that is a customer proposal or a response to a supply chain disruption, suggesting the right people and agents to add.

Designing for both humans and agents also means building new data assets. Some material is not very useful to people but is gold for agents, and Microsoft's Work IQ layer turns it into context: meeting recordings and transcripts nobody would ever rewatch, a legacy email inbox nobody rereads, the terabyte of files sitting in his own OneDrive. Accumulated properly, that record lets a future agent answer questions like why a decision was made and what alternatives were considered. The other half is etiquette. If you cannot be bothered to write ten pages, you probably should not generate ten pages and send them to ten people, and he expects social norms to form around where AI is used and how it is disclosed. He has already seen people volunteer that a document began as a dictation, that they read and copy edited it, and that AI helped generate it. The right direction of travel, he says, is absolutely not generating more stuff for people to read.

Yang puts the title question to him directly: is the human job now just to onboard and teach the agents? Lamanna says there is a lot more to it. The most AI pilled engineers on his team, the ones running software factories, may not craft code line by line, but they think about system design and architecture, about how generated code gets verified and tested, about safe rollout, and about which signals feed the coding agents. It is a different muscle, not a smaller one. Software has climbed this ladder before: engineers once wrote assembly by hand, then compilers arrived, then garbage collectors took over memory, then the cloud took over patching servers, and each step drew nostalgia before it drew relief. A software engineer from 1990 shown a modern cloud console would think most of the job had been replaced, yet there are more engineers than ever, solving higher order problems. Parts of the job will be done by agents; the human part is to zoom out, set the goals and the ambition, set the guardrails, do the verification, and keep monitoring. He is glad to hand off the slicing and dicing of data he used to do by hand before decisions; now AI builds the dashboard the way he wants it.

His closing theme is that building is nearly free now, but alignment is not. Writing the code takes little of the time in shipping a feature, and shipping takes little of it either; the time goes into coordination, getting people aligned on priorities, on whether something is worth doing, and on what the quality bar looks like. His principles for that world are threefold. Write the thinking down: every six months he writes a roughly twenty page memo of what is top of mind, high level rather than feature by feature, with calls such as making Copilot multi model with automatic routing, and those decisions cascade through the organization. Settle debates with experiments instead of arguments: as in search, build the A and the B arm, or five arms, run the flight, and let click through and satisfaction data tell the truth, a habit he wants even for enterprise software. And hire for talent density, then trust people and set them loose, which matters more, not less, when AI does so much of the production. It is, he says, the fastest change of his career, playing out on a mission critical code base that in places is more than forty years old.

Open questions

  1. If agents become the harshest customers of software, how should a product team divide its design effort between the interface humans see and the atomic, programmable surface agents prefer, and which side wins when the two conflict?
  2. Lamanna's etiquette rule is that nobody should generate ten pages they could not be bothered to write. Who ends up enforcing norms like that inside a company, the tooling, managers, or peers, and do the norms survive deadline pressure?
  3. If the scarce skills are no longer writing code but alignment, verification, and judgment, how should engineering hiring and promotion change to actually measure those things?

Lenny's Podcast · Sherif Mansour of Atlassian on the new rules of product management in the AI era

The new rules of product management in the AI era | Sherif Mansour (Head of AI, Atlassian)

On Lenny's Podcast, "The new rules of product management in the AI era | Sherif Mansour (Head of AI, Atlassian)" is a conversation between host Lenny Rachitsky and Sherif Mansour, who has spent 17 years at Atlassian, joined as its first product manager on Confluence, and now leads both the company's AI product strategy and the craft of product management across the company. Mansour spends the episode taking apart popular misconceptions about the PM job, and arguing that almost every piece of product advice is true only inside a particular context.

His central claim is that a great product manager is, above all, a filler of context. The job is to build a shared brain across the team, covering three kinds of context: the customer, the product and the market, and the business the team operates in. He tells his PMs to run away from the Jira backlog, because if a PM believes one card matters more than another, that belief rests on context the rest of the team does not have, and the real work is transferring it. That is why he never does customer interviews without engineers in the room: an engineer who hears a customer directly has an aha moment and an empathy that no summarized report can hand over. Scaling is where this gets hard. Past twenty or thirty people, trust and shared brains start to break, and the PM's work becomes finding the current context gap, which might be vision one quarter and customer understanding the next, and closing it.

A related misconception is that PMs own decision making. Mansour says PMs own decision making velocity. A PM in a squad has no reports and must influence without authority, so the goal is a team that can make good decisions itself, with the PM striving to hold no extra power. He illustrates with a 2010 story: interviewing Confluence customers, his team noticed people marking meeting notes with red and green stars to track done and not done work. An engineer in the room asked why Confluence pages did not simply have a checkbox, and tasks inside a document editor, a concept that did not exist on the web at the time, were born from her observation. When engineers are on the journey, he says, they become mini product managers in their own areas.

To match PMs to teams, Atlassian uses a PM craft triangle created by Geoff Redfern, whose three corners are the general manager, the scientist, and the artist. Scientist PMs lead discovery with hypotheses and A/B tests, general manager PMs lead with market size and business reasoning, and artist PMs lead with vision and experience. Very few people do all three well, though the all-rounders are gold for zero to one projects. The framework is used in hiring and calibration, because a struggling PM is often simply the wrong type for the project they are on, and swapping the project can revive them.

His career advice follows from the triangle: find the thing you are naturally good at and run toward it, rather than spending your career patching weaknesses. Mansour describes himself as a strong artist PM and jokes that he is the worst general manager type imaginable, and he says saying so publicly creates a safe space for others to name their weaknesses and build complementary teams around them, as he has with his colleague Jamille, whose strengths cover his gaps. The biggest unlock he sees for people he coaches is an opportunity just above their level, deliberately matched to their strengths, given at the right moment. Early career PMs should look for the kind of work people already praise them for; senior PMs often accelerate simply by chasing more of the work they are demonstrably great at.

Lenny brings new data from his forthcoming state of the tech job market report: PM roles are currently the most open of any role in tech, engineering openings are slowing, design is flat, and user research has only about a hundred open roles globally. Mansour's explanation starts with an experiment inside Atlassian. A year ago, when the narrative was that everyone would become an AI builder, Atlassian created squads where product managers and designers were moved onto teams with no PM or designer roles at all, and everyone built. In the first weeks a PM told him they had never moved faster. After about a month, the same PM reported that everyone had drifted back to their traditional jobs, because the team had realized throughput was not their problem. Direction was. Mansour wrote an internal post titled "Direct the ship, don't row it," and his reading of the hiring data is the same: as building gets cheaper, companies need more people steering, not more people rowing.

He reaches for an older lesson to explain why specialization survives. In the Balsamiq era, cheap mockup tools were supposed to liberate design, and Atlassian learned that PMs are some of the worst solution designers in the world, because access to a tool does not confer expertise in its domain. AI, he says, amplifies your strengths and amplifies your weaknesses equally. In his Venn diagram image, everyone's circle is getting bigger, but the circles are not compressing into one another, so specialists still set the quality bar in each craft.

Asked what PMs should actually do with AI, he refuses the spectrum from shipping production code to ignoring AI entirely. The starting point is diagnosing the team's context gap: if the gap is vision, prototyping may help clarify it; if the gap is market understanding, send agents out to do analysis with the team. Shipping code to production is very unlikely to be the bottleneck worth speeding up, because that part has become so easy, except in startups and zero to one teams where everyone does everything.

On hiring, three things are trending up. First, artist PMs, because when everyone has access to the same intelligence, taste and experience become the differentiators. Second, people who bring new ways of working with them, since the hardest problem in an established workforce is cultural change, and how a candidate works with AI reveals what culture they would add. Third, and most spicy in his telling, juniors. He spends time debunking the claim that companies no longer need to hire juniors because AI can do junior work. Established employees struggle to unlearn old ways of working, while juniors arrive knowing nothing but AI. His twelve year old son went from Scratch straight to vibe coding his own games and dismisses a weak model output that would once have taken days to build. Atlassian's own research finds juniors are substantially more likely to adopt new tools than seniors, roughly 38 percent on the measure he cites, while seniors are far better at spotting AI slop because they hold the domain expertise. The two groups are complements: in Atlassian's AI builder weeks, the younger staff show off demos and new tools while the seniors gravitate to evals and quality problems. Atlassian has deliberately overindexed on hiring juniors over seniors for this reason.

The artist streak, he argues, shows up early and is partly nature. Reviewing Loom recordings of take home exercises from incoming associate PMs, he could see one candidate lead with graphs, business case, and market size, and another lead with the end to end journey and the emotional connection the product might create, despite neither having domain experience. That connects to a puzzle in Lenny's data: design roles are flat even though experience should matter more than ever. Mansour's hypothesis is that the market simply has not had its aha moment on experience yet. Inside Atlassian, he says, design has never played a more important role in driving the roadmap in his 17 years.

He also defends starting with a solution, against the standard advice to start with the problem. When Atlassian put a general chat and create your own agents into Rovo three and a half years ago, skeptics asked why not just build the specific features people wanted. The general canvas turned out to be the fastest discovery tool: watching what customers did in chat, such as grouping sticky notes into themes on Confluence whiteboards, showed the team which use cases to productize natively. Nearly ten million agents now run in loops every month inside Jira and Confluence, and some patterns, like AI triage, graduate into built features while others stay as the infinite use case. He calls it paving the cow path. Product strategy, likewise, often starts from a solution: inventory the assets you already have, then ask what unique problems they can solve, the way Amazon's giant bookstore became an everything store.

The same pragmatism shapes his view of bolting AI onto existing products, a phrase he treats as a catchy headline rather than a real sin. For an established product, rewriting from scratch is usually the wrong move; the better path is to map the human workflows already modeled in the product and add agents to steps of them, exactly as Atlassian let customers attach agents to Jira workflow columns and cards. That is also how a company carries its existing customers from AI novice to AI native, instead of abandoning them for a reimagined product they never asked for.

On management, he pushes back twice. First, you do not need to become a manager to sit in executive strategy discussions: Atlassian has distinguished PMs and engineers contributing at the executive level on a dual track, and people move between managing and individual contributor work by season. Second, the most common mistake he sees in new PM managers is going hands off too quickly. The best managers he has worked with create space, but they also get in with the team and help work through outcomes, leading by example.

His advice on working with founders and executives returns to context. A visionary leader is often on chapter ten of the movie while the team is on chapter one and he is on chapter three. Rather than relaying orders without conviction, his job is to close that gap: assume the leader, who has usually thought about the problem far longer, is right, ask what led them there, and only then bring the team along. When he still disagrees after catching up, he says so, asks plainly who owns the decision, and will back a decision he argued against. To his own team he presents both sides of an argument, including the strongest case against his own position, because the best PMs can argue against themselves. He adds that PMs should ask the clarifying question leaders often skip, the one Dharmesh Shah's flash tags idea points at: is this an FYI, a suggestion, a recommendation, or a plea?

He closes by asking listeners to create opportunities for someone else, the way others created his, and the lightning round stays close to home: he reads fantasy with his kids, including The Prison Healer and the Wingfeather Saga, loved the film Project Hail Mary, and describes his family playing quiz games with Gemini inside Google Maps on a long drive as a small lesson in workflow proximity, AI succeeding because it sat inside the map he was already using. His personal motto is to decouple your identity from your job. Behind him hangs a quilt his wife stitched from his favorite Atlassian t-shirts, each one a story from the company's history, and he credits Atlassian's open by default culture, where executive reviews are recorded and searchable, with giving both humans and AI the context to decide well. His current hypothesis, now being tested by Atlassian data scientists, is that remote employees may get more out of AI than co-located ones, because remote work forces people to write and share digitally.

Open questions

  1. If direction rather than throughput is the binding constraint at scale, how should a company measure whether its PMs are actually raising decision making velocity, rather than just adding coordination?
  2. Mansour's bargain pairs juniors who adopt tools fast with seniors who can spot AI slop. As evals and AI review tools absorb more of that quality control, does the senior side of the bargain get weaker, and what replaces it?
  3. If his hypothesis holds and remote workers extract more from AI because they write more, is the right response to hire remote, or to make co-located teams document with the same discipline?
