Sourcery with Molly O'Shea · Apoorv Agrawal on Anthropic's $2 trillion IPO and the AI economy

Anthropic Hits $2 Trillion in Historic IPO | Altimeter's Four Seasons of AI

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, Apoorv Agrawal, the AI partner at Altimeter, returns to walk through Anthropic's public listing and the state of the AI economy, in a session titled Anthropic Hits $2 Trillion in Historic IPO | Altimeter's Four Seasons of AI. The conversation was recorded on the eve of the debut, and Agrawal frames it with the three questions he asks of the whole market: how big is this thing, where is all the money going, and what is it being used for.

Start with scale. Big tech capital spending on AI is running at about 860 billion dollars this year, more than tripled in two years and now rivaling United States defense spending. Measured against gross domestic product, the buildout is already past the railroad, oil and gas, telecom and shale booms of the last century. Yet the shape of the AI economy is inverted next to the cloud economy it follows. Salesforce, Adobe and ServiceNow together produce roughly 600 billion dollars of revenue supported by about 300 billion of infrastructure spending and 80 billion a year of semiconductors. The AI economy has models and applications producing only about 140 to 150 billion of revenue on top of a far larger semiconductor base. Agrawal reads that as the classic supercycle pattern from mobile: value lands first in chips, then in infrastructure, and finally in software and applications. The overall AI economy has grown about sixfold in two to three years, while the applications and models layer has grown roughly a hundredfold.

The use case has already shifted once. The chatbot era, dominated by knowledge retrieval and trip planning, gave way around the turn of the year to an agentic era of long running agents and agent swarms, aimed mostly at coding and automation across sales, marketing, support and investment teams. A third phase is forming, he thinks, around proactive agents, very long running agents, and larger models performing surgery on smaller ones. Underneath the shift sits what he calls a tale of two cities. OpenAI's own data shows its top tenth of customers growing usage seventeenfold while the median customer doubled, and Cursor's data shows its top tenth of users consuming two thirds of all tokens. The gap between frontier firms and everyone else is the opportunity: adoption is still in its first inning, and Fortune 500 customers spending more than 100,000 dollars a year on Anthropic say it is less than a tenth of a percent of their total expenses.

Then the S-1. Agrawal's framework for the year is four seasons: Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceX and Google, with open source as the evergreen fifth. Anthropic's filing, he says, has been a total treat. The company has grown tenfold in each of the last three years, did about 17 billion dollars of revenue in the third quarter, and counts some 12,000 customers paying more than 100,000 dollars a year and about 1,900 paying more than a million, with net revenue retention of 900 percent. It is, he argues, the fastest scale up in history, achieved with no customer concentration to speak of.

His favorite part is the efficiency. Anthropic burned less than 10 billion dollars of cash to reach a revenue run rate of roughly 70 billion. The compute cost of serving a dollar of revenue has fallen from 88 cents to 15 cents, which is how the company holds margins around 85 percent, and it now monetizes its scarce compute at roughly 60 dollars a watt, up from about 16. The S-1 describes a company orchestrating heterogeneous compute across TPUs, Trainiums and Nvidia GPUs to squeeze out that efficiency. On price, the rumored range of 1.5 to 2 trillion dollars works out to 11 to 14 times forward revenue, against his estimate of 140 to 150 billion dollars of revenue next year. That is not a heroic multiple, he says, and he expects a very successful IPO from what he calls the private Mag 7.

Where the money goes is mostly data centers: more than half into the IT equipment, the chips and memory, and the rest into getting the buildings themselves running. The United States should end the year with about 35 to 40 gigawatts of compute capacity online, and forecasts call for another 40 to 50 gigawatts next year, but Agrawal expects permitting and regulation to cut that, perhaps by 10 to 15 gigawatts. A shortfall would entrench the hyperscalers, who hold the largest pools of capacity and sit at the bottleneck where compute is bought and sold, while a startup seventeenth in line for capacity faces something existential.

Of the two frontier labs, he sees Anthropic far out in the lead and OpenAI running neck and neck behind it. OpenAI has shown resilience and fragility at once: ChatGPT is past 1.2 billion users, the company went into code red at the end of last year, and its Astra models and Codex product drove a third quarter that ended September at about a 50 billion dollar revenue run rate, ahead of its own year end guidance. SpaceX, meanwhile, has quietly become an AI infrastructure company: about 60 billion dollars of revenue run rate, half of it from its AI unit, a third from Starlink and the rest from launch. SpaceX rents compute at spot prices around 50 dollars a watt, roughly double long term lease rates, and still finds buyers, because Anthropic monetizes compute at 50 to 100 dollars a watt. SpaceX can recoup its capital spending in under a year where a typical neocloud needs two to three, and it is guiding from about 3 gigawatts of compute this year to roughly 13 next year, with revenue targets of 100 billion of run rate by the end of this year and half a trillion or more by the end of next.

Google is his other tale of two cities. Search produces about 250 billion dollars of revenue run rate growing at 12 percent, while the cloud business is half that size at about 120 billion but growing at 103 percent a year, eight times faster. The cloud business carries the TPU franchise with it: Anthropic is a TPU customer, and Anthropic has a commitment with Broadcom to lease TPUs as well.

Open source, the evergreen season, should settle at roughly 20 to 30 percent of all AI spending by his mental model, and the place the cash register rings is the inference layer: Baseten, Fireworks, Modal and their peers. The best open model today is about where the closed frontier was six months ago, which is good enough for the AI native companies, Cursor, Decagon and Harvey among them, that want to own their intelligence rather than rent it. Some 35 companies run 99 percent of all inference, he notes, the five big labs plus about 30 AI native startups. OpenAI has even partnered with Baseten so large customers can burn down their OpenAI credits on open source models, a sign, to Agrawal, of a lab rising to become a platform. Baseten itself is moving up the stack with routing, sandboxes and platform tools, one of several roads that end with companies looking like hyperscalers.

Asked who wins, he answers that every layer wins: chips, data centers, clouds and models. The real question is relative scale. So far it has been a game of emperors, won by the biggest balance sheets, but he is hopeful for the application layer as compute gets cheaper. The growth benchmarks have been rewritten in the process: tenfold growth at single digit billions of revenue, as Cursor and Baseten have done, tripling at double digit billions, as OpenAI has, and doubling at triple digit billions, as Google's cloud is doing. The old software standard of triple, triple, double, double is no longer best in class. When he evaluates startups himself, though, he starts with the founding team, calling talent the leading indicator and the numbers a lagging one, and pointing to founders like Bob McGrew of Arda as the pattern he backs.

The biggest emerging market he sees is cybersecurity. Software engineering is still the largest AI workload and the quant firms spend heavily, but cyber is a cat and mouse game in which offensive actors now hold a toolkit that can check every door and window for the one unlocked entry: one research team recently found a Chrome vulnerability that could hand an attacker control of a computer from a single clicked link. Defense will have to match fire with fire, he argues, and because the edge from frontier models is so high, the frontier labs themselves, wrapped in harnesses built by specialists, are likely to serve much of that demand with continuous testing rather than an annual audit.

He closes where he started. Asked what he is most looking forward to, he says he cannot speak for the next 12 months but he can for the next 12 days: the Anthropic IPO. It is the best business he has had the good fortune to study, he says, an anthropologist of the markets, owning the pun when O'Shea catches it. At the contemplated pricing he expects a phenomenal success in the short term and the long term, with the caveat that the waters can always turn choppy.

Open questions

  1. Will Anthropic's debut at 11 to 14 times forward revenue hold up in public markets, and does it reprice the rest of the private giants the way Agrawal expects?
  2. If United States capacity additions fall 10 to 15 gigawatts short next year, do the hyperscalers gain more from the bottleneck than they lose from their own slowed buildouts, and which smaller clouds and startups get squeezed out?
  3. Will open source really settle at 20 to 30 percent of AI spending with the inference layer capturing the margin, or will the frontier labs absorb that layer as they grow from labs into platforms?

AI News & Strategy Daily | Nate B Jones · Nate B. Jones on Anthropic's 1,000-agent run and the setup gap

Anthropic Ran 1,000 AI Agents For 8 Days. Yours Still Needs The Same Thing Explained Again.

On AI News & Strategy Daily | Nate B Jones, "Anthropic Ran 1,000 AI Agents For 8 Days. Yours Still Needs The Same Thing Explained Again." opens with a gap. On November 2, OpenAI reported that AI sessions had run for hundreds of hours finding and fixing problems in its new system for managing code. Anthropic reported something similar: more than 1,000 agents working for 8 days to build a prototype operating system, and Google's latest Gemini has worked for days on hard engineering problems. Most people's agents, by contrast, still need the same thing explained over and over. Jones argues the difference is not the model. It is how the work is set up.

The cost of getting it wrong is concrete. An AI that works all night can leave you with a full day of cleanup: you still check the price, still fix what it promised, still re-explain a decision you made yesterday. When an agent keeps following a decision you no longer remember making, you pay twice, once in tokens for the run and again in time fixing a result you did not want.

He confesses his own version of the failure. Building a set of questions to test AI against his own work, he had Codex gather more than 339 sources and write over a thousand questions. An agent then got stuck repeating itself while synchronizing his files, and meanwhile what he actually needed had changed: he wanted the 50 most valuable answers, with stronger evidence, not a thousand questions. He stopped the run. The research was all there, he says. The problem was never data availability. It was that the assignment was not structured for a long run, and the learning he picked up along the way never got passed to the next agent.

The first fix is context, and he means more by it than facts. What has to reach the agent is the decisions made along the way, the examples developed while thinking the problem through, and the instructions as they evolve when you change your mind. He notes the labs onboard new employees this way, telling them to ask Codex or Claude so they absorb a project's accumulated context. Agents ramp up the same way, and a project should be treated as an evolving record a newcomer, human or AI, can read, not a one-time snapshot of your opinion. Being explicit about which phase the work is in, discovery or refinement, is part of the same discipline.

His central prescription is to treat project information like code. Instructions, checks for mistakes, and evolving decisions belong in a repo where an agent can find and traverse them, even when the work has nothing to do with software. His example is a client proposal: you agreed on an offer and a price, but the contractor's AI drafts from an old proposal and a bad price slips into a document that otherwise looks fine. Versioning errors like that creep in wherever information is not kept with code-like discipline. There is a reason the fit is natural, he adds: models are trained with reinforcement learning on verifiable domains like code, so knowledge work shaped like code is knowledge work they can navigate.

That leads to his argument about skills. Teams should put their skills, the recipes for using their data correctly and producing work that matches their standards, inside the shared knowledge base itself, rather than letting every person write and install their own. Home-brewed personal skills produce slightly different agents that often stop referring to the same data, and he is blunt about the culture around personal setups, calling much of it skill theater and mini-me theater. A skill built for the team is a set of instructions any model can apply consistently to the same body of work, whether that is a PRD, a proposal, or a deck. Shared skills can even govern the workflow itself, prompting a human to do an edit pass when a new version lands. The point of the central structure is accountability: when the deliverable goes to a client, "the AI wrote that" is not an answer. You wrote it. You own it.

A shared repo also makes roles and permissions practical. A team can spell out what a product manager's agent, an engineer's agent, and a contractor's agent are each allowed to change, so merely being able to view the work does not mean being able to overwrite it. Jones is clear that a markdown file describing roles is not the whole solution, and he believes in real guardrails and identity systems. But for teams that cannot afford the enterprise version, written roles plus human oversight get most of the way there, in a form that is simple and scalable.

Where many agents touch the same work, collisions are the next problem. Agents left to swarm a shared space without structure produce the knowledge-work equivalent of race conditions. The remedy he borrows from engineering is separation of concerns: each piece of work gets its own page and folder, versions are forked rather than overwritten, so an agent starts a version 15 deck instead of quietly reusing version 14, and the skills in the repo train agents to hand work off explicitly, telling the next person it is ready for review.

How should a team judge whether any of this is working? Not by time saved, which he calls the cheap answer. The better tests are whether you are interrupted less by things you do not care about, whether collisions fall, whether you explain yourself less because the agent is working from the team's shared objectives, and whether the questions the agent surfaces get more productive. An agent that never asks you anything is not a success sign. It usually means you are still driving every last thing yourself.

His closing advice starts small. Pick one job you keep explaining and correcting, give the AI real, current information and saved instructions, and try it in a fresh chat to see whether it can learn. Nobody needs a CTO's permission to run that experiment. But wherever you can, he urges, bias toward the team-shaped version, and he asks viewers to share the video with their team leaders: how an organization structures its data is a decision that shapes the productivity of the whole org, and it deserves to be made intentionally rather than by accident.

Open questions

  1. Jones attributes the long-run gap mainly to structure rather than model capability. How would a team actually measure that split before committing to a centralized repo, and what evidence would show the bottleneck sits elsewhere?
  2. His collision discipline imports forking and separation of concerns from software. Where does that analogy strain for knowledge work whose pieces overlap and cannot be cleanly partitioned into pages and folders?
  3. A shared repo of decisions, examples, and skills only stays useful if someone keeps it current as decisions change. Who owns that maintenance in practice, and how quickly does run quality decay when it lapses?

a16z News · Moses Sternstein's charts on Gen Z, the FAA ground stop, and market uncertainty

Charts of the Week: There is No Gen Z

In a16z News, Moses Sternstein's latest Charts of the Week ranges across three stories: an Electric Twin report arguing that Gen Z is too fragmented to count as a single generation, Navan data on the recent FAA ground stop and the AI agent that handled the fallout, and Stanley Druckenmiller telling a TomorrowX audience that he has never had less clarity about the next twelve months.

The first section takes up "The Great Unbundling," a report from Electric Twin on Gen Z. Its premise is not simply that young people differ from older generations, which would be unremarkable, but that they are configured differently from one another. Rather than one Gen Z with a fairly predictable bundle of preferences, there may be many Gen Zs, tribes that have less in common with each other than generational cohorts usually do. The proposed cause is social media. Earlier generations drew on something like a single source of truth for their information mix, with predictable partisan and geographic markers. Zoomers mix and match across channels, forums and influencers, so their preference bundles are sorted by information diet more than by politics or nationality.

The charts build the case step by step. A first chart compares where each generation gets its content: Zoomers favor YouTube, Instagram and TikTok, while Boomers lean relatively heavily on Facebook. A second chart, this one from Pew, tracks how news sources have shifted since 2020. Facebook and Twitter were already common news sources six years ago, but TikTok, Instagram and, to a lesser extent, YouTube and Reddit have risen sharply since. TikTok shows the most dramatic climb, from about 20 percent of users in 2020 to more than half by 2026. Sternstein's reading is that adults as a group did not so much change their habits as the adult population came to include more Zoomers and their habits with them.

The next chart tests how nationally recognizable each generation's politics are. When participants from four countries answered a set of political questions, a model could guess a Boomer's nationality from their politics about 42 percent of the time. For Zoomers, the success rate fell to 29 percent, only modestly better than chance. A related exercise imagines each person walking into a room of 99 others and looking for a perfect match in views. Boomers from the United States, the United Kingdom, France and Germany find like-with-like matches about 15 to 20 percent of the time among their own countrymen. Zoomers top out at about 9.6 percent, in the United Kingdom, with the whole range running only from 6.7 to 9.6 percent, so nationality barely helps and matches are scarce everywhere. A final cut shows that a Zoomer is about as likely to find a perfect match among 99 other Zoomers as among 99 people of any generation, while Boomers match at higher rates across every generation. The conclusion: Gen Z is less a generation than a collection of microcultures organized by online habits, which is awkward for anyone in politics, marketing or institutional trust trying to work out what "Gen Z" wants.

The second section looks back at the widespread FAA outages of the previous week, which grounded planes across the country, through data from Navan, the business travel platform. When the ground stop was announced at 3:54 in the afternoon, hotel and car bookings spiked immediately, reaching as high as double their normal volume and staying elevated into the next day, as stranded travelers found beds and wheels. The quieter story is a win for AI. Help chat volume exploded too, with conversations with Navan's AI support agent, Ava, rising nearly fivefold. Rather than degrading under the load, Ava's resolution rate held at or around its pre-panic level of about 80 percent, the kind of surge-proof customer service that Sternstein notes was not possible before AI.

The remaining travel charts put the disruption in context. Bureau of Transportation Statistics data, which does not yet include the recent grounding, shows the on-time flight rate has fallen by about five percentage points since 2018, with most of the shift driven by delays, though 2026 has also seen a noticeable rise in cancellations. Delays cluster in the afternoon and evening: flights departing between 3 and 9 in the evening post the highest share of late arrivals, since a later departure leaves more room for an earlier disruption to cascade. Chicago O'Hare is the standout airport, accounting for about 6 percent of flights but close to 8 percent of delays and cancellations, with New York, Dallas and, to a lesser extent, Washington also punching above their weight. On severely disrupted flights, Chicago routes dominate the leaderboard for cancellations and delays of sixty minutes or more, although Cleveland to Washington is actually the worst single route for severe disruption rate, and on troubled pairings the leg out of Chicago is almost uniformly worse than the leg in.

The third section reports from Gavin Baker's TomorrowX conference, where the moment that resonated most came from Stanley Druckenmiller. He told the audience he cannot remember a period with less clarity on where things will be in twelve months, and that he is simply not playing: neither long nor short, just taking a break. Over the longer haul he was optimistic that AI will be a game changer for medicine, cybersecurity and the broader business of selling outcomes, but on the next twelve months he professes not to know, and so he waits.

Sternstein connects that stance to a measurement problem at the heart of the AI trade. Public companies report mostly anecdotes, private companies stay mostly out of sight, and investors squint at interesting but wildly incomplete datasets. Databricks has begun publishing token data, and it underlines the confusion. On the open versus closed question, Databricks shows proprietary models accounting for about 70 percent of token volume, while OpenRouter shows almost the exact opposite. On spending, Ramp's per-employee monthly AI spend runs higher than Databricks' figures but accelerates more slowly, except at the top centile. Some of the gap is methodological: Ramp aggregates by firm and divides by headcount, while Databricks counts individual human users, and a top centile user inside a firm may be a very different creature from the average user at a top centile firm. The broader point is that nobody quite knows what is being spent or what the demand signals mean six months out.

Against that fog, sentiment has flipped fast. The last few weeks brought one of the largest hedge fund tech selloffs ever, with net selling of information technology stocks a more than four sigma event and the long-short ratio dropping from a post-ZIRP high to nearly a series low within a few months, nearly half of that drop recent. In Bank of America's manager survey, a record 38 percent of respondents said companies are overinvesting, and a majority expected a capital spending cut in the next six months, up from about 30 percent a month ago and about 12 percent two months ago. Sternstein offers no view on who is right. His closing observation is that the events between September and October hardly seem numerous or substantive enough to justify so profound a shift, and that when investors do not know, vibes and speculation predominate. Right now the vibes are bad, and a boat rocked by uncertainty can rock back just as quickly.

Open questions

  1. If Gen Z really is a bundle of microcultures sorted by information diet rather than a generation with shared preferences, what replaces generational targeting for political campaigns and marketers who still plan around it?
  2. Will AI support agents that hold their resolution rates through a fivefold surge, like Navan's Ava did during the ground stop, become the expected standard for travel and other disruption-prone industries?
  3. If the data on AI spending stays this contradictory, with Databricks and OpenRouter showing opposite open-versus-closed splits, what evidence would actually settle whether companies are overinvesting before the capex cuts the survey majority expects?

a16z · Martin Casado and Anish Acharya with David Heinemeier Hansson on agents, Linux, and Omarchy

Why AI Has DHH Working 16-Hour Days

On a16z, Why AI Has DHH Working 16-Hour Days is a conversation between a16z general partners Martin Casado and Anish Acharya and David Heinemeier Hansson, the creator of Ruby on Rails and co-founder of 37signals. Hansson has spent roughly thirty years building internet software, from Basecamp and Hey to four books on how companies should work. His current project is Omarchy, a Linux distribution he describes as the first native operating system for the age of agents, and the conversation is built around his claim that agents have reopened a window in computing that had been closed for decades.

Hansson's own path to Linux was personal before it was strategic. He used Macs from 2001 until, in his telling, Apple grew addicted to taking thirty percent of other people's business and stopped caring about its relationship with developers. He switched to a Linux desktop about two and a half years ago. But he is clear that grievances like his would never have moved large numbers of people. What will move them, he argues, is that agents have made Linux's historic weaknesses into strengths.

The core of his argument is a reversal. Everything that made Linux hard to use, the heavy terminal, the piles of config files, the inscrutable error messages, turns out to be exactly what agents want: a command line for everything, predictable files they can edit, and error messages full of detail. An operating system built for forty years around mouse clicks cannot be driven that way. He points to a simple example: remapping workspace hotkeys on a Mac means clicking through a settings app one field at a time, while on Omarchy a user can simply ask an agent to change anything about the system, from the start menu to the calendar, and watch it happen in seconds or minutes. Apple, he says, is moving the other way, adding warning popups about letting an agent like Claude reach your files, and he warns that Apple risks becoming the AOL of the agent era, a walled garden in a world that wants the open internet.

Omarchy itself is being built the way he says the future will be built. For the past five or six months the system has been developed by agents, and Hansson says he has not written a single line of its code by hand, though he has written hundreds of thousands of lines through agents. The current version took about two and a half months of part-time work from him and one main partner, yet it competes, in his telling, with the output of thousands of programmers at the largest software companies. Hundreds of thousands of people have switched to it from Windows and Mac. His motto for what comes next is borrowed from an old song: first we take Manhattan, then we take Berlin, meaning the desktop first and mobile after.

Mobile is where the argument gets its sharpest edge. Hansson says the window for a third operating system is open for the first time in twenty five years, the last opening being the launch of OS X in 2001. What killed every challenger since, including Microsoft's expensive attempt, was the lack of applications. That lock in is dissolving, he argues, because software has become cheap to produce. He tells of the one application that still kept a Mac in his life, Adobe Lightroom, with all his camera presets and plugins, until a member of the Omarchy community rebuilt it in about two weeks, better than the original. If any missing app can be recreated in days, and many apps no longer need to exist because an agent can do the work directly, the duopoly's main defense is gone.

He gives his own routine as evidence for the second half of that claim. Setting up the nonprofit Omarchy Foundation, which has raised roughly twenty three million dollars in donations and tokens, required many contracts, and every one of them was drafted by an agent. He reviews them as markdown, approves them, and the agent signs and sends them. No word processor is involved. Photo editing is going the same way: rather than spend hundreds of hours learning Photoshop, people will ask an agent to remove the stranger on the left and make the picture look great. Whole classes of applications, he says, were really professions, and agents are absorbing the profession along with the app.

Speed is the multiplier he keeps returning to. Today's systems run at rates that make software feel sluggish rather than alive, but he cites a recent Fireworks demonstration at fifteen hundred tokens a second, where games were prompted into existence and then mutated live, lasers and bombs added on request. That, he says, is a preview of software that changes as you use it, and he asks what happens when models run at a hundred thousand or a million tokens a second. Development timelines are already compressing from years to days, weeks, or at worst months, and several new model generations have arrived just in the span of this one project. Anyone who still believes there is a moat in source code, even millions of lines of it, is delusional.

The title of the episode comes from the section on taste. Hansson is famous as a craftsman, and one host presses him on whether anything of that survives when the code writes itself. His answer is that craft matters more, because the barrier between noticing an imperfection and fixing it has collapsed. Most builders, he says, get their satisfaction from the first eighty percent and lose interest in the janky last twenty. His own reward sits in the final two percent, and now nothing stops him from chasing it, which is why the author of a book arguing that work does not have to be crazy is working sixteen hour days. Agents give him the productive capacity of a company of sixty thousand people with none of the coordination cost, no committees and no HR. He quotes Shopify's Tobi Lütke calling the return to a normal office, after a weekend of this, like coming out of hyperdrive.

He is equally keen to puncture the idea that this takes serious money. Despite joking that he could buy unlimited tokens, he runs on three Claude subscriptions, and most weeks a single two hundred dollar top tier plan covers everything he does with a frontier model. He hit his limits in only about two weeks out of the last three months. The constraint, for now, is not cash. It is the human in the loop, because he has not yet found a setup where the design comes out perfect without a person's eye on it.

That human role may not last, and he says so plainly. For software development, he argues, superintelligence has effectively arrived: no programmer he has known matches the breadth of what the systems can do combined with their pace, even if individual experts still win in narrow areas. He thinks it is entirely possible that by next year there will be true superintelligence, better than humans at everything, including polishing the perfect computer. He contrasts that with a recent visit to robotics companies in China, where the people working on actuators and mechanics put the same milestone four, five, or ten years out. His own posture is to savor the brief moment when the human is still additive, and to prefer what he calls AI euphoria, excitement about what can be built now, over the AI psychosis that comes from extrapolating several model generations ahead.

On the frontier labs versus open source, he refuses to pick a side and insists on both. Open weights are essential, he says, to keep the whole game honest, and he compares the present moment to the encryption wars of the nineties, when banning public key cryptography would have crippled the internet. A government enforced duopoly, or even a purely commercial one, would be catastrophic, and he points to how Google and Meta captured nearly all of online advertising's value once their duopoly set in. At the same time, he cheers the frontier labs and is glad the lead sits where it does, provided competition keeps them from one day charging five hundred dollars per million tokens simply because everyone would pay. The open models are closing in regardless: he estimates the leading open releases run perhaps six or seven months behind the frontier on some tasks, and a desktop box like a DGX Spark can now run a model that is genuinely useful, something he admits he dismissed as pointless only months earlier.

His ambition has inflated along with the tooling. The project began as a nicer computer for himself and a few friends, grew to tens of thousands of users, and now he states the goal openly: within about three years, he expects Linux led by Omarchy to be bigger than macOS, which he notes has only about one hundred eighty million users worldwide. He would not put majority odds on doing it within twelve months, but he would not rule it out either. If everyone could do what he is doing now, he argues, annual growth could plausibly run three, four, five, or even ten percent higher, because the future he is living is, in the William Gibson line he quotes, already here and just not evenly distributed.

The closing stretch is about culture. Hansson says every programmer he deeply respects, figures like John Carmack, Tim Sweeney, and Mitchell Hashimoto, has leaned fully into these tools, while many mid career programmers feel a real anxiety that the skills they labored to earn may stop mattering. He asks for sympathy for that anxiety, comparing it to what autoworkers and the original Luddites faced, while insisting there is no road back. What excites him most is the return of the pseudonymous hacker: Omarchy's core team includes contributors who work under handles, including the creator of the Quickshell shell, much as the nineties ran on BBS handles and demo parties. That demo culture is baked into the product, whose screensaver runs ASCII art through forty demo effects in the style of a 1995 demo party intro, and whose whole aesthetic rejects the polished, characterless computer in favor of something bespoke and a little mysterious. He ran an elite bulletin board system out of his bedroom at fourteen, he recalls, and his closing thought is simple: the hackers are back, and computers should feel cool again.

Open questions

  1. If the app gap was the real lock in behind the mobile duopoly, can an agent built ecosystem actually produce a third phone platform before Apple and Google adapt their own systems to agents?
  2. Hansson expects the human eye to remain necessary for the final two percent only briefly, perhaps a year. What happens to craftspeople whose value sits exactly there once models close that gap too?
  3. Will open weights stay close enough to the frontier to discipline prices and power, or does the economics of frontier training pull away faster than open releases can follow?

The Riemann Hypothesis has been solved!! Anthropic's Levent Alpöge announces lean verified GRH!

On Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander, “The Riemann Hypothesis has been solved!! Anthropic's Levent Alpöge announces lean verified GRH!” is a video recorded at an airport, where Alexander, a mathematician and philosopher, reads aloud a short document posted on the personal website of Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic. In that document, Alpöge claims that he and the AI system Claude have discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, the most famous open problem in mathematics, which concerns the distribution of the prime numbers and has stood for more than 150 years. It is a claim, announced by its author and celebrated by Alexander. Nothing in the video amounts to an independent check of it, and it has not been peer reviewed.

The claim is larger than the Riemann Hypothesis alone. Alpöge writes that the method easily extends much further, resolving the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and that with more time he expects the Extended Riemann Hypothesis too. He says the arguments have also been formalized without assumptions, meaning written in the Lean proof assistant, whose software checks each step mechanically. He will present only the Lean formalization, he writes, rather than a conventional paper: the argument is incredibly long, and turning it into a huge document of sufficient detail would risk posting what he calls a massive unreadable travesty.

The document is also an account of how the work felt from the inside. Alpöge says the problem was his secret ambition since his student days, and that he spent years learning every tool he might one day recognize in it. He describes guiding the collaboration toward the right situations, and then watching Claude outpace him near the finish line. Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled, he writes. Alexander delights in all of this, and in Alpöge's hope that everyone who loves mathematics will get to share the experience.

The mathematics itself is only sketched. Alpöge describes the argument as a descendant of Stepanov's argument, which settled the analogue of the Riemann Hypothesis over function fields. The obstacle, he writes, is multiplicity, which is hard to find over the integers, something like differentiating whole numbers. New Fourier analytic ideas from a toy setting led them to chain theta-lifts, each lift mimicking multiplicity, and for the van Mangoldt sum of length X, Claude suggested a chain of length the square root of X. A rough timeline in the document runs from October 6 to November 16, 2026, through automorphic determinants, iterated theta-lifts, and a seesaw identity. Alexander, reading on no sleep after a day of travel, repeatedly admits that he is lost.

Alpöge is candid about his own position. He writes that this age has put him in the extraordinary situation of presenting the world with a complete argument before having mastered the massive neighborhood of mathematics around it, and that he personally has little mastery of automorphic forms on higher rank groups, one of the argument's ingredients. He expects that he has baked strange and suboptimal choices into the proof, and that his intuition insists it will be massively simplified, likely immediately, as other mathematicians dig in, following the pattern of other recent breakthroughs.

He also addresses the timing head on. The work finished late on a Sunday night, he writes, and he knows the precise timing looks horrible: there is an obvious cynical view connecting the announcement to Anthropic's recent initial public offering, and he says that view is false, and that he is in circumstances where it is obviously false. His leadership and the few trusted mathematicians he consulted, he writes, held extremely similar views on the right things to do, and he could not live with waiting any longer.

Alexander then reads through the Hacker News discussion of the announcement. One commenter calls Alpöge a supremely qualified mathematician, one of the best in the world at guiding AI toward new discoveries, and says that if he says he has Riemann, then he has Riemann. Another, initially anxious about whether the claim holds, is answered by a reply pointing out that the Lean formalization is already linked. A third compares the steps inside the proof to the Taniyama-Shimura conjecture's role in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. Alexander's own reaction is euphoric throughout: viewers, he says, will tell their great-great-grandchildren that they were there when the Riemann Hypothesis was solved, and he calls the moment one dawn across science of something far greater.

What the video does not contain is any scrutiny of the proof itself. No mathematician in it reports having checked the formalization, and no journal or referee has weighed in. The claim therefore stands exactly where Alpöge left it: a finished Lean formalization, which in principle anyone can check mechanically, attached to one of the largest claims in the history of mathematics.

Open questions

  1. Does the published Lean formalization actually compile end to end, with no assumptions beyond the standard ones? Independent experts checking that is the first test the claim has to pass, and until they report, the proof remains an announcement rather than a result.
  2. If the formalization checks out, who produces the human readable version of the argument, and how long does it take the field to understand, simplify, and teach a proof whose own author says he has not mastered the mathematics around it?
  3. Alpöge says the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis falls to the same method and expects the Extended Riemann Hypothesis to follow with more time. Will those formalizations appear, and how will institutions such as the Clay Mathematics Institute, which offers a prize for the Riemann Hypothesis, treat a machine checked claim of this kind?
