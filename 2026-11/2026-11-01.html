Anthony Pompliano · The six billion dollar Binance hack and bitcoin as the purest AI trade

Bitcoin Survives Biggest Crypto Hack In History?!

On Anthony Pompliano, the Pomp Podcast episode "Bitcoin Survives Biggest Crypto Hack In History?!" is a conversation with Jordi Visser, a veteran macro investor with more than thirty years of experience and the author of the VisserLabs Substack. Recorded for Halloween, the conversation works through the six billion dollar Binance hack, the Anthropic S-1 filing, Microsoft's buyout offer to sixty thousand employees, Eli Lilly's record quarter, and Visser's running argument that bitcoin is the purest AI trade.

Binance was hacked for six billion dollars, with rumors of North Korean involvement and reports that the attackers got in through an engineer's laptop. Bitcoin sold off only about five percent. Binance says it will backstop user losses, and the stablecoin issuers, Tether, Circle, and World Liberty Financial among them, almost immediately froze multiple billions of the stolen funds.

Pompliano presses on what that safety net costs. A hack used to be binary: either you were hacked or you were not. Freezes and backstops soften the bearer asset ethos at crypto's core, and he asks whether that pushes investors toward ETFs and platforms that promise protection. He also notes the flip side: ten years ago a hack this size might have knocked twenty to forty percent off bitcoin, and Binance's response reads almost like marketing for why users should trust the platform.

Visser treats AI driven attack as the new background condition rather than a crypto story. Businesses he knows have been hacked and forced to reboot, and he dates the current wave to around June: a Revolut incident, an apparel company, healthcare firms. Once agents can crack into systems through a single laptop, every company is exposed. What he will watch is the recovery story: how much gets frozen, how much is recovered, and how involved law enforcement becomes.

His broader frame is maturation. He compares crypto now to the NASDAQ of 1999, a new electronic system that broke under its own growing pains and had to earn trust incident by incident. The difference today is that the banks are inside the tent, building tokenization and promoting bitcoin to clients, with fiduciary responsibilities attached, where in the Jamie Dimon era they stood outside calling it garbage. Incidents like this hack, and the quantum computing claims now circulating, including companies publicly saying they will break into bitcoin by 2028, force the security work to happen faster, at firms like BlackRock as much as at exchanges.

The muted price reaction also needs context, he argues. Google fell thirteen percent this week and Intel twenty five, while Lilly rose ten percent in two days; trillion dollar companies now swing like this routinely, and bitcoin's volatility has run below the big AI names all year. He does not expect the upside to be muted in the same way, because the buyers who would drive it still barely own any: institutional and wealthy investors are largely absent, and he expects tokenization, pointing to a DTCC announcement this week, plus the agent commerce Stripe is seeing, to supply the fundamentals that carry the next leg.

On price, he describes a market stuck in mud. Bitcoin rallied from about fifty nine thousand to the eighty seven to eighty eight thousand area, and he expects a test of the two hundred day moving average, possibly a brief pierce below it, after the seventy three to seventy four thousand zone was nearly touched overnight. For six weeks bitcoin has tracked the semiconductor stocks almost one for one, and a mix of oil and ten year yields as well. Money this year had to be made inside short windows; the rest has been back and forth.

Pompliano draws the parallel to four Novembers ago, when the FTX collapse marked the bear market bottom. In his firefight metaphor, the other side had ten bullets and has fired nine; the hack was the ninth, and the midterms are the tenth. Once that last one is spent, the noise clears. Visser is warming to the idea: outside one small Micron purchase in the summer, he had stayed out, but over the last week and a half he has been buying AI names on panic days, three or four entries made only into fear.

Anthropic's S-1, released this week, landed better than feared. Visser says you can read it to a revenue run rate above one hundred billion dollars if the October figures after the Opus 5.5 launch hold. He lingers on one disclosure: about two hundred fifty of the Fortune 500 are Anthropic customers, while Anthropic's share of those companies' total expenses is under one percent, a measure of how early enterprise adoption still is. The IPO itself still has to get done.

The story he calls the week's most important and least covered is Oracle's investor day. Oracle, whose debt was the first to wobble when this period started, laid out that it will not need to raise much money between now and 2030, after raising more than sixty billion dollars since last September. The AI build out has been financed by a concentrated capital raise, more than a trillion dollars of equity and debt this year across roughly five names, and that issuance competing with the Treasury is, in his telling, a main reason long term rates pushed higher. With real ten year yields around two hundred fifty basis points, the drag has hit crypto, gold, and the AI trade alike.

Underneath, breadth has quietly healed. The S&P 500 is roughly flat for the month, yet nine of eleven sectors are up, and about three hundred fifty of its five hundred stocks gained at least two percent against only seventy down that much. Tariff effects roll off next year's comparisons, and Goldman Sachs expects roughly a one hundred basis point drop in core PCE on base effects alone, with memory prices and oil already flattening on a year over year basis. His candidate catalyst is the November 4 quarterly refunding announcement: credible speculation, echoed by Citibank, says the Treasury could cancel long end supply and shift issuance to the front end, not yield curve control but an admission that long yields should not go higher. Ten year yields have already slipped below their fifty day moving average.

On the bubble debate, Pompliano argues the healthy pattern is exactly what is visible: revenue, profits, and earnings rising while valuations cool, which makes cooled off prices better entries rather than a bursting bubble. SpaceX grew revenue one hundred sixty percent year over year. Visser blames social media for the missing middle in the argument, and notes the split underneath the indexes: the big SaaS names such as Salesforce are down on the year, and the four hyperscalers as a group are roughly flat.

Microsoft's buyout offer is where the labor story becomes concrete. Sixty thousand people is twenty seven percent of a workforce of about two hundred twenty three thousand, against eight thousand in the previous round. At roughly four hundred thousand dollars per employee fully loaded, full take up would save about twenty four billion dollars a year, every year, and return headcount to 2020 levels while revenue has grown about seventy percent. Visser calls it a labor versus capital story with a political edge: employees pay tax rates near fifty percent while corporations pay far less, so every displaced worker is lost government revenue, and affordability is becoming the defining issue on both sides of the Atlantic. It is also a statement of confidence that AI can cover the gap, at five to seven times the scale of the last round, and one more reason he gives for bitcoin benefiting from the shift.

Pompliano offers his own company as the same story at small scale. Its compute bill was five hundred fifty thousand dollars a month in March 2026 and is down by half; he hopes to move off frontier models entirely, which he puts at a ninety seven percent saving per token. With ten people total, the company shipped five separate products or features in five days this week, and he points to SpaceX's Grok shipping fifty to one hundred features a week. Against private market rumors, Cognition and Ramp both talked about at forty billion dollars and up, he finds public valuations look rational. Adoption is the constraint: more Americans spend money on sports gambling than on AI, and he puts real usage at one to two percent of people.

Trust is the brake on the consumer side. Visser cites William Mougayar's idea of trust as an asset: every hack, Binance included, pushes trust down for everything at once, so individuals will adopt slowly, will probably never pay, and will be served by free tiers that keep improving. Workers are a different case; a young person who refuses the tools will struggle to be hired, because companies that forbid them will not stay in business. He notes the cost of being weak in this market: junk rated borrowers now pay about sixteen percent.

Eli Lilly supplied the week's earnings spectacle. Revenue came in at twenty four point four billion dollars, up thirty nine percent year over year against expectations of twenty two point six billion, with volume up fifty three percent, prices down fifteen percent, and gross margin at eighty three percent. Non-GAAP earnings per share beat expectations by more than a dollar. Mounjaro brought in about ten billion dollars, up sixty five percent, and Zepbound about five billion, up fifty percent, and Lilly raised its 2026 revenue guidance to ninety one to ninety two billion dollars from eighty five to eighty seven. The stock hit all time highs, up ten percent in two days.

Visser has argued for years that Lilly will become the biggest company in the world within five years. It sits ninth now, closing on the names just ahead of it, Tesla and Meta among them, on its way to a tenth straight positive year. His case rests on the spending being guaranteed: healthcare demand is non-cyclical, and the obesity drugs have reached only about ten percent of diagnosed patients in the United States and about two percent globally, with an oral version still to come. Around that cash cow sit options on dementia, oncology, and immunology. The contrast he draws is with Novo Nordisk, which paid its GLP-1 windfall out in dividends; Lilly reinvested, in the TuneLab platform, its own GPU data center, a Silicon Valley site working with Nvidia, and a program of buying unfinished biotech intellectual property. Nvidia, by contrast, has seen its multiple compress to roughly twelve or thirteen times earnings despite seventy percent revenue growth, because memory shortages cap how fast the infrastructure trade can actually build.

Asked for cybersecurity exposure, Visser says he does not pick single names in the space. His one software holding is Palantir, a position he has been growing relative to the chip names, built on the sovereign AI theme he dates from Alex Karp's push on sovereignty. The demand is clearly there, he says, but at these multiples the stocks can still go sideways for a year and a half while earnings catch up, exactly as the semiconductors just did for four months.

The closing stretch is about the tools in daily life. Pompliano set up a Grok bot as a genealogist, fed it his parents' and grandparents' names and family lore, and watched it surface marriage and birth certificates and voter rolls, trace one branch to Sulmona, Italy, where records reach back to about 1865, and draft emails in Italian to the town asking for the older books. Visser used AI video editing to turn his weekend video into short clips in about six or seven minutes, and to cut old family ski footage into a single film. Pompliano then walks through Silvia Legacy, a feature launched this week that lets a Silvia user name up to three people who, after a chosen period without a login and a sequence of unanswered check ins, receive a full package of the user's accounts and assets; he chose his wife at sixty days and included his chat history, which prompts a discussion of chat history itself as a new kind of family asset. Visser's own pick is Meta's Muse, which he praises for memory: he logs every meal by voice and photographs his journal pages, and the record is simply there when he asks for it back.

Open questions

  1. If stablecoin freezes and exchange backstops are now what limit the damage from a hack, how much of crypto's bearer asset premise survives, and who gets to decide when a freeze is legitimate?
  2. Visser's near term case leans on the November 4 refunding tilting Treasury issuance toward the front end. If long end supply is not cancelled, what is left to pull bitcoin and the AI names out of the mud he describes?
  3. His Lilly call needs a drugmaker to outgrow the AI infrastructure names while its own AI bets pay off. What would the next few quarters have to show, in GLP-1 volumes and pipeline results, for that path to stay credible?

Paid user-generated content as a growth engine at Wispr Flow

86 minutes of paid-UGC alpha | Hector Serrano (Wispr Flow)

On Lenny's Podcast, host Lenny Rachitsky sits down with Hector Serrano, the head of content at Wispr Flow, for "86 minutes of paid-UGC alpha." Serrano joined the AI dictation company at 19, before it had product-market fit, and now runs what Lenny calls one of the most innovative paid growth programs in Silicon Valley: a roster of 50 creators producing 500 to 600 ads a month. Over the past year the company's revenue grew thirtyfold and it raised at a valuation of two billion dollars, and this conversation is his attempt to hand over the whole playbook.

His first lesson is that none of it works without the product. Wispr Flow grew because people loved the tool, and everything else is fuel on that fire. Founders who visit asking for the hack get an unwelcome answer: if a product is not growing on its own, no channel will fix it, and the honest move is to go back and rethink the positioning. Paid spend, in his telling, is best understood as a learning engine. Turning it on early tells you quickly whether your messaging converts cold traffic, which is a signal most companies cannot get from organic content, because few teams are actually good at organic yet.

The program itself was adapted, not invented. A colleague, Tobin Tang, had earlier built an organic UGC group for Wispr Flow in which creators posted about the product on their own accounts and were paid per view. It produced half a billion views in about two and a half months, but the views were vanity metrics that did not convert, and the program was shut down. About seven months ago Serrano took Tang's roster and community playbook and applied it to paid ads instead, with creators pitching the product directly and getting paid on performance. The tooling is Trybe, a platform built by Lukas Pakter, who had run the same model for his own clothing brand on TikTok Shop. Serrano describes it as TikTok Shop's affiliate mechanics unlocked for Meta ads, which SaaS companies could never use before. Wispr Flow was one of its first customers.

The compensation design is the heart of it. For a SaaS product, creators earn a percentage of the ad spend behind their videos. For a commerce product, where purchases are easy to attribute, they earn a percentage of gross merchandise value instead. Pure commission is demoralizing at the start, so the program adds what he calls a smart retainer: a creator who hits a quota, say 25 videos, is guaranteed a floor amount, and commissions top it up. Earn 500 dollars in commissions against a 1,500 dollar floor and the company adds the other thousand. Earn past the floor and there is no retainer, just commission. Earnings are uncapped, payouts land every seven days, and the best creators now make over 13,000 dollars a month, more than many full-time influencers, some with only a thousand or two followers of their own.

Just as deliberate is what the creators are not given: instructions. Serrano is a creator himself, and he made every one of the company's ads for its first year and a half. He knows what a ten page brief does to motivation, so Wispr Flow sets no hooks, no scripts, and no lists of forbidden phrases. The group stays small and tight knit, the creators are genuine fans and power users of the product, and the result is ads he says he could never have briefed into existence, like a top performer that opens on a keyboard sitting in the rain on a roof, raindrops typing on their own.

Recruiting the first creators is the part most companies get wrong, because a new program has no track record to show. His Trybe profile now displays roughly 300,000 dollars paid out, and the group receives a thousand to fifteen hundred applications a week, of which he accepts one to three. From zero, though, he went off platform. He searched Instagram by keyword for niches adjacent to the product, went down follower lists, and sent 150 to 250 carefully personal messages over a week. Mass outreach tools produce slop and weak creators, in his experience. Five or six good creators came out of that first push, and the roster then grew in steps of five or six every few weeks. A referral sweetener pays creators a hundred dollars for each accepted creator they bring in, but the cap stays at 50.

Onboarding is run like closing a sale and building a friendship at once. Creators are used to being treated as transactions, so his first call starts with their story, not his slides. Then he introduces himself as a fellow creator, introduces Eric, the media buyer, explains the product and the money flow, shows example ads, and gets the creator fully set up before the call ends. Culture is maintained with weekly meetings, creator coaches, giveaways, and a retreat in San Francisco that will fly in top creators from around the world. Reputation is the asset underneath all of it: word travels fast among creators, and brands that pay late or treat people badly stop getting the best ones.

The subtler craft is coaching against sameness. Creators can see which ads spend the most, and the natural move is to copy them. Meta's Andromeda system, he explains, scans an ad's frames, transcript, and on-screen text, and groups together anything that looks more than about sixty percent similar, which accelerates fatigue. So the team pushes every creator to keep experimenting in their own direction. The volume math forces the issue anyway: at Wispr Flow's budgets an ad is effectively dead once it passes about 100,000 dollars of spend, sometimes within two or three weeks. The company has run roughly five thousand ads in its lifetime and keeps eight or nine hundred live at once, across UGC, statics, influencer and celebrity videos, in-house films, and founder-led pieces.

None of this requires a large team. The essential pair is a creative lead who can make content and judge it, and a media buyer who lives in the ad platforms. Wispr Flow's whole marketing org is about fifteen people, flat, with no middle management, inside a company that has grown from four employees when Serrano joined to more than 120. The program also works for enterprise software, he argues, because companies are made of individuals: win one person with an ad that speaks to them, and usage spreads inside their team until an enterprise deal becomes possible.

His hiring advice doubles as the episode's second thesis. The profile that matters is not grades, school, or resume. It is evidence of hard work, evidence of being a good person who fits the culture, and proof of having built an audience, even ten thousand followers, with Instagram the strongest signal because it rewards polish. He had a 2.5 GPA and no target school; Tang, his opposite on paper, had a 4.0 from Waterloo, and both became excellent marketers. Posting a 200,000 dollar job listing for this role attracts exactly the wrong people. Posting for interns and hungry kids works, provided the company then gives them time: his own first seven months were a mess, and he was carried by a team willing to let him fail and learn.

His model for this is sports, which he knows firsthand. He played academy soccer in Spain, in MLS Next, and Division One in college, and he points to academies that scout teenagers, coach them for years, and only then promote or sell them. His example is Lamine Yamal, a star while still a teenager. Startups, he argues, abandoned internships just when they became most valuable, and young people are not a compromise hire in this work but an edge: an eighteen year old inside the Discord chats and the daily scroll understands channels that a forty year old founder simply does not, which is why the company's own founder still asks Serrano what is working on Instagram.

The same instinct explains his most quoted line: the best marketers are allergic to AI. Not allergic to automation behind the scenes; even the most automation obsessed operator he knows keeps a human on the final ten percent. The allergy is to AI touching the creative output people actually see, the AI slop ads and synthetic influencers that audiences now recoil from. With everyone shipping volume, he argues, the noise is the opportunity: make fewer things, make them well, and cutting through gets easy. It is also why he runs founder-led content. He manages the founder's channels, and the founder's YouTube channel, about three months old with five videos, has drawn millions of views and roughly twenty thousand subscribers by treating each video as a long term asset rather than a quota.

YouTube is his pick for the most underrated lever in SaaS, and specifically its packaging. A masterpiece with a weak title and thumbnail is invisible, so the long form team matters more than the short form team: get the story, pacing, title, and thumbnail right once, and short form editors overseas can clip the best moments from a clean master. The payoff compounds because YouTube videos keep working for years, unlike an Instagram post that dies in two days. One of his own videos went from forty thousand views to six hundred thousand in a year.

His own story threads through everything. The son of immigrants, studying finance because content did not look like a real career, he quit Division One soccer and three days later, just after turning nineteen, applied to some two hundred creator jobs on LinkedIn and landed at a tiny startup he had never heard of. His first assignment arrived by Slack during a calculus lecture: fix the launch video, make it feel Gen Z. A summer finance internship in New York cured him of the safe path within two weeks; he spent it sneaking off to cafes in Soho to work on Wispr Flow. On his hiring call he asked the founder for equity, and the founder laughed and liked him for it. His closing advice to founders is to take that kind of chance on young talent, and his book recommendation is Naval Ravikant's Almanack, for its idea that media is leverage that needs no one's permission. His motto is Steve Jobs on connecting the dots: they only join up looking backwards.

Open questions

  1. The program's edge comes from a hand-built community, uncapped performance pay, and one creative lead's taste. Which of those breaks first as imitators copy the playbook and the best creators get bid up?
  2. Paying creators a percentage of spend ties their income to the size of the budget rather than to profit. Does the model stay aligned when a company needs to cut spend, or when attribution gets good enough to pay on revenue instead?
  3. Serrano's hiring edge depends on spotting young, platform-native talent before the market prices it in. Once every company recruits this way, and once his own generation of hires ages out of the feeds it grew up in, where does the next edge come from?

Rebuilding Office for AI agents, with Yijia Liang of Compound AI

Why We Rebuilt Microsoft Office From Scratch for AI Agents - Yijia Liang, Compound AI

On AI Engineer, the talk "Why We Rebuilt Microsoft Office From Scratch for AI Agents" is given by Yijia Liang of Compound AI. Liang spent about three years as a vice president at Coatue, working on late stage private software investments, and says he spent far too much of that time in Excel and PowerPoint. He left to build tools that would replace his old job, and the talk is his account of what it takes to make AI agents genuinely useful inside institutional finance.

He opens with a puzzle. Personal agents have taken off in the last three months, and Compound's internal answer for why has three parts. Background agents have become reliable enough to delegate to, so a task like booking a flight actually gets done. The agents are proactive, doing things without being asked. And they are integrated with the context that matters to a person: email, drive, and calendar.

Almost none of that transfers directly to a buy side firm, he argues. The finance equivalent of booking a restaurant table is turning a data room into a full set of investment committee models, slides, and memos, a process that normally takes four weeks of iteration and review and would need to compress into a few days. Proactivity is a liability when a managing director does not want an agent running around burning tokens without soliciting input. And the context is not an inbox. It can be a data room of 15,000 files, or a SharePoint and Box archive holding petabytes of unstructured data collected over a firm's forty year history.

Compound's response has three parts of its own: make the agents multiplayer, rebuild the office software they work in, and build machinery for searching that context.

Multiplayer comes first because deal work is team work. Liang points out that Claude and ChatGPT deployments inside institutions today are single player. One person produces an output in a plugin or a web app, then downloads the file and circulates it by email or Teams to get feedback. Compound instead gives every agent it creates a built in inbox, on the theory that institutional finance runs through Outlook and long email chains, and the agent should be a participant in those threads rather than a tool one person visits.

Designing email for agents turned out to be nontrivial. There is an authentication problem: everyone on a thread has to have read and write access to the agent, which matters at firms where every deal sits under an NDA and privacy is paramount. There is also a behaviour problem. If several people reply in quick succession, a teammate would not fire back a separate acknowledgement to each one, so the agents bake in latency to feel human and avoid spamming the group.

The second piece is the radical one. To run many long tasks in parallel, Compound evaluated the desktop plugins for Excel, PowerPoint, and Word, computer use agents driving virtual machines, and a range of existing engines, from open source packages like openpyxl to commercial products. None of them were good enough. With coding agents driving the cost of software down, the company decided to rebuild Office from scratch, running agents in loops against a simple objective: recreate Office in full fidelity, using Office itself as the judge. Liang puts the totals at 1.5 trillion input and output tokens and over 8 million lines of code across the project.

Excel shows the method. The team started from IronCalc, an open source spreadsheet engine, benchmarked against real files drawn from public corpora and their own work, and let agents iterate against the real product as an oracle. Eight hundred thousand lines of Rust later, they had a replica he describes as one for one and considerably faster than Excel itself. The same loop approach produced PowerPoint and Word equivalents, with Rust based renderers checking that what Compound shows on screen is pixel for pixel what a downloaded Office file will show. That parity matters, he says, because firms only trust what they can download and send.

Owning the engine also buys speed and a different way of working. Desktop Office is bound to one machine's CPU, while a headless engine can run tasks in parallel and recalculate a twenty megabyte workbook four times faster. The talk's own slide deck was built inside Compound: Liang generated a first draft, then left comments on the formatting throughout, drawing on what he had learned about good slides at Coatue, and let the agent apply them. A five person team, he claims, can work through a hundred page deck in an afternoon this way. Owning the stack also means owning the write, render, check, and fix loop. A library like openpyxl offers no render to check formatting against, so an agent using it is guessing. Compound's engine can render its own output and iterate on it.

The third piece is context, which he splits into breadth and depth. For breadth, the model is borrowed from coding agents: swarms of agents read files directly, a lead agent slices the directory, and the swarm reports back with findings that are grounded in citations to the source documents, so an analyst can trace every number. Indexing everything into a vector database is reserved for narrow cases. For depth, the example is a thirty megabyte model with two million formulas and five sensitivity tables that takes five minutes to recalculate. Driving that through code libraries forces the model to read the workbook as printed out code, which is slow and expensive. Because Compound owns the Excel engine, it can lay the model out in a compact form the model can process, and expose a richer set of tools over it.

The goal of all three pieces is to move humans up a level. Instead of hunting for files and reminding the model how things should be done, analysts should spend their time imparting judgment: how a firm models a deal, how its materials should look. That, he says, is what expands the set of tasks that can run on autopilot. Spreading comps is already there with off the shelf tools. Drafting a fifty page investment committee presentation, or building a model exactly the way a particular private equity investor wants it, still sits in copilot territory, where people want to stay embedded in the process.

He closes with an analogy to coding. MNX, the exchange built for the AI economy

OpenAI and Anthropic Will Pull Away From Rivals | Stephen Grugett and Alex Masmej

On MTS, the episode "OpenAI and Anthropic Will Pull Away From Rivals | Stephen Grugett and Alex Masmej" is a conversation with the two leaders of MNX, a new exchange that launched about two weeks before the recording. Stephen Grugett is its co-founder and chief executive, and Alex Masmej is its chief marketing officer. The exchange was built for the AI economy: it lists futures contracts on the valuations of private AI labs, alongside traditional assets, and it originates markets on questions that until now had no venue at all.

The flagship products are futures on the valuations of the big AI labs, including OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, and Moonshot. Around those, MNX lists perpetuals and futures on ordinary financial assets such as gold and Tesla stock, binary prediction markets, and a format the team calls a scalar prediction market, where traders bet on a continuous number rather than a yes or no outcome. One example is a contract on the combined revenue of OpenAI and Anthropic next year, designed so that investors can hedge how the large labs are doing directly. There are also markets on San Francisco median home prices and an H100 price contract that tracks the SemiAnalysis index.

Asked how this differs from Polymarket or Kalshi, Grugett says MNX is a full service futures exchange that originates its own markets rather than only listing event contracts. The point, he argues, is hedging: there are obvious questions that will decide how trillions of dollars of capital get allocated, from cybersecurity outcomes to which lab produces the best model under a criterion like the Epoch index or LMArena, and portfolio managers who want to hedge those risks currently have nowhere to trade them. A smaller exchange wins, in his telling, by being the only venue with liquidity in the markets it creates, and using those markets to draw traders into the rest of the book.

The conversation spends real time on crypto security, because MNX is a decentralized exchange and the recording followed a major hack at Binance the day before. Grugett calls crypto the canary in the coal mine for the whole tech and financial sector: so much money sits secured on chain that attackers have the strongest possible incentives there first. He thinks the industry is near peak security destabilization now, with more carnage in the next month or two, but that within about a year it will have adapted, through formal verification of contracts and custody setups and through using frontier models to penetration test systems proactively. Masmej adds the structural point: a custodial exchange keeps billions in hot wallets in one place, while on a decentralized exchange every user holds their own wallet, so there is no single honeypot. Bitcoin, he notes, is the world's largest honeypot and its core protocol has still never been hacked.

Compute futures get a careful treatment. MNX lists a contract on H100 prices, but Grugett says the rival price indices in this space are surprisingly uncorrelated with each other, which would be impossible in a mature market where everyone tracks the same visible spot price. The spot market for compute is still underdeveloped, and compute sits at the awkward end of his spectrum of commodity likeness: gold is divisible and priced the same everywhere, oil has grades and regional premia, and compute varies by electricity price, interconnect, and data center, and workloads cannot easily move when prices shift. His conviction is that compute becomes the next trillion dollar commodity market once the spot market matures and portability improves, something he expects AI itself to help with, and that derivative contracts will ultimately hold the vast majority of the volume.

The title claim arrives when the host asks what someone who believes in short AI timelines should trade. In a shorter timeline scenario, Grugett says, OpenAI and Anthropic pull ahead of the other labs: they can use their compute to research improvements to their models faster than rivals can copy them, so they sustain their margins and become great investments in their own right. Labs racing toward recursive self-improvement would bid up training compute and reinforcement learning environments, lifting compute prices in the short term, which favors neoclouds whose financing is tied to short term compute prices. Semiconductor producers, fabs, and energy companies do well too. His one short is bonds, because the investment demand from the labs alone would push interest rates up, with secondary effects from people expecting to be richer, and from rising doom probabilities pulling consumption into the present.

That thesis is tested against the day's news: Intel stock had fallen 30 percent and the semiconductor index about 8.5 percent. Grugett calls the market irrational, while allowing he may be wrong. Intel has real headwinds, he says, since rising memory prices raise the cost of an average consumer laptop and depress those sales, and the company has not moved at the speed investors expected. The host illustrates the memory squeeze with his own gaming PC: the 64 gigabyte DDR5 kit he bought for 205 dollars in September 2025 is the same listing now priced at 1,279 dollars. If the short timeline world is the real one, Grugett argues, moves like Intel's drop will look like a small blip; if it is not, more pain makes sense.

Masmej closes the product tour with a dashboard the team built for the show, at monitoringthesingularity.xyz, which surfaces MNX markets on exactly these questions: recursive self-improvement timelines, a five percent drop in the labor force, and a PDoom market. The PDoom contract has a design problem, since a genuine doom event could never pay out, so it resolves instead by asking a panel of large language models at the end of next year what their PDoom is, on the theory that model answers track received wisdom as sentiment shifts. One practical limit hangs over all of it: MNX does not serve United States customers today, and Grugett estimates the licensing needed to move onshore will realistically take about a year, though Americans can still watch the prices.

  2. The PDoom market resolves by polling language models rather than observing an event. If model answers mostly track training data fashion and public sentiment, what is the price of that contract actually measuring?
  3. The title claim rests on the two largest labs converting compute into research speed faster than rivals can copy the results. What observable evidence over the next year or so would confirm that lead is compounding rather than closing?

Marvell, Lumentum, and vertical integration in AI optics

Why Marvell Wants Lumentum: Vertical Integration in AI Optics With Cignal AI

On Semi Doped, the episode "Why Marvell Wants Lumentum: Vertical Integration in AI Optics With Cignal AI" is a same-week reaction to Bloomberg's report that Marvell Technology is in advanced talks to buy Lumentum, the data center optics maker. Hosts Austin Lyons and Vik bring in Andrew Schmitt and Scott Wilkinson of Cignal AI, a market research firm that has covered networking components and hardware for a decade. The two analysts had not compared notes before recording, so their agreements and disagreements arrive unscripted.

The stakes, as the hosts relay the Bloomberg report, are large. Lumentum carries a market value of about 83 billion dollars against Marvell's 221 billion, a transaction could be announced as soon as next week, and the deal would likely rank among the ten biggest tech and telecom mergers ever. Lumentum sits at the top of the laser supply pile just as data centers begin a shift to optical interconnect on a scale the industry has never attempted, which frames the episode's two questions: why would Marvell pay so much, and why would Lumentum sell?

Two explanations are circulating, and the analysts reject one of them flatly. The rejected story says Marvell is really buying laser supply and capacity to protect its existing plans. Schmitt calls that wrong: Marvell does not need laser capacity, the only lasers it buys are tunable coherent lasers for its ZR modules, and its supplier relationships are strong. The real motive, in their reading, is vertical integration, owning the stack from the DSP through the lasers to the finished transceiver.

Owning that stack means competing with your own customers. Marvell's coherent DSPs sit inside most of the 400 gigabit modules built by anyone other than Acacia, Ciena, or Huawei, and at least one module maker has told Cignal AI it will have to exit the market because it cannot compete against a supplier that uses the same DSP in its own products. The analysts note this model is new for DSPs, though Lumentum has long sold modules built around its own lasers while its rival Coherent keeps its lasers for internal use. Lumentum's Cloud Light business is the template in miniature: its silicon photonics transceivers pair Lumentum's own laser with a purchased PAM4 DSP, and those two parts account for nearly all the value inside the box.

The deal therefore involves a deliberate trade. Marvell may lose merchant DSP sales as customers defect to Broadcom, Nvidia, or Credo, but integrated products capture more of the value in each transceiver, and the coming shift toward near-package and co-packaged optics needs less standalone DSP content anyway. As one of the analysts puts it, if your business card said DSP salesman, you would be a little worried.

The assets doing the persuading are the ones nobody else can ship at volume. Lumentum was among the first out with 200 gigabit EMLs, it leads in the ultra-high-power lasers that co-packaged optics need, and it is effectively the only supplier shipping optical circuit switches in major volume, an emerging piece of data center infrastructure with a crowd of startups chasing it. Wilkinson's point is that the moat is manufacturing depth: plenty of companies can build a hundred of a device, but building fifty thousand or a million of them is a different game entirely.

Why would Lumentum's board sell a company holding those cards? Price, mainly. An offer beyond ten times current revenue is hard to turn down, and chief executive Michael Hurlston has run this play before, having organized Finisar's sale to Coherent during a short stint there. Schmitt adds that Lumentum might be worth as much or more to a different integrator, with Nvidia the obvious candidate: it bought Mellanox to integrate networking with its GPUs, and it has put two billion dollars into Lumentum while also holding equity in Marvell and Coherent, so a merger mostly rearranges stakes it already owns. Whether laser customers then flock to Coherent, he says, depends on how Marvell manages the merger and reassures them.

The capacity fear gets a hearing and mostly dissolves. Lumentum has expanded its indium phosphide capability roughly fourfold in a couple of years and is scaled to sell lasers to many buyers, Marvell does not build datacom transceivers, so the merger creates no new internal demand that would starve outside customers, and Schmitt sees no reason Marvell would expand those fabs any better than Lumentum can on its own.

Underneath the tactics sits a repricing of the whole component layer. Optical components that once sold at gross margins near 25 percent now sell at nearly double that, because a part that lets an AI cluster train far faster enables value wildly beyond its price. That inversion explains the timing. Chip companies spent decades refusing to buy 25 or 30 percent margin businesses while guarding their own 50 percent, fixated on the margin percentage rather than the margin dollars, and Marvell could have bought Lumentum for roughly three billion dollars six or seven years ago instead of perhaps thirty times that figure now.

Wilkinson resists the idea that AI has repealed the industry's laws. Each new standard is what he calls an IEEE lottery: five or six companies build to it, and a few years later about two of them ship 80 to 90 percent of the volume. What is genuinely different this time is capacity and concentration. Eoptolink arrived late to 800 gigabit and still became the world's number two supplier because it could build as many units as anyone wanted, and the same contest is starting at 1.6 terabit Ethernet. The buyers have also changed: a single hyperscaler can now carry an entire business, as with optical circuit switches or the 2.4 terabit coherent design being built for exactly one customer in the world, a pattern that looked reckless a decade ago, when Inphi's single-customer ColorZ product at Microsoft was the exception.

The long game is co-packaged optics. TSMC is effectively the gatekeeper for production-grade CPO in volume, end customers want more than one source, and a vendor who can supply the ASIC, the packaging, and the optics as one kit holds a strong hand. Wilkinson traces that logic to Broadcom's Ethernet switching playbook: bundle the switch chip, the copper PHYs, the software, and the reference design, price the kit so point-product rivals cannot breathe, and take the market. The same kit approach should work for near-package optics. Marvell's custom XPU silicon business, by contrast, sits in a separate pocket from its module and connectivity lines today, unlike Nvidia's integrated system business, so this deal would make connectivity the company's center of gravity.

What happens next is the closing question. Schmitt, stressing that he has no inside knowledge of the talks, thinks the leak looks deliberate, possibly floated to draw out competing interest, and he sees a good chance of a bidding war, with Nvidia a plausible alternative buyer running the same vertical integration playbook Marvell is pursuing.

Open questions

  1. What commitments does Marvell make to the module makers who buy its DSPs today, and do Broadcom, Credo, and Nvidia absorb the defectors faster than Marvell's integrated business grows?
  2. Does a rival bid actually materialize, from Nvidia or another integrator, and does Lumentum's board use the leak to run an auction before any Marvell offer hardens?
  3. When co-packaged optics reach volume, does owning the laser supply inside the kit prove decisive against Broadcom's bundle and TSMC's packaging gate, or does raw manufacturing capacity remain the binding constraint?
