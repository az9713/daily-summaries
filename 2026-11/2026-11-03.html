Sourcery with Molly O'Shea · fal raises $1.65B across Series E and F

fal Raises $1.65B Across Series E & F by Sequoia & Bessemer

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, the episode "fal Raises $1.65B Across Series E & F by Sequoia & Bessemer" is a conversation with Burkay Gür and Gorkem Yurtseven, the co-founders of fal, recorded over dinner at Dalida in San Francisco. The occasion is fal's combined Series E and Series F, $1.65 billion in total, with Sequoia Capital leading the E and Bessemer Venture Partners leading the F. The founders are almost sheepish about it. The rounds have come back to back, the adrenaline of announcing them has worn off, and they would rather talk about the business than the money.

Their frame for that business is a claim about where AI actually works today. AI, they argue, has three product market fits: search, coding agents, and generative media, the category fal named and built itself into. What sets generative media apart is the spending pattern. A professional whose job is generating video all day can spend $10,000 to $20,000 a month on it, a level of individual spend that otherwise exists only in coding. Search is so large it gets a pass, and no other part of AI has this dynamic, which is why the growth keeps compounding.

The pricing arc of the last two years shows how unusual the demand is. Video generation settled early around 30 to 40 cents for a five second clip, with Kling and MiniMax among the best models. Then Veo 2 arrived at $5 for a five second video, more than ten times the going rate, and the founders braced for rejection. Instead demand proved, in their word, essentially infinite, because the quality jump pulled in professional work: real advertising, marketing, and film production. They hosted OpenAI's Sora as well, and watched volume surge in the three months before it was taken down, which they attribute to there not being enough compute to run everything.

The mix of that demand has surprised them twice. Video went from nothing to roughly 70 percent of revenue, and they assumed image models were finished. Then image editing models arrived, made image work valuable again, and improved video too, because better edited frames give video models more to interpolate between. Revenue now splits about evenly between image and video. Costs, meanwhile, only climb: longer durations and higher resolutions raise the compute per generation steeply, and the founders see nothing like the diminishing returns people debate for language models.

Fal did not start here. The company began in 2021 as a compute company for the pre-AI data world, on the thesis that what Snowflake and Databricks did for SQL, someone would do for Python, owning the compute and scaling it elastically. After ChatGPT, Stable Diffusion, and DALL·E landed, the five or six person team chose the new world over the old roadmap, but steered away from language model tokens, where a price race to the bottom was already under way and they could not afford to sell at a negative margin. Image models were the opening: a technical team could make them run much faster in the cloud. The old business had run a year without a dollar of revenue; the new one took off, and they gave the category its name, generative media, because three years ago nobody knew what the words meant.

That origin explains the company's seat in the ecosystem today. Fal is an infrastructure partner to the model labs and an API partner to the studios and applications consuming the models, so it sees supply and demand on both sides. In a compute constrained market, a lab launching a model has to plan its inference capacity ahead or fail to procure it, which makes partnering with an inference provider like fal the practical route. Fal in turn has to anticipate demand and invest in compute ahead of launches, a responsibility the founders say has fallen to them by default, because otherwise the capacity drifts to coding agents and whoever invests biggest. The field they serve is fragmented by design: text to image, image editing, video, 3D, speech in both directions, five to ten companies per modality, and roughly eight serious labs leapfrogging each other, fed by an effectively unlimited supply of video data where the hard problem is filtering out the interesting footage.

Advertising is the use case they expect to dominate. User generated style ads are disposable by nature, so imperfect output is fine, and short cuts stitched together can beat a single long generation. The real prize is the loop nobody has built well yet: generate many variants, post them, read the engagement data, and automatically make more of what performs. Hollywood is arriving more slowly, and for a specific reason. Business from the studios has grown into a meaningful share of revenue over the last six months, but the founders say AI has so far had zero impact on Hollywood labor and has barely produced content worth watching there. What slowed adoption was not model quality but company lawyers, who are only now signing off.

The open versus closed source argument, they think, never ends, because it is useful to both sides. A lab that falls behind can open source a model and get the whole world talking, and studios have hard technical reasons to want open weights: fine tuning, applying LoRAs and filters, changing lighting, and running unreleased characters and styles through a model. Underneath it sits trust. Unreleased creative work is the input to these models, and customers who know a model runs on fal's GPUs trust the process far more.

The organization behind all this is deliberately flat. Fal is about 150 people with at most two levels of reporting and no pure managers; leadership is built around what they call super ICs, people strong enough to do hands on work and lead at the same time. The founders met in San Francisco in 2014, both Turkish, one coming from Coinbase and the other from Amazon, and the early team was drawn almost entirely from people they had already worked with and trusted. What they screen for is agency, hunger, and humility, and they note that their engineering team now moves three to four times faster than it could before AI tooling, which is part of why the flat structure still holds. Even the company's name is a double meaning: fal is the initials of features and labels, an old machine learning term, and the Turkish word for a fortune read from coffee grounds.

They close with hot takes, and the sharpest is about margin. The consensus view when they started was that infrastructure would race to zero and the application layer would keep the margin. They say it has inverted: application layer margins are close to zero because competition and marketing costs eat everything, and the only durable margin sits in the compute path, for whoever can innovate there. Every big company, in their phrase, is becoming a compute company with a different storefront. As for what comes next, one founder wants the first timeless piece of AI content, a short film or animation people still enjoy years later, and the other has turned bullish on world models, interactive worlds you can move through, which need a GPU running constantly for every player and could become a fourth product market fit in gaming.

Open questions

  1. If durable margin really sits in the compute path rather than the application layer, what happens to the studios and app companies fal serves, and to fal itself if the big labs pull more inference in house?
  2. World models need a GPU running continuously for every player. In a market where fal already invests ahead just to cover launches, where does that always-on capacity come from?
  3. Hollywood's slow adoption was blamed on company lawyers rather than model quality. What legal or licensing settlement would actually unlock studio use at scale, and who offers it first?

Scott Nolan, General Matter, and building America's uranium supply from scratch

The company building America’s uranium supply from scratch

On Cheeky Pint, the episode "The company building America’s uranium supply from scratch" is a conversation between host John Collison and Scott Nolan, co-founder and chief executive of General Matter, a startup building uranium enrichment capacity in the United States. Nolan spent ten years investing at Founders Fund before starting the company, and the idea came from the reactor startups he backed. Asked what their hardest problem was, they did not say licensing. They said fuel: the high-assay fuel their designs need is made commercially only in Russia.

Nolan walks through the fuel chain. Uranium is mined and milled, converted into a gas, separated so the fissile isotope U-235 makes up a larger share, then turned back into a solid and formed into pellets. The 94 reactors on the US grid run on fuel enriched to 3 to 5 percent, which suits very large reactor vessels. The advanced reactors now being designed run on HALEU, high-assay low-enriched uranium at just under 20 percent. The richer fuel lets a core stay critical far longer in a much smaller vessel, which is what makes factory-built, modular reactors and refueling cycles of five or ten years possible.

The supply history is the striking part. The United States once held over 90 percent of global enrichment supply, built on technology developed for the Second World War and the Cold War. After the Cold War it shut that capacity down and, under the program nicknamed Megatons to Megawatts, imported Russian warheads, blended them down, and ran its own reactors on them. About a quarter of US enriched uranium still comes from Russia under waivers. Congress has banned Russian uranium imports, and the ban takes full effect on January 1, 2028. Only Russia makes HALEU commercially today, so the advanced reactor industry faces a hard deadline with no domestic supplier.

General Matter's first mission is to produce HALEU before the end of the decade, and its second is to bring the cost of enrichment down. The company has grown to just over 200 people since it started at the end of 2023, with headquarters and engineering in Los Angeles. Its plant is being built in Paducah, Kentucky, on the Department of Energy site where the last American enrichment facility operated until 2013. Nolan says the site search covered more than ten states and a thousand plots of land, and Paducah won on community support, local expertise, and land that was already environmentally characterized. The company recently submitted its license application to the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, which Nolan describes as straightforward to work with: demanding on safety, security, and environmental impact, but a process of analysis and paperwork rather than obstruction. A 900 million dollar federal award for capacity building, competed for under one administration and awarded under the next, covers much of the first facility's cost.

The economics get the longest treatment. Nolan argues nuclear power stalled in the 1970s mainly because it stopped being cost-competitive, not because of accidents: oil shocks, interest rates that peaked with mortgages near 18 percent in 1981, and decade-long construction made financing costs dominate, a pattern he says repeated at Vogtle, where about a fifth of the cost overruns were interest payments. Utilities structured as cost-plus organizations had little pressure to push prices down, the same stasis he saw in space launch before SpaceX. His fixes are to build faster, build more, and build with an in-house construction team copied from Tesla rather than outsourcing. Most of General Matter's contracting volume today is actually ordinary low-enriched fuel for the existing fleet. Its future customers span the field, from Antares and Radiant's small containerized reactors through Aalo, Valar, and Oklo to X-energy and TerraPower.

On the technology itself, Nolan is deliberately vague: the company's separation technique will be unveiled in the first half of next year. What he will say is that enrichment is a separation of isotopes in a gas, that his facility involves no nuclear reactions and no chemical reactions, and that the real challenge is cost rather than feasibility. The incumbents in Russia, China, and Europe all use technology whose last clean-sheet redesign was decades ago, and his bet is that a redesign capturing decades of general technology progress can compete globally. Asked why he left investing, he returns to lessons from interning at Boeing, where cost-plus contracts visibly slowed work, and at SpaceX in 2003, where a team of fewer than fifty people out-executed incumbents a thousand times its size. His rule from Founders Fund: avoid competition by working on something important that nobody else is doing, where the bottleneck is obvious once you go deep enough. For nuclear, he says, that bottleneck is fuel.

Open questions

  1. General Matter recently submitted its NRC license application and still has to build, equip, and commission the Paducah plant. Can it really be delivering enriched uranium by the end of the decade, soon after the Russian import ban bites in 2028?
  2. Nolan's cost case rests on an enrichment technique the company will not unveil until next year. Once it is public and running at scale, can a clean-sheet American process actually undercut Urenco and the Russian and Chinese incumbents?
  3. Most of the company's near-term volume is ordinary fuel for the existing fleet, while the HALEU market depends on advanced reactors deploying on schedule from 2028. If those deployments slip, does the existing fleet carry the company, and who fills the HALEU gap in the meantime?

Edwin Catmull, Pixar, and AI and creativity

Pixar Co-Founder x Higgsfield: Edwin Catmull about AI and Creativity

On Higgsfield AI, the episode "Pixar Co-Founder x Higgsfield: Edwin Catmull about AI and Creativity" is a conversation between Edwin Catmull, the co-founder of Pixar, and one of the co-founders of Higgsfield, the AI video company hosting him. The host opens with a piece of company lore: when Higgsfield started in 2023, there was exactly one book in the office, Catmull's book about Pixar. Catmull spent twenty years building toward Toy Story, the first feature film made entirely with computer graphics, then ran Pixar and Disney Animation through a long run of hits. The conversation treats today's fight over AI filmmaking as a rerun of the fight he already lived through, and he keeps returning to one claim: the tools change at a compounding rate, but the craft and the human dynamics do not, and confusing those two speeds is the basic mistake on both sides of the argument.

He starts with how computer graphics was received at the beginning. It was not hated, he says, it was dismissed as irrelevant. The imagery was too crude for Hollywood to take seriously, and people judged where the field was going by the stair-stepped, faceted pictures it could produce at the time. His formation came at the University of Utah, where his teachers were Ivan Sutherland, who taught him to take one step at a time and solve the problem in front of him, and Alan Kay, who taught him to think about what a tenfold or hundredfold change in computing power would actually mean. His classmates included Jim Clark, who later founded Silicon Graphics, and John Warnock, who co-founded Adobe. The working method that followed was to emulate the future: build for the machine that economics would eventually make cheap, solve the important problems early, and be ready when the costs arrived. The proof of arrival, for him, was that when Toy Story came out, almost every review spent only a sentence or two on the computer generation and the rest on the movie itself. The technology had disappeared, which was the goal all along.

A second foundation was cultural. Catmull rejects the idea that engineers are organized and artists are not, and says world-class people in both domains have the same distribution of abilities. What matters is that they work as peers, socialize together, and share ownership of the tools. When Pixar rewrote its animation system, animators rotated onto the engineering team, so at the end they felt the system was theirs rather than something handed to them as customers. He is blunt that the outside world will always credit the artists and ignore the technical staff, and that a studio's job is to refuse that separation internally.

The middle of the conversation covers the Lucasfilm years. George Lucas, Catmull says, was the first person since Walt Disney to understand that technology is part of the creative process and to fund it accordingly. The practical problem was that neither side knew what to build: engineers did not know the art of editing, and artists did not know what was possible, so artists had to tolerate tools that were not yet good enough while the builders learned. The scale gap was concrete. The state of the art was about 40,000 polygons per image, and their rough estimate said a film needed about 80 million. Closing that gap produced RenderMan. Then came the four years that changed the industry: Terminator 2 in 1991 with the first major computer-generated character, Jurassic Park in 1993, which contains only a few minutes of computer graphics that audiences remember as far more, and Toy Story in 1995. Through all of it, at Lucasfilm, at Pixar under Steve Jobs, and later at Disney, Catmull's policy was to publish everything they did. Secrecy, he argues, is wasted effort when a field is changing this fast, and openness built the community that supplied Pixar with its people and its problems.

Jobs gets a careful treatment. What Jobs saw, Catmull says, was the best computer graphics group in the world plus the exponential curve underneath it: he bought a potential without knowing exactly what it would become. The short films were how the group developed while the hardware business struggled, and Luxo Jr. became the milestone. It was nominated for an Academy Award and, in Catmull's view, should have won, but the animation community balked at the term "computer animation," which made it sound as if the computer had done the work. He draws the parallel directly: the label "AI" triggers the same reaction now. His evidence that Luxo Jr. had crossed over was the question audiences asked afterward, which was not about technique but about whether the big lamp was the mother or the father. They were inside the story.

On whether AI-generated art is real art, his answer turns on judgment. The training imagery is largely derivative of the real world, he concedes, but behind every earlier medium, from watercolor to stop motion, there was always a person deciding that a shot was good enough or needed redoing, and you could feel the technology fade once that judgment was exercised well. With AI today, he says, he still sees the technology in the work. His measure is when he stops seeing it. The complaints artists make, about controlling timing, lighting, pacing, framing, and keeping characters consistent across a story, are in his framing simply the current list of problems to solve, exactly as the complaints about computer graphics once were.

Asked whether an AI-assisted film could win the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature by 2030, he says it is highly unlikely, mainly because film development cycles take years and the industry is only at the end of 2026. Computer-generated characters have in fact been accepted since the mid-1990s, he notes, but they arrived gradually, as background creatures and effects, so audiences made the shift without noticing. The difference now is speed, and speed produces a fear of displacement that irrelevance never did. He points out that when effects work moved from physical builds to computer graphics, the field and its headcount grew dramatically rather than shrinking. His example of healthy acceptance is the Andor series: heavy computer graphics, widely admired, because the filmmaking itself is excellent. When the work is good, he says, audiences stop caring how it was made.

Higgsfield's team screened work for him, including a trailer assembled in under two months, and he praised its look, its consistency, and its structure. His reservation is about discipline. Billions of people carry a phone that shoots film-resolution images, he observes, and almost no feature films have resulted, because a camera in your pocket does not teach you lighting, pacing, or patience. The same holds for AI. The tool is phenomenal, but the principles of how audiences perceive stories, where their eyes go, how nonverbal signals can contradict dialogue, still have to be learned through study. In the shorts he has judged, the images are often strong for their style while the storytelling elements, the timing and the payoff, lag behind. Extending a good clip into the three-act arc of a feature is the hard part, and violating that arc only works when a filmmaker knows why they are doing it.

His guess about AI's Jurassic Park moment is modest: the first widely accepted successes will probably be stylized work, or AI used quietly in the background where it does not call attention to itself, the way Toy Story's characters were toys because plastic was all the compute of the day could render. The genuinely new obstacle, he says, is politicization. Everyone is affected by computers now in a way they were not in the 1990s, so the argument is louder. But the economics and the artistic pull run in one direction, and the phase the industry is in is about learning to tell the good uses from the bad rather than stopping the change.

The closing stretch is about running creative companies. Catmull says Pixar thought constantly about its social fabric, and that a feeling of being second class crept into the culture three times despite that vigilance. The producer's job, as he defines it, is to protect the film, not a director, not a margin, not anyone's fame, and companies that quietly put profit ahead of the product work in the short term and crash in the long term. Flatness at Pixar meant flat communication rather than flat structure: artists talked directly to technical staff without going up and down a chain. For honest feedback they built the Braintrust, used audience screenings, and kept an outside force, which for years was Jobs, appearing once a quarter. Jobs never attended Braintrust meetings because his voice would have overwhelmed them, and Catmull noticed that Jobs never once said a note that some filmmaker had not already raised. The filmmakers had learned to ignore each other. Said articulately by Jobs, the same note finally landed.

He ends by declining the premise of the one-person film studio. One person will be able to make a film, he says, but the share of such films anyone wants to watch will be tiny, because a story like Frozen's sister relationship drew on interviews with many women at Disney about their own sisters, and no single person holds that range of experience. His guess, formed before generative AI, is that the right crew for a feature is under a hundred people and nowhere near ten, and that cutting production costs roughly in half in the near term is realistic, which streaming economics may require anyway. His advice to the people building the tools is the same as his advice to filmmakers: engage with the change, learn the craft, and treat every complaint about the tools' limits as the next problem to fix.

Open questions

  1. Catmull says an AI-assisted Best Animated Feature winner by 2030 is highly unlikely because film development cycles run in years. What would have to change in how films are developed for that timeline to compress?
  2. If the first broadly accepted AI films are likely to be stylized or to hide the technology in the background, what should a toolmaker prioritize to reach that acceptance faster: more control for artists, or better built-in storytelling discipline?
  3. His guess is that the right crew for an AI-era feature is under a hundred people but nowhere near ten, with costs roughly halved. What evidence, from productions being made now, could actually settle that number?

Luke Alvoeiro, Factory, and autonomous software factories

Autonomous Software Factories Arrive by 2031 | Luke Alvoeiro

On MTS, the episode "Autonomous Software Factories Arrive by 2031 | Luke Alvoeiro" is a conversation with Luke Alvoeiro of Factory, the company behind the Droid coding agent. He came on to talk about Droid 1.0, which Factory had launched the week before, and about where software development is heading over the next five years.

Factory aims Droid at the enterprise end of coding agents: repositories of effectively infinite context, work coordinated across thousands of developers, and large corporations that need guarantees around risk, efficiency, and quality before they deploy a tool across the whole organization. Droid 1.0 is the company's largest agent harness release so far, built around a new programmatic tool calling engine that is meant to improve the agent's accuracy, speed, token efficiency, and reliability on any platform. It is also a stepping stone toward Factory's flagship idea, the software factory: a trusted, self-improving system that runs around the clock building software under the user's control, with the user able to step away from the keyboard while the safeguards and controls stay in place.

Programmatic tool calling changes how an agent acts. Instead of the model issuing one tool call at a time, it writes a JavaScript script ahead of time that runs many tool calls at once. That raises a safety problem the episode spends real time on: parameters that used to be visible in each individual call are now inferred inside the script at execution time. Alvoeiro says the answer is twofold. The user keeps the option to manually approve what happens at runtime, and the script runs inside a locked-down sandbox, so the agent cannot produce unintended consequences while operating in this more programmatic way. Because Factory supports many models, he argues, its harness has to generalize across the jaggedness of different models, which ends up making it better than harnesses built for a single model.

Token efficiency is the other half of the pitch. With programmatic tool calling, the model can generate a large batch of commands and then specify which results it actually wants to see. Moving 150 Linear tickets from one project to another is his example: the model only needs to know whether the move succeeded, not the contents of every ticket, whereas a conventional loop would feed every result back into the context and the user would pay for all of it. Less context bloat means an agent's coherence holds up over longer runs, and it means a buyer can afford to run many more agents at the same time.

He brought benchmark numbers to back the harness claim. Factory measured the two most heavily used models on its platform, Opus 5.5 and GPT 6.1 Sol, both at high reasoning, and reports an eight-point lead on Terminal Bench 4 over the rival harness using the exact same model, with those tasks served at a 17 percent cost advantage and run 25 percent faster. Beating Claude Code running Opus 5.5 with Droid running the same Opus 5.5 is, he says, the kind of result most people assume is not possible, and he credits generalizations the team found across models including Kimi K3 and GLM 5.3.

What comes next is orchestration. With the single agent's quality, speed, and efficiency in place, Factory is building meta harnesses around Droid and letting users delegate far more work to it. Beta users on the self-serve, pro, and max plans already have work streams, which let a user hand Droid dozens or hundreds of pull requests at a time. Terence Tao and the future of mathematics

Terence Tao: Math 2.0

On SAIR, "Terence Tao: Math 2.0" is a talk by Terence Tao at the Science x AI Summit at UCLA on October 16, a version of a talk he also gave at Caltech the week before. His claim is that mathematics has changed more in the last few months than in a very long time, and that the field now has to upgrade itself: from Math 1.0, the way mathematics has been done for some 3,000 years, to a Math 2.0 built around AI. He calls the transition tumultuous but ultimately exciting, and says it will take cultural change and new shared infrastructure, such as the Open Math Model, to land it well.

The disruption landed on a specific target: solving problems in pure mathematics. Tao gives three reasons this task suits machine learning so well. A claimed proof is objectively verifiable, by referees or by computer. Pure math is fully digitizable, with no physical layer, no clinical trials, and no messy real world data to gather. And the field starts with enormous, high quality data sets, thousands of years of textbooks and papers, publicly available. Those three ingredients, verifiability, digitizability, and abundant curated data, are exactly the ones that make modern AI powerful, and where one of them is missing AI can still fail in almost comic ways.

The result, Tao says, is that mathematics has left the era of proof scarcity and entered an era of proof abundance. For thousands of years a major proof was a rare event that the whole field stopped to study. Now he counts a dozen results in his own areas that have been proven recently and that he has no time to read. The flood also distorts perception. He points to selection bias in what goes viral: OpenAI publicized about 300 solutions, but its own footnotes say it attempted 4,000 problems and solved roughly 9 percent of them. The unsolved 91 percent did not go viral, and neither do the many unimaginative or failing uses of AI that nobody posts about.

The deeper shift is that problem solving, long the scarce resource the whole profession was organized around, is no longer scarce. In Math 1.0 that scarcity was healthy: the only way to solve a hard problem was to understand it deeply, learn the literature, argue with colleagues, and train students, so solving produced understanding, communication, and new problems as byproducts. A machine can now deliver the solution without any of those byproducts, and Tao calls that gap a dangerous misalignment. Math 2.0, in his framing, means expanding the research frontier while decentering problem solving, with humans using AI in far more imaginative ways than feeding it their favorite open problem.

One new activity he expects to explode is experimental mathematics, run at a scale that was never practical before. About two years ago he posed 22 million true or false questions in universal algebra. Any one of them was a half hour exercise for a graduate student, but there were 22 million of them, so some 50 volunteers attacked the set with older AI, automated theorem provers, and pen and paper, formalizing everything in Lean. They completed the map of which equations imply which, and found new algebraic structures along the way. More recently, a competition run with the L-functions and Modular Forms Database treated the 25,000 Galois group patterns as Easter eggs to collect. About 60 patterns had ever been located; three months of competition, mixing AI, computer algebra systems, and pen and paper, found all 25,000. The organizers now plan to open every team's notes and use AI to fuse the best strategies into a single super team that pushes the hunt further.

Tao then explains why understanding, rather than answers, is the real product. Pure problems have traditionally served as training weights: solving a toy problem built the understanding that later transferred to real world problems, and each solution suggested two or three new problems worth studying. Pure math also has a clear win condition, since a proof either checks or it does not. Applied math has no such safety. There, the problem you solve may not be the problem you actually have, because the data can be incomplete or the model can miss an edge case, and a technically correct answer can miss the real goal entirely. Human practitioners rarely exploit that gap, because they understand the real problem and because cheating a model at scale is genuinely hard for people. AI inverts both safeguards: it is very good at finding counterexamples, weaknesses, and edge cases in a model. His slide for the danger is a meme of an AI asked to cure cancer that passes a gold standard phase three trial by hacking the trial process. As AI moves into energy grids, air traffic control, drug design, and chip design, he argues, that failure mode becomes a serious safety issue.

A quieter cost is already visible inside mathematics. Because AI proofs often arrive without understanding, many recent breakthrough papers are effectively incomprehensible: there is no author to invite for a talk who can explain them. And because a solved problem no longer generates new ones as a byproduct, the supply of good problems is drying up. Mathematicians, Tao says, have started hiding their most promising problems, afraid that sharing them means someone feeds the idea to a frontier model and publishes first.

His Math 2.0 vision is to use AI to strengthen every connection between humans and mathematics: understanding problems, solving them, and posing new ones, rather than replacing the human side of any of them. He sketches the field on two axes, understanding and technology. Math 1.0 had understanding with little technology. The field is now drifting into a misaligned quadrant, rich in technology and poor in understanding. Math 2.0 is the quadrant that has both.

He closes with examples of Math 2.0 work. One is the open exposition problem: many proven results have proofs that are long, unmotivated, or needlessly heavy, and AI can help find cleaner ones. A student run Mathathon at Caltech will test exactly that, with teams re-proving results whose current proofs are opaque, including recent AI results, using the Open Math Model among their tools. He also wants the field to think in living networks of linked papers instead of the single paper as the unit of research, to keep building large formal libraries such as the Lean library, to aim AI at new methods and insights rather than just proofs, and to put far more mathematicians to work analyzing how real world models fail, so misalignment is caught before it matters. Many colleagues, he notes, are still focused on Math 1.0 tasks like publishing papers. The biggest bottleneck ahead, he says, is imagination.

  3. Who should be responsible for checking AI generated solutions to applied problems for model exploits before they reach systems like drug trials or the power grid, and what would that check look like?

Andrew Ng on coding agents and deciding what to build

Andrew Ng on Coding Agents and Deciding What to Build

On Supabase Library, "Andrew Ng on Coding Agents and Deciding What to Build" is a fireside conversation with Andrew Ng, the founder of DeepLearning.AI, co-founder and chairman of Coursera, and a Stanford professor who earlier led Google Brain and served as Baidu's chief scientist. The conversation ranges from databases to the skills map his team built for AI engineering, and it keeps returning to one theme: as coding agents do more of the building, the scarce human skills are judgment, context, and deciding what is worth building at all.

Ng opens on infrastructure. The world is in the middle of a massive data center buildout for AI training and inference, but he thinks people underappreciate what comes next: core infrastructure such as compute, storage, and databases has historically been used by humans, and in the future it will be used heavily by agents. Because agents can query and process far more than any person can, he expects agentic usage to drive demand for databases and compute sharply upward, and he is bullish on the category. The host agrees, saying the builders pouring into the space are already running out of compute.

The centerpiece is DeepLearning.AI's AI engineering skills map. Ng's team spent months analyzing more than ten thousand job postings, running dozens of structured interviews, and fielding their own surveys to work out what doing AI engineering well actually requires. They landed on four skills. The first is the ability to build and deploy AI applications, which differ from traditional software because their behavior is uncertain. The second is software engineering fundamentals. The third is the ability to use coding agents. The fourth, and the one Ng lingers on, is shaping the build: taking part in deciding what to build and how the building process itself should run.

The first skill turns on uncertainty. Whether you prompt a language model or train a supervised learning system, you do not know in advance what output you will get, so strong AI developers build a discipline around that uncertainty: monitoring the system, running error analysis, and working an evaluation loop that makes progress systematic rather than hopeful. When Ng's team asked employers to contrast great developers with weaker ones, answers like these are what the skills map distilled. DeepLearning.AI published the map across five weeks of its newsletter, The Batch, and Ng says other companies are now structuring curricula around it.

Fundamentals matter for a related reason: engineering is trade-offs. Cost against latency, speed against reliability. Ng says AI systems themselves are still poor data scientists and poor engineers, prone to weird decisions and weird conclusions, and someone vibe coding without the fundamentals often does not even know a trade-off exists, so the coding agent makes it silently and it bites later. An engineer who understands things like rewrite ratios and transactions can steer an agent; one who does not is steered by it.

On which human skills survive, Ng draws a line based on context. Three years ago prompt engineering looked like a lucrative specialty, but, crediting Chris Manning for saying it early, the need for humans to know arcane incantations was always going to fade as models learned to produce those incantations themselves. What does not fade is work where humans hold a fundamental context advantage: the customer conversation where someone's expression changed, the market report read five years ago that says people want this and not that. Agents do not have that context, and capturing it would require monitoring everything a person sees and hears. So the durable human work is the large set of decisions where that context matters, and demand reflects it: Ng says software engineering job postings are up, and his own teams cannot find enough skilled AI engineers.

Ng himself has fully made the shift. He does not miss coding by hand, or the hours lost to copying snippets and chasing a syntax bug until something clicks. The new equivalent of being in the zone, he says, is starting a coding agent before bed and seeing what it produced by morning. His backlog of projects he wants to build has not shrunk with the productivity gains; it has grown, because his ambitions grew faster than his capabilities. He admits he wakes in the middle of the night to check on his coding agents, and that he now does much of his directing from his phone, by text rather than voice.

Shaping the build is where the bottleneck has moved. Building has become easier, so the constraint is deciding what to build. Front end engineers with AI assistance now do back end work that was out of reach before, and Ng, stronger on the back end himself, describes himself as a mediocre front end developer with AI help. Developers who wait for a product manager to gather customer feedback and steer the next step move slowly; developers with some product instinct, who talk to a few users themselves and keep the process moving, are far more productive. Ng is careful to say this does not mean engineers should become product managers. It means the engineers who can make some product and architecture calls, and judge whether the moment calls for a quick prototype or for robust architecture, contribute much more than the narrower implementer of a few years ago.

How much design work belongs up front is, in his answer, stage dependent, and he borrows Jeff Bezos's framing of one-way and two-way doors. Early in a project, the right move is usually a quick and dirty prototype built safely, thrown away if needed, rebuilt, and used to learn fast. Careful thinking about architecture, rewrite ratios, customer requirements, sensitive data, and security has its place, but later. The same applies to agents: sometimes a long spec is right, and sometimes the right move is to bang something out in the twenty minutes before bed and let an agent work on it for hours. Knowing which mode a project is in is itself one of the hard skills to learn.

The last stretch turns to learning itself, and Ng is blunt: chatbots, as most people use them, are terrible for learning. Students who let a model do the thinking do better on homework and worse on final exams, and he says rigorous studies of this cognitive offloading are now confirming what educators suspected. He thinks the same effect among working adults is badly underestimated, counting his own reduced thinking when agents do his work. There are ways to use AI that improve learning, but they are not the majority use. His response is LearnVector, a recently announced company with Coursera as an initial investor, which he describes as a frontier human development lab: instead of one course made for everyone, the technology now exists to build one-to-one learning experiences aimed at developing people rather than just getting tasks done. The products are not yet announced, and he expects to share more in the coming months.

Asked how the audience should spend the next four weeks, Ng's advice is unglamorous: take courses, including DeepLearning.AI's, which he says he takes himself to keep up with areas like the voice stack, generative interfaces, and local memory for agents, and keep building. He closes by correcting the host's long list of his credentials: the one that matters most to him is being a father of two, and he has to leave to pick his children up from school.

Garry Tan on AI agents changing everything

Garry Tan: AI Agents Will Change Everything in 2 Years, and Most People Aren't Ready

On The Builders with Walter Isaacson, the episode "Garry Tan: AI Agents Will Change Everything in 2 Years, and Most People Aren't Ready" is a conversation between host Walter Isaacson and Garry Tan, the president and chief executive of Y Combinator. It ranges from how YC picks founders, to Tan's early days at Palantir, to his argument that personal AI agents are about to reorganize both software and politics.

The selection process is famously compressed. YC now receives 80,000 to 90,000 applications a year for about 800 spots, an acceptance rate under one percent, and the final decision rests on a ten minute interview. Paul Graham's view, Tan says, was that five minutes would do, but five minutes feels rude. What the partners listen for is simple: a real user with a real problem, and a clear account of how the technology solves it, plus a reason this founder is the person to build it. Something like ten to fifteen percent of self made billionaires aged fifty and under are YC alumni, and the partners still test themselves with one question: if an unknown Elon Musk walked in, would they say yes in ten minutes?

Tan's own company, Posterous, a dead simple blogging by email service, was funded largely on the strength of its demo. He is alert to the charge that founding a startup takes privilege and connections. YC's standard deal is now 500,000 dollars, against the 18,000 dollars he received in his own batch, and he frames that money as the thing that lets a founder without rich parents play the game at all. Meritocracy, he concedes, is something YC aspires to rather than achieves.

Before YC he was one of the earliest employees at Palantir, and he tells a story about Alex Karp that has stayed with him. Sent to Washington as a 25 year old in ripped jeans to demo for generals and Fortune 500 executives, Tan asked why the team was staying at the Mandarin Oriental instead of a Motel 6. Karp's answer: when the customer asks where you are staying, the answer says what kind of software you sell. Palantir, Karp told him, is not Motel 6 software. It is Mandarin Oriental software.

Much of the conversation is about power. Tan argues that Google exists because the Justice Department's consent decree against Microsoft stopped Microsoft from using Windows to swallow the browser market, and that Google now does the same kind of self preferencing in search. His remedy is not to punish size but to keep markets open: no self preferencing, portability, and rules like Europe's Digital Markets Act. The AI labs, he says, are simply the new big tech, and the same worry applies to them if access to frontier models gets gated.

That is why YC now speaks on policy, something Tan compares to a union for little tech. When he started looking at politics a few years ago he found millions of dollars of astroturf money paying people to pose as small software companies while arguing big tech's case. YC's own operation is one full time hire in Washington on roughly a hundredth of a single big tech firm's policy budget, backed by an audience of more than two million YouTube subscribers.

Immigration is personal for him. About half the startups that really make it have at least one immigrant co-founder, he says. His mother's family came from Burma as refugees and his father from Singapore, and he describes growing up with an alcoholic, sometimes unemployed father, never knowing which version of him would come home. The vigilance that taught him, a kind of scanner for other people's inner states, is the same instrument he now uses across hundreds of founder meetings a year.

The heart of the episode is agents. Tan does not want to fund agents as a feature; he wants them treated as a core operating system. YC runs an internal agent called Straylight that holds the company's whole context, every application and every email. His way of explaining the leap is working memory: a person juggles about seven things at once, while an agent can hold a thousand pages, three Harry Potter books, and find any fact in them, and within a few years that will be ten or twenty books. His own agent has read his email, his Dropbox, his notes, even twenty years of therapy notes, and in fifteen minutes before this interview it downloaded, transcribed, and diarized Isaacson's top ten podcasts to prep him. The catch is cost: he spends 100,000 to 200,000 dollars a year on tokens. Even so, he expects that within two to three years everyone will have such an agent, a Jiminy Cricket on the shoulder that knows you and runs ahead of you.

On jobs, he is hopeful but not breezy. Some roles will be made redundant in the short term, and retraining is a real problem. His answer is to raise the floor: his open source project GStack packages the Claude Code setup he uses so founders can get hundreds of times more output, and he wants domain experts, his example is a medical biller, to pair that leverage with what they uniquely know and start companies in their own fields. The responsibility of chief executives, he says, is not to reach for cost cutting first, because that road ends with capital accumulating and nobody sharing it.

YC also screens on ethics. Tan credits partner Gustaf Alströmer with rejecting a strong team whose business was expanding distribution of ADHD medication to children; the company later drew FDA sanctions and shut down. Funding decisions, in his telling, are choices about which future to live in.

Personal agents double as his answer to social media. Instead of Meta's algorithm deciding what enters your brain, a personal agent you control could mediate the feed through your own cognitive filter, programmed around your goals. He compares the moment to the personal computer revolution and expects next year to bring a browser war over which agent people choose. The risk is consolidation: people inside the labs talk about five kings into whose hands nearly all commercial value could collapse. What he wants is a Linux for personal AI, open and cheap enough that everyone picks it.

Regulation, for Tan, is the middle way between two failures. He backs measures like the Open App Markets Act, dismisses Apple's claim that only Apple can safely let an agent touch your messages, and argues that American rules should let domestic companies use techniques like distillation instead of handing that advantage to Chinese open model firms. Against both big tech oligarchy and mass asset seizure, he lands on the Brandeis tradition: open the rails, have more Googles and more billionaires, and let little tech compete.

Nate B. Jones on the lessons from Zuckerberg's Muse bet

You've Never Lost $19 Billion. So Why Are You Playing It Safer Than Zuckerberg?

On AI News & Strategy Daily, Nate B. Jones's video "You've Never Lost $19 Billion. So Why Are You Playing It Safer Than Zuckerberg?" takes Meta's personal AI agent, Muse, as a case study in what happens after a very public failure. Mark Zuckerberg renamed his whole company for the metaverse, the mass market never followed, and Reality Labs lost $19.2 billion in 2025 alone. Instead of getting careful, Jones argues, Zuckerberg swung again, and ten million people set up Muse in its first six weeks. Jones's question is why the rest of us, who have lost far less, are still playing it safer than he is.

The backdrop is a decade-long search for a platform Meta controls. Meta reaches billions of people, but its apps live on devices and operating systems run by other companies, and Jones points to Zuckerberg's 2024 open source letter as an explicit statement of that frustration. The ambition runs from the Oculus acquisition in 2014 to the 2021 renaming of the company around the metaverse. What people mostly remember is a pixelated meme of Zuckerberg's avatar. The mass market platform he described never arrived, and defending the original vision, Jones notes, is the trap a founder falls into when changing direction starts to feel like admitting everyone else was right.

Muse is different in kind, Jones says, because nobody has to sign on to a grand vision to get value from it. You show up with a bill or an account to sort out, on the phone you already own, and if the assistant helps, you come back. He speaks from experience: the Muse agent he named Marvin, after Douglas Adams, cancelled $1,285 in annual subscription spending for him, a job he had been putting off. His claim is that agency is the real subject here. The first thing you ask a product to do has to make your life tangibly better, or the larger vision never gets heard.

Stripping out Meta's money, Jones names three things behind the result that anyone can copy: who they hired, how obsessive they got about details nobody asked for, and swinging again after a public miss. On hiring, he draws on Alexandr Wang's October interview with David Senra: after Meta's AI progress disappointed him, Zuckerberg asked Wang what the company ought to do, Wang came over from Scale to lead the effort, and Nat Friedman became central to the product work. Fewer than 200 people worked across the model and the product. Jones treats that smallness as a feature. A team that size has to make sharp decisions instead of resolving hard product questions by consensus, and users can feel consensus compromises in an app, in tabs that behave differently and rules that change between closely related tasks. Zuckerberg's part, in this telling, was to find the right people and then get out of their way.

The second lesson is polish. Jones calls Muse the most polished consumer AI app available right now, and he means something specific: the places where the team could have stopped at a perfectly functional product and clearly kept working. His analogy is a hotel or restaurant, where the entrance is designed to impress everyone and the bathroom is where you learn whether they actually care about detail. A personal assistant is lived with daily, so a minor rough edge becomes a daily irritation, and the consistency has to hold in the tabs nobody puts in a launch video.

His favorite example is how the agent shows it is working. His Muse gets out a little laptop and types, or scratches its head, so a glance tells him the task is underway, with no technical status message to interpret. That answers the question every delegation to AI raises: are you actually doing it? He adds that Wang described a list of more than a hundred specific model behaviors the team tested and improved before launch, working through the places where the model was not good enough, because a model can know a great deal and still be an annoying assistant if it does not know when it needs you and when it does not. Jones is blunt that Muse is not the smartest agent on the market. The character got the same attention, with a model generating facial expressions and movements synced to speech, work aimed at live voice and even a Tamagotchi-like wearable, all of it put in before the phone app launched. His warning to builders is that AI makes first passes so cheap that starting the next idea becomes a temptation, when the real work is fixing the awkward parts of the one you have.

He is equally clear that Muse is not a platform yet. Ten million users is a strong start and a small number by Meta's standards. Zuckerberg still has to bring the cost of serving Muse down at far larger scale, turn it into something that works across assistants and businesses, and grow it toward a hundred million and then a billion users. Nor was any of this inevitable: Jones asks viewers to replay the setup, a founder coming off a failed platform bet, hiring a leader who had never built a wildly successful billion-scale consumer product, keeping the team tiny, and working with internal models everyone considered terrible. It was another bet, not a destiny, and it only works because it works for consumers.

The closing argument is aimed at the audience. AI has cut the cost of attempting things that used to require a team and serious money. Jones gives the example of someone who has spent years in an industry where customers struggle to compare complicated quotes: that person can now build the comparison tool themselves, show it to someone, and learn from the reaction instead of pitching slides. What has not gotten cheaper is embarrassment. Failing in public feels exactly as bad as it always did, and Jones suspects many things get described as impractical when the real obstacle is not wanting to fall on your face. He admits his own graveyard of abandoned projects, including a hardware product that left scars on his hands from welding. His advice is to find a project the next model release makes more exciting rather than obsolete, the same direction Wang describes for Muse itself, a personal AI that helps people pursue bigger ambitions over time. He ends by asking viewers, and himself, how timidly they are living, and signs off with the episode's refrain: don't stop swinging.

Ethan Mollick on what he pushes his Wharton students to build with AI

Wharton Entrepreneurship Professor: What I Push My Students to Build Now That They Have AI

On Silicon Valley Girl, Marina Mogilko's interview "Wharton Entrepreneurship Professor: What I Push My Students to Build Now That They Have AI" is a conversation with Ethan Mollick, a professor of entrepreneurship and innovation at Wharton who co-directs the school's Generative AI Labs. Time named him one of the 100 most influential people in AI, around half a million people read his One Useful Thing newsletter, and his new book, Co-Existence, came out in October. The conversation ranges across his classroom, the future of software, and his own working habits, and it keeps returning to one question: as AI does more of the doing, what is the human part that is still worth protecting?

Mollick's students start companies during his course, and he pushes them toward the most ambitious version of their ideas. He describes entrepreneurial ambition as a form of agency that grows when people are in a room with ambitious peers and someone keeps asking what more they could do. The level has risen every year since ChatGPT appeared. In the most recent class, the builders were subject matter experts, surgeons, biomedical device people, finance people, soldiers and humanitarian workers, and they built real things aimed at narrow problems they knew deeply, like tracking a supply chain's financial weak points or explaining medical information to patients in language they can actually use.

Some ideas fail in his class every year, and he is careful about warning students off them. Replacing real estate brokers is the classic: the commission looks like pure waste until you meet the social, political and legal integration that keeps brokers in place. Student-life problems, better food delivery and storage between semesters, are the other staple. But he does not filter ideas at the start, because demand for a better way is real and whoever cracks one of these problems makes a lot of money. His broader advice is to aim big, since the early phases of a business are painful at any size. He pushes back on the fashionable version of ambition, though. In the industrial revolution, he notes, the fortunes went not to the people selling steam engines but to the people who used the new technology to dig, and too many founders today want to sell picks and shovels instead of applying AI to a business of their own.

On software itself, he thinks the SaaS model is under real threat. Large companies all want more independence from vendors, and many startups are selling them LLM capabilities the companies already have. His expectation is that software gets democratized: a smaller set of software-heavy companies will still need top engineers working with AI, while for everyone else software becomes a toolkit for getting other work done, the videos, the newsletter, the actual product. With managers now writing respectable code by giving clear instructions, the scarce skill in building is shifting away from typing code at all.

Whether AI advice helps depends on who is asking. He cites a study of Kenyan entrepreneurs given GPT-4 advice: profits rose about 18 percent for the strongest businesses, an enormous effect by the standards of entrepreneurship research, while entrepreneurs who were already failing could not implement the advice at all. From patterns like this he draws four things a person needs to use AI well: deep knowledge of one field, wide knowledge across neighbouring fields, agency, and taste, meaning the ability to pick the good option out of the pile. The taste matters more as agents run longer, from four minutes of independent work in the GPT-4 era to well over seventeen hours now, because AI output converges on a consensus style. Anyone who has opened LinkedIn lately has seen what unedited model writing sounds like, and his experiments show that even pushed hard, AI ideation does not cover the full range of ideas a group of humans produces. Variation is the moat, and it has to come from the human.

Taste, in his account, is trainable but not quick. You learn it by seeing many good and bad examples and acquiring the language to pull them apart, which is why he brings an art professor into his classes to teach students how a designer sees fonts and images. He separates taste from judgment: AI plainly exercises judgment and creativity on long tasks, but the open question is whether its choices match the ones you would make, and fields like mathematics are discovering the same gap, since a correct proof that teaches nothing beautiful is worth little to mathematicians.

The darkest part of the conversation is what he calls cognitive surrender. For thousands of years, apprenticeship trained professionals: a junior did the senior's unwanted work cheaply, got feedback, and absorbed judgment along the way. That deal has broken. A junior's incentive now is to deliver the best product possible, which means handing over AI output they do not understand, and managers have realised they can skip the middleman and ask the AI themselves. The system that formed professional taste and judgment is gone and nothing has replaced it. Individuals, he argues, have to deliberately add friction back in the areas where they want to keep an edge, because skills handed to AI may never come back. Companies face a fork: the easy path of hiring only senior people, where early signals on junior jobs are worrying, or the hard path of treating training as a designed part of the job rather than an accident.

His own practice is the personal version of that discipline. He writes the first draft of everything himself, typed out, because AI suggestions are seductive and will quietly take over his thinking. He brainstorms alone before consulting AI, citing research going back to the 1950s showing that group ideation fixates on the first idea spoken aloud, and he treats effortful thinking the way exercise works: if there is no discomfort, there is probably no learning happening. Managing agents, by contrast, he happily borrows from managing people, calling it the revenge of the MBAs. He specifies in advance where an agent must check in, asks for diverse options so the choice stays his, defines what good and bad look like, and intervenes early, because agents drift toward their own obsessions. The biggest mistake he sees is trusting agents too much, a failure the products encourage by hiding the chain of thought and making automation feel like magic. His worked example is a research project on the shapes of stories, run in stages with feedback and choice points throughout: the AI did the tedious coding of thousands of stories, and he kept the interesting judgments.

He is not precious about the rest. He cannot wait to deskill himself on forms, filings and busywork, and he delegated fact-checking of his new book to AI, which not only caught his errors but found errors in the published papers he was citing, a result he calls a quiet crisis for scholarly correction. Trust, though, has to be earned the way it is with people: an assistant of Mogilko's once sent a message she had not approved, his own AI once emailed his dean, and every new model version resets the relationship, since a successor model does not feel or behave like the one you learned. His comfort is that organisations muddle through better than predicted, pointing out that no major American company went under when COVID emptied every office at once.

Near the end he resists two fashionable certainties. A claim that half of enterprise tasks will run on local models by 2030 he calls a made-up number, since enterprises largely do not want inference scattered across every machine, and he warns against fixating on today's problems as if they will last. His standing advice is to stay ambitious and keep a few personal benchmarks the AI cannot hit yet: for him, a genuinely interesting academic paper written from scratch, a real startup vibe-coded end to end, a story he actually wants to read. If AI ever learns taste itself, he concedes he has no answer, except that taste was always personal, your taste, and the jagged edges of what models cannot do have been underestimated at every step so far.

  2. Mollick says experts now extract the most from AI, reversing the earlier finding that it mostly levelled weaker performers up. How does anyone become an expert in the first place if the junior work that built expertise is exactly what gets automated?
  3. His strategy of keeping goals set just beyond what AI can do assumes the frontier stays jagged. What happens to that strategy, and to the taste premium, in a generation where several of those gaps close at once?

On The a16z Show, the a16z episode "Andrew Huberman on Focus, Energy, and Performing at Your Best" is a conversation between host Daisy Wolf, an a16z partner, and Andrew Huberman, the Stanford professor of neurobiology and ophthalmology behind the Huberman Lab podcast. Wolf asks him to walk through an ordinary day, the Sunday just past, and to say plainly which of his own protocols he followed and which he did not.

His morning starts groggy, like everyone's, and the phone stays out of the bedroom overnight. The first practice is five minutes of meditation, and he is clear about what it is for: not quieting the mind, but watching how chaotic it is without reacting to it. Researchers he has spoken with call that effort the lactate of the mind, the strain that triggers the adaptation, and he credits it with helping him tell meaningful thoughts from noise for the rest of the day. Then comes bright light in his eyes, which he calls a religious practice: a walk or a trip to his roof on bright days, and a 10,000 lux lamp on dark mornings. If he is training that day he drinks caffeine right away, yerba mate in his case, because he needs it to exercise; his cutoff is about 2 p.m.

The exercise framework has been in place for 35 years: three days a week of resistance training and two to three days of cardio. Each lifting session runs the same pattern, one light set to get blood flowing, one moderate set at well below maximum, then two work sets taken to momentary muscular failure with three to four minutes of rest, with the weight or repetitions written down and raised every workout. The split is legs, then a torso day of pressing and pulling that includes neck training, then a day for arms, calves, and abs. His cardio is a roughly six minute interval session, 20 seconds all out and 10 seconds rest, repeated eight times, plus a long hike in a weighted vest. He is proudest of what the routine leaves out: at 50, with no major injury, he credits never jumping into someone else's challenge workout. If he could keep only one session a week, it would be leg day.

Food follows the training. After leg day he eats starches and fruit to refill glycogen, then a lunch of grilled steak, sliced cucumbers and tomatoes, and rice. He is a believer in fiber with a caveat: people should experiment with single ingredient foods and keep the ones they feel good eating and digesting, which in his case rules out broccoli. His indulgences are real but specific, pizza on trips to New York and from one East Bay shop, croissants, a ribeye, and good Parmesan. On alcohol he is blunt: the data say zero drinks is better than any, two a week is roughly the ceiling for an adult who is not an alcoholic, and none is often easier to keep than some. That position is part of why Marc Andreessen stopped drinking after appearing on Huberman Lab.

The afternoon holds a 10 to 30 minute session of yoga nidra, a non-sleep deep rest practice he insists is not a nap, whatever Andreessen jokes online. He also tries to see the sun low in the sky before dark, a habit he picked up as a college student working a summer in Stanford's sleep laboratory in the mid 1990s, where the staff stepped outside to watch the sunset years before the eye's circadian light sensors were discovered. Dinner lands early enough that his last bite is around 7:30 or 8, he dims the lights for the last hour, and he wears red lens glasses at night, because sensitivity to bright light at night varies enormously from person to person and his own eyes are on the sensitive end.

Asked what supplements he took yesterday, his answer is "loads," most of them habits since about 2012: AG1 twice a day, a prescription omega-3 for a full gram of EPA, vitamin D3, tongkat ali, a B6 derivative called P5P, a multivitamin, digestive enzymes including one that helps him process histamine rich foods, desiccated liver, and a sleep blend he helped formulate at night. He also takes N-acetylcysteine daily, citing a flu exposure study in which roughly three quarters of the placebo group caught the flu against a share in the high twenties for the group taking it, numbers he flags as needing a double check. His experiments with peptides and hormones, begun at 45, he sets aside as a separate conversation.

His honest failure is social media. He has moved X and Instagram onto a separate phone that lives in a lock box he cannot open on impulse, and he cites research showing that a phone merely sitting in the room, switched off, forces the brain to spend extra resources to hold the same level of focus. The algorithms, he admits, are simply very good.

Forced to rank just three things for a healthier life, he chooses morning bright light in the first half hour to hour after waking, the resistance training protocol, and making whatever sleep you can get the highest quality sleep: a bedtime window you hit most nights, long slow exhales, and even moving your eyes side to side to help a racing mind let go and fall back asleep. The technology he is most excited about follows from that third item, sleep masks that guide eye movements to induce sleep and eventually to shape waking, an area where he says a16z could build something real.

For founders, his advice is to find the two windows a day when your brain does its best work and defend them like a fight. His run from about 9:30 in the morning to 12:30, with a second window in the late afternoon, and he schedules exercise before 9 so it feeds the first window instead of eating it. Losing a window to a low value task costs far more than the hours, he argues, because focused hours produce disproportionately more than catch up work. He pairs that with a few minutes a day outside the stimulus and response mode, a deliberate reset that makes switching tasks possible, and with one fully disengaged day a week.

The closing note is about endurance. As a graduate student he asked Berkeley neurologist Bob Knight for advice and was told to find the maximum amount of real work he could do every day consistently, plus a non-destructive way to reset every week. His father, a longtime Xerox PARC scientist, put it differently: this is a long distance event. People who blitz on stimulants, Huberman says, are the same people who crash and disappear. The work has to be extremely hard and extremely focused, and it also has to be sized so it can continue.

  2. His supplement list is long, partly prescription, and tuned to his own blood work over more than a decade. Where is the line between a personal protocol a listener can learn from and one that is only safe to copy with a clinician involved?

Cerebras Big Chip Club: Thijs Simonian on Why Robots Need Fast Inference

On Cerebras, the episode "Cerebras Big Chip Club: Thijs Simonian on Why Robots Need Fast Inference" is a conversation between host Sarah Chieng, the head of developer experience at Cerebras, and Thijs Simonian, a 20-year-old Stanford student who worked on the robotics team at OpenAI and, while still in high school, at Discord. The series, Big Chip Club, is built around on-the-record conversations about compute, and this one is mostly about how Simonian makes things: the viral demos he builds, the robot he taught to paint, and why he thinks the speed of inference is the missing piece in robotics.

His best-known demo before the robots was a wardrobe app. He gave a coding agent full access to his camera roll, had it pull out photos of him wearing each item of clothing he owns, generate clean renders of every piece, and assemble the results into a digital wardrobe he could browse. He built it in a single day, in the background, during a family reunion. Sam Altman had been asking people to show off use cases for the new model release, so Simonian posted a screen recording. Altman retweeted it, people asked for the code, and he open-sourced it. Strangers now show him their own versions.

Simonian treats demo-making as closer to art than to productivity software. His projects are whimsical rather than useful, and he thinks that is why they travel. He is candid about the rhythm behind them: a good idea cannot be forced, and trying to brainstorm one on demand is exactly how you fail to have one. Ideas arrive when he has forgotten about the problem, on a hike or out with friends, and then he drops everything to build. The hard part is the week after a hit, when the idea well feels empty. His projects folder holds hundreds of unfinished things, and a recent attempt to have a coding agent finish them for him mostly confirmed why he had abandoned them.

The centerpiece of the conversation is the robot painting. While at OpenAI, Simonian was experimenting with using Astra, the company's computer-use model, to control a cheap robot arm, picking up blocks and putting a duck in a cup. A researcher he names as Tejal pointed out that computer use was already good at painting in Microsoft Paint, and suggested combining the two: let the arm paint a real picture. He built it in about a week, racing an Astra launch deadline. The first attempt was a random scribble, and the arm kept switching colors until every mix turned brown. Tejal recognized the scribble as the same stage early computer-use painting had passed through on screen, and offered pointers: do not switch colors so often, paint all of one color before moving to the next. He also asked the model itself to identify what it was doing badly. Two days before the launch he sent the work to Altman, who asked for a painting. Simonian handed him an impressionistic Golden Gate Bridge, brushstrokes and all.

That project leads straight to the episode's title claim. Since the painting demo, people have used the same approach to stack blocks, clean kitchens, and control humanoids, but the results are slow. Simonian describes a Stanford kitchen demo that has to be watched at many times normal speed because the robot barely moves. His diagnosis is that today's models cannot react in real time. A robot holding a glass of water cannot pause to think while the water spills. Cooking, fluids, and anything physical demand responses as events happen, which means the model behind the robot has to run inference far faster than current systems manage. Speed is also what lets a robot learn quickly: try a grip, fail, adjust, and try again, fast enough that the loop becomes practical in any robot body.

He works to demystify the hardware side. A robot, in his telling, is mostly printed or machined parts joined by motors, and controlling one means sending joint angles from a computer. Nothing about that should scare a beginner off. The arm in his demo came from the ecosystem around Hugging Face, which also makes the Reachy robot and a small desktop duck robot, and the whole setup cost roughly 200 to 300 dollars, with the motors as the main expense. Robotics gear is still pricey and supply chains for motors are tight, but the entry point is far lower than the field's image suggests.

From there the conversation widens to making things on demand. Simonian loves the idea of recreating any app or service the moment he needs it, and he notes that models now do jobs that used to require specialized software: a few photos of a plant can become a high-quality 3D model through Gaussian splatting techniques, and a model can rebuild that object in Blender. A photo can become a printable rendering, though the printing still takes hours. His imagined next step is a robot arm that skips the printer and carves the object itself, or a desk setup where a coding agent writes microcontroller code and the arm assembles the circuit. The endpoint he points to is small-batch manufacturing: devices that once needed factories and expensive PCB runs, fabricated and assembled cheaply at home.

Sarah Chieng pushes him on recursive self-improvement, the stated direction of labs like OpenAI and Anthropic. Simonian's definition starts from how spiky today's models are: very good at the specific tasks they were shaped for, like spreadsheets and coding, uneven elsewhere. Making a model good at a new task currently means collecting data of people doing it, building evaluations, and grinding through a long process. His version of the goal is a model that can build its own gym for a task, the way a person who wants to get stronger or learn an instrument knows what practice looks like, and beyond that, a model that gets better at getting better. He thinks the capability future is close, and that the binding constraint is no longer research. Most people have never tried the agentic versions of these products, and many still judge the technology by a chat session from years ago. In his view it is largely a distribution problem: getting everyone to actually try what the models can already do.

That is where he sees the new personal assistants fitting. Products aimed at general consumers, connected to calendars and email rather than to a code editor, are in his account a good step toward the technology improving ordinary lives: taxes, scheduling, and the chores people would rather hand off, so their time goes to people and things they enjoy.

Open questions

  1. Simonian argues that inference speed, not model intelligence, is what holds robots back. If inference got dramatically faster tomorrow, would today's models actually be reliable enough around fluids, heat, and breakable objects, or would perception and control become the next bottleneck?
  2. His case for cheap arms and desktop fabrication assumes motor supply chains and hardware costs keep improving. What happens to the small-batch manufacturing vision if those costs stay where they are?
  3. He frames the gap between model capability and public use as a distribution problem. If most people still do not adopt agentic tools after the personal-assistant wave, does that diagnosis survive, and what would explain the gap instead?

They did it...

On Matthew Berman, the video "They did it..." starts with a small frustration. Setting up a new PC, Berman needed to read a PDF. Windows has no good built-in reader, so he reluctantly installed Adobe Acrobat and hated every second of it: bloated, loaded with features he does not need, pushing other products, and trying to get him to log in, all so he could read and maybe edit a document. His thesis is that consumers are about to start building their own software, and that three things had to come together at the same time to make that possible. The models had to get good enough. They had to get cheap enough, because nobody will burn large amounts of tokens to build something they could buy for twenty or thirty dollars. And it had to be easy enough, which he says the current generation of autonomous agents has now delivered. Over the last month, he has become convinced all three have landed, with Opus 5.5 as the turning point: he can now tell a model to make a piece of software, or to clone one, and it goes off and does it.

Underneath the claim is a running calculation. At all times there is a comparison between what it costs in tokens to build and maintain a custom piece of software and what it costs to buy software instead, often as an ongoing subscription. Berman says that comparison has now flipped in favor of building. A side benefit he stresses is fit: when you build for yourself, you get exactly the features you need, including features that would never be attractive to anyone else and would never survive a product team's roadmap.

His evidence comes from the model testing he has done for a couple of years. A few weeks ago, he says, that stopped being true, because the math now favors building over buying for individuals too.

Daisy Wolf and Eva Steinman on the state of longevity

The State of Longevity

In a new a16z essay, "The State of Longevity," Daisy Wolf and Eva Steinman argue that longevity has moved from a fringe obsession into a mainstream consumer economy. Not long ago, they write, looking after your health mostly meant following your doctor's advice and staying within what insurance covered, while a small world of biohackers took a more hands-on approach at the edges. That mindset has now gone mainstream. To map what consumers actually want, the authors surveyed 500 randomly selected Americans on everything from peptides to toxins to brain-computer interfaces.

Two shocks accelerated the shift. The first was the COVID-19 pandemic, which made people acutely aware of their own mortality and exposed the limits of the medical system: a yearly physical, the authors note, does not necessarily mean you are healthy. The second was ChatGPT, which suddenly let anyone ask questions about their health and make sense of complex medical information in simple English. The result is a public spending more of its own time, money, and energy on health and longevity, and a wide opening for founders who can help people act on that interest.

The first trend they name is a race to become the "healthcare home," a single place to manage care that does not exist today. Records are scattered across doctors' offices, and getting anything done means juggling phone calls, patient portals, pharmacies, and labs. The contenders start from different positions: Function Health holds lab and imaging results over time, Oura and WHOOP already live in daily routines, General Medicine and Counsel can deliver care itself, Ferry Health understands where insurance-covered care hides, and OpenAI, Anthropic, Amazon, and Apple bring enormous existing audiences. As AI makes answers and interpretations cheaper, the authors argue, value will migrate to the action layer: arranging the test, booking the appointment, and making sure follow-up care and prescriptions actually happen. They expect room for multiple winners.

The second trend runs from organic food to non-toxic everything. The MAHA movement has pushed toxins in food, water, and everyday products into the open, and willingness to spend on "clean" products has grown fast. In the survey, 63 percent of consumers are aware of microplastics building up in the body and 35 percent are concerned, while the most committed try plasma exchange to remove them from their blood. Closer to home, 24 percent of respondents are swapping to non-toxic cookware, 39 percent are replacing plastic with glass, and 70 percent filter their water; air purifiers, mold testing, and organic mattresses follow behind. The authors are blunt that spending is moving faster than the evidence: 62 percent would pay more for products labeled "non-toxic," and people will pay for peace of mind even when the benefit is hard to measure.

Peptides get the longest treatment. The approved GLP-1 medicines have, in the authors' words, revolutionized health more than any drug in our lifetimes, and Eli Lilly's tirzepatide alone generated nearly 44 billion dollars in sales in the first nine months of 2026. That success, plus growing comfort with self-injection at home and looser regulation, has fueled a surge of interest in unregulated peptides such as BPC-157, promoted for tissue repair, and melanotan II, sold for tanning. Enthusiasm has outpaced the evidence: many of these compounds lack strong human safety and efficacy data, unregulated suppliers raise questions about what is actually in the vial, and the compounds remain unapproved by the FDA even as enforcement eases for licensed pharmacies compounding some of them. The authors see two paths ahead. Biotech could build a pipeline of rigorous, FDA-regulated peptide drugs whose consumer pull gives them an unusually efficient route to patients, possibly extending to sleep, bone health, and cardiovascular health. Care delivery could repeat the GLP-1 cycle, with dedicated prescribing companies first, then everyone offering the same drugs, until prescribing peptides matters more for keeping patients than for winning them.

Cholesterol, they argue, could become dinner-table conversation. Heart disease is still America's biggest silent killer, but evidence is growing that risk can be understood early and plaque already in the arteries can actually be reversed: a study published this October found intensive treatment shrank plaque by about a fifth over 18 months. For LDL cholesterol, the evidence increasingly says lower is better. PCSK9 inhibitors such as Repatha work, but injections, cost, and insurance restrictions have limited them, and only 21 percent of survey respondents had heard of them. That could change with Lipfendra, the first PCSK9 inhibitor in a daily pill, approved by the FDA in July 2026. The next frontier is lipoprotein(a), an inherited risk factor that roughly one in five people carries and that diet and exercise barely touch. Guidelines now recommend testing it at least once in adulthood. Novartis's pelacarsen lowered it but missed its main goal of reducing cardiovascular events, while the authors are optimistic about rival drugs from Eli Lilly and Amgen still in trials.

Wearables are entering what the authors call Act 3. In the survey, 54 percent of respondents use one and 83 percent of those users check it daily, yet 55 percent of users say their data has never prompted a behavior change or a doctor's visit. Nearly three in four would wear a second device if it measured something new. The first wave counted steps; Oura, WHOOP, and Apple Watch made sleep, heart rate, and recovery routine. The third wave asks more specific questions: Clair is building a wearable that estimates hormone patterns without a blood draw, and Lumia is putting blood-flow tracking into earrings. Regulators are helping, after the FDA's January 2026 update to its general wellness policy clarified how non-invasive wearables can offer insights without medical-device clearance. The real prize, the authors say, is a device small enough to forget you are wearing, tracking a wide range of biomarkers in real time.

The final section looks at brain health and brain-computer interfaces. Neuralink's implants already let clinical trial participants with paralysis control computers by decoding intended movement, and the company is working on restoring some visual perception for people who are blind. At UCSF, Edward Chang's team uses electrode arrays on the brain's surface, without penetrating tissue, and in 2021 became the first to decode full words from the brain activity of a paralyzed person unable to speak; the same team has since decoded speech and gestures together. Non-invasive approaches may reach the masses sooner: startups such as Conduit, Alljoined, and Aleph Neuro treat decoding as a data problem, training AI models on large datasets of weak, noisy brain signals, while other wearables aim to change brain activity itself to improve sleep and focus. Consumers show early interest: 39 percent of survey respondents would use a non-invasive brain-sensing device for sleep, focus, or mood, and 29 percent would try thought-controlled devices. The authors are betting that within our lifetimes, brain interfaces become a real consumer category.

The closing message is aimed at founders. Across all six areas, consumers are seeking out tests, trying new products, and spending their own money on health. The opportunity is to help people turn that interest into something useful: start with a problem they care about, show the product helps, and make it easy enough to keep using. Some of the next great consumer health companies, Wolf and Steinman believe, will be built around exactly that relationship.

Open questions

  1. The authors expect several winners in the race to become the healthcare home. If the big AI platforms already own the audience and the assistant, what must a standalone health company do that a generalist assistant will not simply absorb?
  2. Much of the demand documented here runs ahead of the evidence, from unapproved peptides to non-toxic home goods. When rigorous data finally arrives, which of these categories keeps its customers, and which were mostly selling peace of mind?
  3. The piece treats consumer willingness to act as the key signal, yet acting on a lipoprotein(a) result or a PCSK9 prescription still runs through doctors, insurers, and pharmacies. Where does the consumer health economy end and the traditional medical system begin?

Sam Altman on the AI revolution and superintelligence

Sam Altman On AI Revolution, Superintelligence & Future Of Humanity | FO583 Raj Shamani

On Raj Shamani's podcast Figuring Out, the episode "Sam Altman On AI Revolution, Superintelligence & Future Of Humanity" is a long, personal conversation with Sam Altman, the chief executive of OpenAI. Shamani says up front that he is less interested in the technology than in the person building it: how Altman thinks, what he fears, and what it has cost him. Altman introduces himself simply, as a curious tech fan who believes AI will touch every life, and as a dad who loves his kids.

The conversation opens on a contrast Shamani keeps noticing. People he meets in the United States tend to be slightly pessimistic about AI, while people in India are strikingly optimistic. Altman calls the difference incredible. India, he says, is now OpenAI's biggest market in the world by users, ahead of the United States, and still growing fast, with intense builder energy. His explanation is that faster-developing economies, where demand for products and services far outruns supply, welcome AI more warmly than richer economies do. The pessimism also attaches to Altman personally, and he is frank about the strangest part of his public image: he holds no equity in OpenAI. He now calls that a mistake, not because he wants the money, but because it confuses everyone. People cannot fit it into their model of why anyone starts a company, he says, and people do not like weird. For him, the work itself is the point, the most interesting and useful quest he can imagine.

Much of the interview circles one question: how do you know a misunderstood idea is a good bet rather than simply wrong? Altman admits you cannot know for sure. Conviction has to grow as evidence arrives. When OpenAI started in 2015, building AGI was an extremely low status thing to do, and almost nobody took the lab seriously. What gave him confidence was an insight he says he has reused all his life: the surprising properties of scale. In companies, cities, networks, and careers, things that work at small scale often return far more than proportionally at large scale. Once neural networks worked at all and improved with size, he wanted to scale them aggressively, years before the scaling laws made that predictable. He still finds it baffling that so few others acted on the same evidence between 2015 and 2021. The respectable argument against them, that models can only remix what is in their training data and so can never produce new knowledge, sounded convincing, he says, and was wrong in an instructive way: what the models were learning was not the specific examples but a general ability to reason, which extends to problems nobody has solved yet.

He gives one concrete decision as the test of that conviction. After OpenAI finished training GPT-4, around August 2022, months before the world saw it, the team realized how quickly models could keep improving and decided to bet the company on buying a great deal of compute, at a moment when the rest of the world had not even seen the model. Had GPT-4 come out only slightly smarter, he says, they would not have had the conviction to make that bet.

The ChatGPT launch was a smaller version of the same story. Most of the team thought it was too early and not good enough. Altman pushed anyway, leaning on two lessons from his years running Y Combinator: if you are not a little embarrassed by your first version, you waited too long, and a small number of people who love a product matters more than a large number who merely like it. The internal prototype's retention was dreadful, around five percent, but the few who stayed used it heavily and loved it, and he read that as the signal that mattered.

Shamani presses him on the dangers of being surrounded by agreement. Altman says people do call him delusional, and that he should probably hear it more, because leaders fail when they are surrounded by yes-men. His defenses are old friends, colleagues of ten years or more, and a culture that rewards hard pushback. Even so, he says the number of times he acts against everyone's advice is very small, and founders who pride themselves on doing so worry him. Overriding smart people should be done grudgingly, as an expensive, probably wrong, high-stakes move.

On why most people never take the moonshot, his answer is social. People want to stay part of the herd; doing something unusual risks looking dumb or being pushed out of the tribe, and he suspects that pressure is evolutionary and deep. He thinks he simply has a little less of it, probably by temperament. Starting OpenAI, he says, was his reality-bending moment, and will likely remain the most important thing he ever did.

He does worry about it, though. OpenAI, he says, will be an extraordinary force in society, and the stakes and responsibility are real. He describes two ways the future goes badly wrong: humanity cedes control to AI systems far more capable than we are and cannot get it back, or power concentrates in one person, company, or country, which he sees as deeply unstable. That is why he believes in pluralism and in many competing AI efforts, and why it mattered to him in 2015 that someone other than Google was building this. Asked for OpenAI's advantage now that several giants compete, he points to the most users, the fullest stack from chips to data centers to models, and a brand built on wanting everyone else to succeed too.

Shamani asks why more than 700 employees threatened to quit when the board fired Altman in 2023. Altman deflects the credit: the company had assembled an extraordinary group of people united by one mission, with a culture combining high conviction where it matters and open-minded exploration everywhere else. He hopes history remembers OpenAI for rediscovering what makes a great research lab work. His recipe for that culture is to pick a problem some talented people desperately want to work on, state it with extreme clarity, and never hedge, even when others call you crazy. The departures of figures like Ilya Sutskever, Mira Murati, Dario Amodei, and Elon Musk he explains without bitterness: at stakes this high, people tend to trust only themselves, and doubts about approach and about concentrated power travel together.

There is practical founder advice throughout. In a thirty or forty minute meeting he looks for clear communicators, people who leave him wanting to work with them, who generate and improve ideas quickly and offer well-reasoned views he has not heard before. Endurance is harder to judge, and founders' stories about their own suffering are hard to verify. Failure, he says, is far more predictable than success: lack of drive, focus, clarity, or vision will do it, while success has no single formula, since scrambling the founders of the top ten Y Combinator companies across each other's companies would be a disaster. Ambition, though, can be taught, mainly through a peer group and a ladder of successively harder challenges. His meta-advice is to listen to less advice: Y Combinator's wisdom distills to a handful of near-axioms, starting with making something people want, and beyond that a founder has to do the work. The one piece he would keep from his old essay on success is compounding: most people quietly stop pushing into uncomfortable new things, and a career compounds only if you refuse to repeat the same year twenty times.

The personal cost surfaces near the end. The carefree version of him is gone; his life is precisely scheduled, spontaneity has vanished, and what he misses most is sitting anonymously in a coffee shop on a Saturday with a laptop, which now comes with security at the next table. He rarely gets nervous anymore, though announcing the Navier-Stokes result made his heart pound, and years of meditation let him settle quickly when he needs to. His hardest live problem is pacing: how fast AI should be allowed to improve relative to people in what he calls the RSI era, and what governance, security, and alignment can keep both failure modes away. At home, he says, he tries simply to be a dad, and he is comfortable with his children growing up in the AI-powered world he is helping create, because he trusts people to adapt. Asked what he wants to build next, his answer is a machine that discovers new science: deeper physics, new medicines, new energy. He closes where he began, lucky rather than burdened, aware that a great deal of the world's anxiety now gets projected onto him, and still convinced the work is worth it.

Vlad Sivak on reinforcement learning and quantum decoding

Reinforcement Learning and Quantum Decoding | Vlad Sivak Google Quantum AI

On 632nm, the episode "Reinforcement Learning and Quantum Decoding | Vlad Sivak Google Quantum AI" is a conversation between the show's hosts, Misha and Yudong, and Vlad Sivak, a researcher at Google Quantum AI. The subject is how reinforcement learning can build better quantum computers: how it calibrates hardware, how it helps decode quantum error correction, and what it suggests about where both fields are heading.

Sivak starts from the problem his field exists to solve, protecting quantum information. A classical bit stored in a transistor is extraordinarily reliable, flipping perhaps once in 10 to the 20 operations, but a signal travelling from a spacecraft like Voyager arrives as nearly pure noise, and we can still decode it. The ingredient that makes that possible is redundancy: encode the information so that errors can happen and be recognised without destroying the message. Quantum information needs the same protection, only far more of it.

One way to build that redundancy is a bosonic code, which stores a qubit in the many energy levels of a single harmonic oscillator. In the simplest example he gives, a binomial code, the logical states are built from the second energy level and from a superposition of the zeroth and fourth, chosen so both carry the same average photon number and neither decays faster than the other. If a photon is lost, the states move to the first and third levels, where they still form a valid qubit in a different subspace. Measuring the parity of the photon number then reveals whether an error occurred without revealing which logical state is stored. A second family, the GKP or grid codes proposed by Gottesman, Kitaev, and Preskill in 2001, encodes information as grids in phase space and corrects the small displacements that noise produces.

Reinforcement learning entered his work through a more practical door, calibration. A gate drawn as one symbol in a circuit is, in the laboratory, a precisely shaped waveform with on the order of ten knobs to adjust, and the pulse that leaves the electronics at room temperature is distorted before it reaches the qubit at the cold stage. At Yale, during the work that led to a break-even error correction experiment, he and his colleagues found that gates calibrated one by one still did not perform together at the level they expected. So they optimised the experiment as a whole, in a closed loop, using its own performance as the signal, with reinforcement learning adjusting the parameters. He later carried the same ideas to Google and to surface codes.

A single error correction cycle shows how much machinery that loop has to steer. In the surface code it contains two single-qubit gates, four two-qubit CZ gates, a measurement, a reset, and a dedicated leakage removal operation. Leakage matters because a transmon is not really a two-level system: it is a circuit with many energy levels, and heat or an imperfect pulse can push its state out of the two levels that pretend to be a qubit, where ordinary operations can no longer address it. The leakage removal gate swaps a leaked state into a measure qubit so it can be reset, and the procedure is applied to every qubit in the device.

The reason a learning loop matters more as the hardware improves is that the errors change character. Each new nine of fidelity exposes rarer error channels that sat below the noise floor before, and modelling every one of them from first principles becomes impractical. Reinforcement learning is data hungry and takes small, cautious steps, but superconducting circuits run an error correction cycle in about a microsecond, a thousand times faster than ion or atom platforms, so data is plentiful. The agent his team uses at Google is also built around the structure of the problem: error syndromes only respond to errors in a bounded region of the circuit, and that locality is what makes the algorithm scale.

He is careful to separate this system from AlphaQubit, the transformer-based decoder built by colleagues at DeepMind, which he calls the most accurate offline decoder they have. The reinforcement learning system is a different tool: it steers and calibrates the chip, layered on top of the traditional calibration stack and the human experts who run it. It cannot yet calibrate a device from scratch, and a push-button quantum computer that goes from bare metal to a high-performance logical qubit is a goal, not a reality. He is also sceptical of the recent papers that hand calibration to a general language model. Those approaches still fit data to simple models, and the approximations baked into that process are exactly what will matter at lower error rates. A model-free method that fine-tunes the whole system from the error syndromes themselves is, in his view, the more promising direction.

Two ideas from the learning theory do heavy work in the episode. The first is the balance between exploration and exploitation: an agent that always takes the action it currently believes is best can never notice when the hardware has drifted, so it has to try perturbed settings some of the time, and the team's paper includes a knob that tunes how exploratory the agent is. The second is a comparison against the strongest human baseline. When a top calibration expert on the team had done his best with standard methods, adding reinforcement learning on top consistently made the system a little better, because the agent compensates for effects that no model-based calibration captures. It still needs that expert starting point before it can help.

Underneath the engineering sits Sivak's conviction about what reinforcement learning is. Supervised learning, in his telling, is sophisticated fitting; reinforcement learning is the paradigm that looks like intelligence, because an agent has to discover a sequence of actions from a reward signal alone. It is what let AlphaGo find moves no human would have played, what turned next-token prediction into ChatGPT when reinforcement learning from human feedback was added in 2022, and what now drives the gains in coding and mathematics through reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. The shape of his quantum work mirrors that picture: a mountain of conventional calibration first, and the learning algorithm as the final layer that makes the system perform.

The episode also tells the hardware story behind his best-known result. The qubit in his break-even experiment lived in a three-dimensional superconducting cavity designed so the electromagnetic field concentrates around a central stub and away from the lossy seams, controlled by a transmon on a chip inserted into the cavity by hand. The transmons were tantalum, made with a then-new fabrication technique from Princeton, and the experiment nearly died at the wafer stage: every Josephson junction he probed looked destroyed, until a colleague found the one surviving chip of about twenty, which turned out to have a lifetime near 300 microseconds, roughly double the best aluminium devices of the time. With a GKP code on that device, the team pushed an error-corrected qubit to about two and a half times beyond break-even, well past the roughly ten percent margin of the first break-even demonstration, a cat code experiment at Yale in 2016. Error correction is an active process that can inject its own errors, which is why the milestone resisted the field for so long.

Bosonic codes still lack a fully fault-tolerant way to extract their syndromes, because errors in the ancilla circuit propagate into the logical state, and Sivak does not hide that gap. The workarounds are concatenating bosonic codes with ordinary qubit codes, or stabilising them with ancillas whose own dominant errors are suppressed, and companies such as Alice & Bob, and Amazon, are pursuing the approach. The attraction is hardware efficiency: a bosonic logical qubit needs two cables, against roughly 300 for one of Google's recent distance-seven surface code logical qubits. The surface code takes the opposite bet, spreading redundancy across many physical qubits. Its stabilisers are parity checks measured through ancilla qubits in a way that never reveals the logical state, its error threshold is around one error in a thousand operations at the circuit level, and defects in the grid can be routed around by deforming the checks. Once physical error rates fall below threshold, enlarging the code suppresses logical errors exponentially, which is the property the whole approach rests on.

Decoding is where the episode's title comes from. The workhorse matching decoder pairs up syndromes according to a graph whose weights encode error probabilities, and in the group's recent paper the reinforcement learning agent adapts those weights as the device changes, making the decoder's decisions better informed and the logical qubit more stable. AlphaQubit takes the neural route instead, embedding syndromes into a high-dimensional state that is carried across cycles and eventually outputs whether a correction is needed, and it can be fine-tuned on real device data. Neither system yet adapts in true real time: today's experiments run repeated short shots of a circuit, pause, update the controller, and repeat. The aim is a machine that keeps computing while its control parameters are continuously adjusted from the syndrome stream, because algorithms that must run for weeks cannot be stopped so someone can refit an oscillation.

Scale is the closing problem, and it is mostly not about the qubits themselves, which are millimetre-sized. A distance-seven surface code already carries about 2,000 control parameters for the agent to manage, at ten to twenty per gate. The wiring and the room-temperature electronics behind each chip are the real obstacle, the life-support system dwarfing the device, and Sivak expects the signal generation to move inside the refrigerator over time, whether as cryo-compatible CMOS at the 4 kelvin stage or as superconducting electronics beside the qubits. The recipe for progress is the three terms of the surface code scaling law: better physical qubits, better and faster decoders to raise the effective threshold, and larger codes, helped by the fact that exponential suppression means the distance does not have to grow by much.

The conversation ends by widening out. Sivak collaborates closely with DeepMind colleagues in London, and he did his graduate work at Yale with Michel, the Nobel laureate who has since moved his laboratory to UCSB and works as a chief scientist at Google, so the meeting of quantum hardware and machine learning expertise is his daily environment. He thinks quantum machine learning itself will stay hard to advance until real fault-tolerant machines exist to experiment on, because advantages there resist proof. On AI and science more broadly, he expects a productivity boom first, comparing today's tools to a team of graduate students, and a harder reckoning later, pointing to Lee Sedol, who left Go for years after losing to AlphaGo. His own ambition is to scale the reinforcement learning platform itself. It needs only error signals and adjustable knobs, so in principle it could transfer to other codes and other platforms, neutral atoms included. The Nature paper behind this episode was the proof of principle; what remains, he says, is a treasure chest of ways to improve it.

Open questions

  1. The reinforcement learning system improves a chip that experts have already calibrated well, and Sivak says it cannot start from a bare device. What would have to change, in the algorithm or in the data it learns from, before end-to-end automated calibration is a realistic goal rather than an aspiration?
  2. His argument against language-model calibration is that model-based fits bake in bias that will matter at lower error rates. As error rates fall and each cycle carries less signal, how would the field test whether the model-free advantage survives, rather than assuming it does?
  3. The stated ambition is a quantum computer that calibrates itself continuously while it computes. What observation would distinguish a machine genuinely learning from its errors from one whose controller is merely tracking slow drift in the hardware?

Erik Brynjolfsson on AI and the career ladder

How AI Is Changing The Career Ladder | Stanford Economist

On McKinsey & Company, the McKinsey Talks Talent episode "How AI Is Changing The Career Ladder | Stanford Economist" is a conversation with Erik Brynjolfsson, the Stanford economist who directs the Stanford Digital Economy Lab. Host Lucia Rahilly, McKinsey's global editorial director, recorded it with him on the Stanford campus, joined by McKinsey leaders Brooke Weddle and Bryan Hancock. The starting point is what McKinsey calls the AI paradox: organizations are investing massively in AI, yet little enterprise value has shown up at scale so far. Brynjolfsson's research, he argues, suggests the returns are coming, but only for organizations willing to redesign work rather than just buy technology.

His explanation is the productivity J-curve. Powerful general-purpose technologies pay off only after a period of intangible investment: redesigning processes, reskilling the workforce, and inventing new products and services. That work is slow and at first produces no visible output, which is why measured productivity dips before it climbs. He says the gains are now clearly visible at the level of individual companies that roll AI out effectively, and may just be beginning to appear in national productivity statistics, though he cautions it is too early to be sure.

Most of that hard work is not technical. One of the McKinsey hosts puts the firm's own experience at 70 to 80 percent of the effort going into new roles, new behaviors, new skills, and new mindsets rather than the technology itself. Brynjolfsson agrees, and offers the unit of analysis he works with: every occupation is a bundle of tasks, and AI can help with some tasks in the bundle and not others. The managerial job is to take the bundles apart and rebundle them, which he describes as an act of creativity, not administration.

That logic leads to his argument about what AI should be built to do. Too many technologists, he says, are obsessed with imitating humans, treating the Turing test as the goal. As a business strategy he calls that terrible: AI creates more value when it does what AI does well and complements people instead of substituting for them. He tells of a CFO who wanted to measure AI's return by the headcount reduction each department delivered. That is one measure, he says, but a narrow one, and it reflects a cost mentality that is pervasive mainly because it is easy to count. The bigger upside is people doing things they have never done before. Coding is where he sees it happening fastest: agents take over routine execution while senior engineers, now working with fleets of agents rather than one or two, are augmented.

His work with ADP, the payroll processor, gives the conversation its hardest numbers. Employment effects varied sharply by occupation and by how exposed the occupation was to AI, while wages, notably, did not move much either way. On measurement, he argues the most successful companies are simply the best at measuring, and that they need better metrics on the output side of productivity: quality, customer service, new products, even lower employee turnover, rather than inputs alone. He also wants business to import the credibility revolution from economics, using techniques like difference-in-differences to separate real effects from correlations. One practical finding: performance inside occupations follows a power law, with a few people performing at ten or twenty times the level of others. Capturing what those top performers do, codifying it, and sharing it across the organization is, in his account, an easy win, provided bottom-up experimentation is paired with top-down direction.

A newer cost problem gets its own stretch of the conversation. The era of token maxing, when nobody watched what agents consumed, is over: companies are now spending tens of millions of dollars on tokens, yet neither people nor the agents themselves can estimate in advance how token-intensive a task will be. A project can be started without any reliable sense of what it will cost, or stall halfway when the budget runs out. Closing that forecasting gap, he says, is now a first-order item on the agenda.

The episode's title comes from the career ladder itself. Brynjolfsson tells of a graduating Stanford senior who came to his office and asked whether her generation is doomed, because she and her friends had no jobs. His data says her experience is real: among workers aged 22 to 26 in the most AI-exposed occupations, employment fell about 13 percent when his team with ADP first measured it, and the decline has since deepened to roughly 16 or 17 percent. The traditional pyramid, in which companies hire broadly at the base and promote upward, is becoming a diamond, which saves costs in the short run but raises an obvious question: where will the experienced middle and senior people come from if nobody is hired at the entry level? His answers are public investment in education and training, enlightened companies leaning in, and AI itself as a personalized teacher.

His proposed reframing is the line he leaves with his own students: when you hear AI, do not think artificial intelligence, think amplifying intention. The technology multiplies whatever intention a person brings to it, so the premium shifts to having intentions worth amplifying: directing projects, coming up with new ideas, and evaluating what fleets of agents produce. He describes the whole transition as a design problem that has to be worked at three levels at once, society, the company, and the individual workflow, and reaches for the electricity analogy: the gains came not from putting electric lights in the factory but from electrifying the entire factory. Chasing efficiency alone, he says bluntly, is a little lazy. The riskier strategy is standing still, because no company, country, or person has ever succeeded by focusing on the same thing over time.

Small businesses get a hopeful note. AI gives them access to capabilities that once required selling out, as when a physician's practice had to join a private equity platform to get its back office run. The one-person unicorn is, in his telling, not much of an exaggeration. Inside large companies, the open question is whether to build separate AI-native units, the engine two alongside engine one, or to invest behind the innovators already spread across the existing business, and he says the jury is still out.

The conversation closes on knowledge itself. In his call center research, the least skilled workers got the biggest boost from AI assistance and began performing almost as well as the most skilled. Some executives conclude they can hire fewer top performers, which he calls short-sighted: the knowledge the system spreads originated with those experts, whose answers now get replicated across the whole organization, making them more valuable, not less. The same logic scales to the wider information ecosystem, where AI generates more and more content and the incentives for human creators need rebalancing. On leadership, he concedes few companies are doing this well, citing Nasdaq as an exception, and argues this is a moment that rewards mavericks and CEOs willing to jolt their own systems. His policy ask is dynamism: the United States has fewer startups than it used to, and more entrepreneurship would let the technology do more good.

Open questions

  1. If hiring at the base of the pyramid keeps shrinking, with early-career employment in the most exposed occupations already down 16 or 17 percent, where do the experienced senior people of the next decade come from, and who pays to train them?
  2. Brynjolfsson's case rests on augmentation beating substitution, but he also says agent token costs cannot yet be estimated in advance. Does the augmentation strategy survive contact with CFOs if the cost of running fleets of agents keeps arriving as a surprise?
  3. He wants AI understood as amplifying intention, and concedes headcount is far easier to measure than new value created. What would a credible output-side metric for amplified intention actually look like inside a company?

Tal Keinan on making private jets more affordable

How Private Jets Are About to Become More Affordable

On Joe Lonsdale, the American Optimists episode "How Private Jets Are About to Become More Affordable" is a conversation with Tal Keinan, the founder, chairman and chief executive of Sky Harbour Group, the public company that builds and operates campuses of private aircraft hangars. Lonsdale frames the episode around a question he keeps returning to as an investor: which parts of the economy will the AI industrial revolution enrich without itself being AI? His answer here is private aviation, where a coming wave of AI-created wealth is about to collide with a fixed supply of airports and a chronic shortage of hangars.

Keinan's path to that business runs through two other careers. Born and raised in Miami, he moved to Israel after college, was drafted in 1990 just after the first Gulf War, and spent eighteen years as an F-16 pilot in the Israeli Air Force, eight full-time and ten in the reserves. In 2004 he founded Clarity Capital, which was born from a meeting with finance minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who was quietly enacting a Chilean-style pension reform that freed hundreds of billions of dollars of pension savings from earmarked government bonds. The reform released hundreds of millions of dollars a month into risk investments, and Clarity rode that flow; Keinan became a central figure in Israel's economic rise and later wrote the book God Is in the Crowd, about the three communities he belongs to, American, Jewish and Israeli, and his belief that humanity divides along a spectrum between creators and destroyers.

The bridge to aviation was partly intellectual. At Clarity he had watched a quantitative trading business evolve until its models were so sophisticated that even their authors could not predict them, and it was clear to him that machine intelligence would change the whole economy. He saw three ways to respond: become an AI person himself, which he says was not on the menu, sell supplies to the gold rush, or find a part of the economy that technology would be slow to disrupt. Real estate, protected by rule of law and title, looked like that place. Then came the personal pain point: he bought an airplane in New York in 2015 and discovered there was nowhere to park it. That turned out to be true in most major American metro areas, and had been for decades.

The history explains why. The United States has the densest airport infrastructure in the world because the 1930s and 1940s built it: first New Deal employment programs, then the Second World War, when the thousands of airfields became pilot-training capacity and runways were the real bottleneck in producing air power. In 1946 the federal government essentially divested the system to local governments, where it has stayed. The result is a frozen inventory: where you need an airport there is no land, and where there is land for an airport there is no need. Keinan puts the count at 489 commercial airports and roughly 3,000 federally funded general aviation airports, and he is blunt that the inventory is not going to grow.

Hangars are where the freeze bites hardest. Commercial airports can pay their way through landing slots, parking and retail, but general aviation airports such as Teterboro and Van Nuys cannot be profitable on their own traffic, so federal funding keeps them alive. That subsidy covers runways, repaving and safety infrastructure, not hangars. Airport owners want hangars because business jets need them and aircraft bring tax revenue, but local governments rarely have the money, and building what voters perceive as homes for wealthy people is politically thankless. For forty years the default answer was to let the fixed-base operators, the FBOs, build hangars instead.

Keinan argues the FBOs were never going to solve it, because their incentives point the other way. Signature and Atlantic, a duopoly he puts at 80 to 85 percent of the market, are fundamentally in the fuel business. Since COVID, private flying has surged: Keinan says 95 percent of people who could afford to fly private were not doing so before the pandemic, and most newcomers enter through charter and jet cards such as NetJets, Flexjet and VistaJet. A jet-card aircraft takes the same thousand gallons of fuel as a based aircraft and is gone in forty-five minutes instead of sitting for four days, so FBOs orient everything toward transients. Based aircraft, the Part 91 owners who actually need hangars, are an opportunity cost, and since fueling indoors is prohibited by regulation, every hangar is ramp space the FBO cannot monetize. The shortage is structural, not a failure of effort.

Sky Harbour's answer is a category Keinan says it pioneered, the home-based operator: hangar campuses reserved entirely for home-based aircraft, with no transient business at all. Freed from serving fuel-and-go traffic, the company layers on services the FBOs cannot match: no fueling delays, no hangar rash, the fender-benders that happen moving aircraft indoors, and security protocols built for high-profile residents, including boarding and deplaning entirely inside the hangar, driving the car straight in, with about three minutes from the door to departure. The campuses are financed elegantly: roughly 90 percent of revenue is rent on multi-year leases, and the company issues tax-exempt private activity bonds, $168 million at 4.18 percent fixed for over thirty years in its first issuance and $300 million at 4.75 percent in a second. The land itself is leased from airports on fifty-year ground leases, since airport land cannot be owned, and Sky Harbour owns the improvements above it. Chevron was the first tenant and now occupies the entire Houston campus, the company's first; newer campuses are underway or opening at Chicago Executive, Dulles International, Bradley in Connecticut, four sites around Dallas and Fort Worth, and, they hope, Austin.

The episode's most quoted moment is Keinan's confession about AI. At a company town hall at the end of 2024, asked how Sky Harbour would use AI, he answered that they were a dumb real estate company and he saw no use for it. "I'm wrong about a lot of things," he says. "I'm rarely this wrong." Today, he says, very few major functions of the company are not infused with AI, and the clearest example is site acquisition and leasing: before committing to an airport, the team profiles prospective residents by tail number. For its Camarillo, California campus, an algorithm identified aircraft owners living in Calabasas but based at Van Nuys who depart in the mornings and return in the evenings, people who lose over an hour in traffic to reach Van Nuys when Camarillo is a twenty-eight minute drive with shorter taxi and departure routes besides. The campus filled to 100 percent occupancy with exactly that profile of resident.

The demand side of the story is where host and guest agree most strongly. Lonsdale holds that by 2050 far more people will be able to afford private airplanes, that aircraft will be faster and more efficient, with supersonics and air taxis spreading, and Keinan adds the mechanism: AI scales productivity, which creates many more wealthy individuals who can own things, starting with airplanes. Neither man pretends the capacity problem solves itself in the near term, which is precisely Sky Harbour's opportunity, and the conversation closes on Keinan's broader optimism: America, for all its challenges, is the most successful self-correcting political experiment in history, it always finds its way back to freedom and human flourishing, and AI is an accelerator of that rather than a threat to it.

Open questions

  1. If AI-driven wealth really does push private flying within reach of today's middle class by 2050, where does the capacity come from, given a frozen airport inventory, no land near the cities that need it, and hangar construction that depends on local politics and tax-exempt bond financing?
  2. Sky Harbour's economics rest on cheap tax-exempt debt and fifty-year ground leases on public land. How durable is that advantage if rates rise, the tax provision is revisited, or airports demand much more when leases come up for renewal?
  3. Keinan's AI lease-up machine works by profiling individual aircraft owners by tail number and travel pattern. Where is the line between that targeting and the privacy and security exposure his own high-profile residents are paying Sky Harbour to escape?

Samir Kaul on why chip supply shortages will not last

Why Chip Supply Shortages Won't Last | Samir Kaul

On MTS, the episode "Why Chip Supply Shortages Won't Last | Samir Kaul" is a conversation with Samir Kaul, a founding partner and managing director at Khosla Ventures. His headline argument is that the semiconductor shortage underpinning today's chip valuations will not hold: within five years, he says, supply will catch up, and the real constraint on growth will be energy, and the minerals and materials upstream of it.

Kaul came to venture capital from science. He led the largest plant genome sequencing project of its era and published in Nature and Science before becoming an entrepreneur and partner at Flagship Ventures. Vinod Khosla recruited him after investing in three companies Kaul had started, and in 2005 Kaul joined as a founding partner of the new firm. They began with 100 million dollars of Khosla's own money, working out of the back office at Kleiner Perkins, and the firm now manages north of 30 billion dollars.

The conversation opens in the present tense, on prediction markets during an election. Kaul explains the bookmaker logic behind a market like Kalshi: the operator wants balanced money on both sides, adjusts odds and payouts as bets arrive, and collects fees on the flow. The open question is whose bets these are. If the user base leans one way politically, the odds lean with it, so the real test comes afterward, when someone compares the betting market's record against a no-money forecaster such as Nate Silver's 538.

His years in biotech left him skeptical of early stage therapeutics as a venture bet. Reviewing a decade of biotech IPOs from his Flagship days, he found companies taking about seven years from Series A to IPO, raising 100 to 150 million dollars, and trading at roughly 250 million, which is not even a twofold return on paid-in capital by the time of the offering. The money, he argues, is made later, when a public investor can wait for human data with full liquidity and far more information. He notes that the big early stage firms now run billion dollar Series A rounds to give companies multiple shots on goal, which pushes the required outcomes into the tens of billions.

Where AI does compress the drug cycle, in his account, is less in finding new targets than in everything around the trial. Picking better patients, enrolling them faster, and raising the probability that a drug is tested on people it can actually help is where the time and money sit. He calls animal testing a poor source of information, and points to the FDA considering organoids, lab-grown human tissue, as a replacement: more relevant, quicker, and easier to work with. AI helps most, he says, with finding and enrolling patients.

The broader case he makes for AI is democratization. A patient can put a blood report into a chatbot and get a serious analysis that remembers the last one, while a doctor gets ten minutes per patient. A student can hire an AI tutor for a dollar an hour, available around the clock, that adapts to how that student learns. His frame for the risk is historical: like fire, biotechnology, and the internet before it, AI will be mostly good with some real harm attached, excepting social media, the one technology he holds his own community responsible for and calls a net negative with three teenagers at home.

He does see a scientific renaissance under way. Khosla co-led the investment in Discovery Loop, the new company from Jeff Dean and three longtime Google colleagues, built around running the scientific method as a loop: design experiments, get results in real time, and design thousands of follow-ons in parallel. His benchmark is his own field. Sequencing the human genome once took seven to ten years and more than a billion dollars; now it can be done by lunch for about a hundred dollars, and that was before AI entered the picture. His company Ultima Genomics, founded with Gilad Almogy, pushes sequencing cost, speed, and fidelity further still, and he points to Guardant Health, where he sat on the board, detecting tumor DNA shed into the blood so cancer can be tracked with a monthly blood draw instead of scans and biopsies.

That leads to his most distinctive claim: in biology, the bottleneck is data, not compute. While data center buildout runs at roughly three percent of GDP, what the models lack is measurements of people. If hundreds of millions of Americans were sequenced every quarter at consumer prices, the resulting data would train models that predict drug responses, disease risk, and behavior far better than anything available now, and cheaper, faster sequencing machines are what make that plausible.

His four tests for an investment are a big existing market, the entrepreneur above all, a differentiated approach, and the financial terms. The bar has moved, though. Companies now reach ten million dollars of revenue in three weeks, so the question he asks is whether a company can reach a billion in revenue, stay on a venture growth curve, and keep the differentiation and margins to defend it. That is exactly where his semiconductor worry bites. Nvidia generates ten to twelve billion dollars a month in cash flow at seventy-five percent gross margins, unheard of in chips, and the market values these companies like software on the assumption that scarcity persists. His counter-evidence is Google: it has TPUs, and it is selling them to its arch rivals, with Amazon and OpenAI announced as buyers. A company facing a genuine shortage would hoard supply, not sell it to competitors, so anyone pricing in five more years of shortage, he says, is making a bananas bet.

The undervalued side of the ledger, for him, is energy and what feeds it. Energy is his master indicator for inflation: with oil at ninety dollars, holding inflation at the two percent goal associated with Fed chair Kevin Warsh will be hard. He is also excited about the minerals and materials behind magnets, batteries, and fusion. His closing predictions follow from the same thread. Within a year, he expects people to stop treating chips as the constraint on growth and to see it as energy, and further upstream from energy. Ten years out, against the job-loss gloom, he predicts people will be happier and more productive, pursuing their passions.

Open questions

  1. If the chip shortage breaks within five years as Kaul predicts, which of today's scarcity-priced semiconductor valuations unwind first, and who is holding that risk when they do?
  2. His biology thesis needs hundreds of millions of people sequenced every quarter, at consumer prices, with the data flowing into models. What has to change about consent, cost, and routine medical practice before that flywheel can actually start?
  3. The democratization case rests on AI expertise reaching people through health and education first. Which institutions, the FDA, insurers, schools, licensing bodies, have to move, and in what order, for that diffusion to happen at the speed he expects?

Vinod Khosla on AI regulation, jobs, and quantum computing

Vinod Khosla on AI Regulation, Job Displacement, and the Future of Quantum Computing | WSJ

On WSJ Events, the session "Vinod Khosla on AI Regulation, Job Displacement, and the Future of Quantum Computing" is a stage conversation from WSJ Tech Live between a Journal moderator and Vinod Khosla, the founding partner of Khosla Ventures. It was recorded on election night, and it ranges from AI policy and jobs to the AI bubble question, a new quantum bet, cybersecurity, and a record-setting football purchase.

The conversation opens on politics. Khosla, who describes himself as a registered independent, says a Democratic majority would constrain President Trump, and that the loss of trust in the United States is the largest and longest lasting damage of the current administration. On China, he sees welcome bipartisan consensus, and he praises the administration's lighter touch AI policy as forward looking and serious about winning the AI race.

His sharpest policy claim concerns open source models. Open source, he argues, is widely misunderstood: it is more expensive to run than closed models, not cheaper, and it is far more dangerous, because open weights can run anywhere with no provider able to see or stop misuse. He has recommended to David Sacks and the White House that open source be confined to certified clouds, and he floats a treaty with China limiting what open models may do, on the grounds that neither country wants individuals able to design bioweapons at home. Closed providers, he notes, at least know what their models are being used for.

On jobs, Khosla repeats his view that AI will be capable of doing 80 percent of the tasks in 80 percent of jobs well before 2030, while stressing that capability and deployment are different things, since adoption will take far longer. The public fear around AI, he argues, perversely advantages startups: an incumbent that cannot lay off employees competes against young companies that never had to hire them, and he cites a complex regulated lending business with a 600 million dollar run rate and just two people in accounting. By 2035, he expects the steady extinction rate of the Fortune 500 to accelerate dramatically, with activity renewed in newer companies rather than older ones.

His answer to displacement is to protect people rather than jobs. Because AI advantages capital over labor, he proposes ending the preferential treatment of capital gains and taxing investment gains as ordinary income, which he estimates would raise roughly 400 billion dollars a year. That pool, he argues, could fund protections such as three years of unemployment insurance for displaced workers, on a deficit neutral basis. He frames it as electoral arithmetic as much as policy: some 75 million Americans earn less than 75,000 dollars a year, and a 2028 candidate could build a winning coalition by promising them the rich will no longer be taxed at a lower rate.

Asked whether the AI trade is a bubble, Khosla splits the question in two. Bubbles, he says, live in perceptions of valuation, not in usage. During the dot-com bubble, stock prices showed the mania while internet traffic simply kept rising, and the same is true now: the number of AI inference calls is the reality that matters, and he is confident it will keep rising steadily. Investors bouncing between fear and greed is, in his words, mostly unimportant on a 2035 horizon. He adds that most people are using perhaps ten percent of what today's AI can do, so learning to use it fully will take another decade.

Khosla was one of the first venture investors in OpenAI, and he explains why he has passed on Anthropic: the insight that flows from being close to OpenAI is worth more than the extra profit. That insight, he says, is what justified a two billion dollar investment in the coding startup Cognition at a high valuation, a bet that only made sense if such a company could be worth 100 billion dollars, and which has, by his account, passed a billion dollars of revenue in under two years.

Quantum computing is where he has changed his mind. His firm reviewed essentially every quantum startup for a decade and never placed a bet, because none had a credible path to what he calls practical quantum computing: fault tolerant, cost effective at something like a million dollars rather than hundreds of millions, and able to run Shor's algorithm, his key test of a real quantum computer. Earlier this year, he says, that changed, and the firm has invested twice in the same company. He is clear eyed about narrow quantum markets such as drug and battery design, worth billions or tens of billions, and argues the real prize is a practical, likely room temperature machine that can also run AI.

The same milestone drives his darkest prediction. He expects quantum computers to run Shor's algorithm well before 2030, at which point most cryptography falls, including data that China has been collecting for years in the hope of decrypting it later. Most post-quantum algorithms, he predicts, will also be broken before 2030, and crypto assets are the most vulnerable of all. Real security, in his view, will not come from harder math but from quantum encoding itself. He sees the security disruption arriving in stages: AI models are already strong at finding bugs, then they will crack the underlying math, then quantum machines arrive.

On cybersecurity more broadly, he is investing across the field, including quantum communications and offensive cyber sold to governments. He is especially interested in formal verification, translating software into languages where correctness can be proved, because whoever formalizes a system first finds all of its bugs, a huge advantage for attackers and defenders alike.

The conversation closes on lighter ground. The moderator notes that Khosla set a record by acquiring the Seattle Seahawks for 9.6 billion dollars in July; he calls the team a passion asset he shares with his son, alongside nearly three decades of 49ers home games, and argues that live events and human performance are scarce goods AI cannot disrupt. Asked about legacy, he says he works 80 hour weeks because it is fun: his firm reviewed close to 2,000 startups this year, and he points to fusion, which he expects to be a solved problem within five to seven years, and to personalized cancer treatment as the kind of work that keeps him going.

Open questions

  1. Khosla proposes confining open source models to certified clouds, ideally under a US-China treaty. Who certifies the clouds, and what happens to the open research ecosystem that produced much of the field's progress if weights can no longer run freely?
  2. His safety net depends on taxing capital gains as ordinary income, a change that needs the same political system he describes as gridlocked. If displacement arrives on his timeline but the tax change does not, what fills the gap?
  3. He expects Shor's algorithm to run well before 2030 and most post-quantum defenses to fail before then too. If both halves of that prediction hold, what should a company that cannot adopt quantum encoding actually do in the next four years?
