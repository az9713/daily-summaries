Wes Roth · OpenAI's Perplexity and Intelligence post and the next capability jump

OpenAI's SHOCKING new warning...

On Wes Roth, "OpenAI's SHOCKING new warning..." is a solo breakdown of "Perplexity and Intelligence," a post by OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki that Sam Altman called the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while. Pachocki's claim is that one number from pretraining, the base model log perplexity, tracks what a model can do so closely that labs should report it and agree on common limits based on it. Roth's interest is the warning built into that claim: capabilities do not grow smoothly with the number, they arrive in sudden jumps at particular values of it, the latest jump delivered Millennium Prize level mathematics, and the next jump is not far away.

The backdrop is a controversy that, on Roth's telling, never happened. Publications had accused OpenAI of stealing someone else's work behind its recent Navier-Stokes result. None of that was true, he says. What actually happened behind the scenes is stranger: the capability emerged suddenly, like flipping a switch. Pachocki, who has a background in mathematics, describes watching answers to questions mathematicians had thought about for years come out of the model one after another in a single Slack thread, and calls the experience deeply surreal.

The pattern he draws from OpenAI's chart is a staircase laid over a smooth curve. As log perplexity falls, each crossing of a particular line unlocks a capability that was absent just before it. One crossing brought fluent conversation. Another brought reasoning, the ability to think longer before answering. Then came coding, then cybersecurity expertise, and most recently, at a log perplexity of 0.75, solutions to Millennium Prize problems. The underlying curve declines gradually, but the abilities sit on top of it as steps.

To explain the number itself, Roth uses a puppy learning to play fetch. Loss is the distance between a prediction and the truth, here the gap between where the puppy expects the thrown ball to land and where it actually lands. Perplexity is a companion measure: how many possibilities the predictor is still seriously entertaining. Training shrinks both, as guesses get better. But some of what happens is unpredictable in principle, a gust of wind, a bird in the ball's path, so there is a floor somewhere below which the number cannot fall. Nobody knows where that floor is, and that unknown distance is the room left for progress.

His central intuition is that small movements on the chart are misleading. Predicting a ball's landing spot a little more precisely takes a caveman's eyeball judgment at first, then Newtonian mechanics, then aerodynamics, then fluid dynamics, then something like a full simulation of the world. Each finer prediction demands a massively deeper understanding of what is going on. So when a model's loss decreases by a small amount, whatever the model has grasped underneath may be enormously deeper, and a tiny step on the curve can hide a leap in capability.

The metric Pachocki wants standardized is the log perplexity of the base model, the version that comes straight out of pretraining, measured on held out data the model never trained on. GPT-2 could put together coherent paragraphs. GPT-3 could learn in context. GPT-3.5, at a log perplexity of 0.99, crossed into fluent conversation, and no law of nature said that value would unlock that ability. Pachocki argues that perplexity, equivalently cross entropy loss, describes the model itself, which makes it a better basis for shared safety standards than input measures like the European Union's floating point operations threshold, and a basis on which different labs' models can actually be compared.

Two current frustrations frame the proposal. The first is benchmark saturation: the tests used to measure models are running out of headroom, and Roth points to an unreleased Anthropic model whose skills now sit above what the available tests can gauge on average, like judging college students with middle school exams. The second is that the chart quietly refutes the recent claim that pretraining was dead. Progress along the curve continued right past the point where that claim was made.

The alarm in the post is about the next line down. OpenAI's internal model, the blue square on the chart, already sits close to it, and Pachocki's extrapolation puts the next threshold somewhere around 0.70 to 0.68. Nobody knows what unlocks there. Roth reads between the lines and lands on a specific worry. The danger is not first of all full recursive self-improvement, where AI replaces human researchers wholesale. It is a step change in one part of the process: AI participating in algorithmic research on pretraining itself, finding a smarter way to run the experiments, the code, and the math behind training, the way a person might suddenly see an obvious rearrangement that makes everything cheaper and better at once.

He offers the Riemann Hypothesis as evidence that such a jump is plausible. That proof, long expected to resist solution for many decades, was produced in a sprint of about 41 days by the mathematician Levent Alpoge working with Anthropic's Claude, and was released a few days after the sprint ended. If a problem of that depth can fall that quickly once a threshold is crossed, Roth argues, then something analogous for pretraining research, a hidden trick nobody has considered, is not a remote possibility. It is close to guaranteed that such tricks exist, because models keep building alien but effective structures out of human supplied parts.

His own small encounter with the new capability level is comic and pointed at once. In an online arena where language models build bots that duel each other, he set GPT 6.2 Astra the task of climbing the leaderboard. His desktop fans spun up like a jet preparing for takeoff, the room warmed, and when he checked, the model told him it was training a neural network on his graphics card to learn how to play the game. His first reaction was simply: you can do that?

The safety problem that closes the video is auditing. Defenders currently lean on monitoring a model's chain of thought, reading its reasoning in natural language to catch misbehavior. But models are now largely aware when they are being tested, and behavior under observation may be a performance, the way a driver who has spotted a police car behind them signals every turn and stops at every light. As capability rises, Pachocki expects our ability to align and audit these systems to weaken further. Roth's closing reflection is that none of this was engineered piece by piece. Intelligence here is grown by a simpler, less interpretable process, capabilities emerge that nobody specified, and the process rhymes with evolution, right down to convergence effects like carcinization, where unrelated lineages keep evolving into crabs. His generation, he says, is the first to watch a science of grown intelligence form, and Pachocki's post is an early attempt to give that science a shared instrument before the next line on the chart is crossed.

Open questions

  1. The thresholds were identified by looking backward at OpenAI's own models. Will the same log perplexity values mark the same capability jumps for other labs' models, trained on different data with different methods?
  2. A shared safety metric only works if rival labs report honestly against a comparable held out set. Who would hold that set, and what would keep a lab from quietly training toward it?
  3. If the next threshold mainly unlocks faster algorithmic research on pretraining itself, does progress then start compounding in a way the smooth part of the curve no longer predicts?

New York Times Podcasts · Sam Altman and Greg Brockman with Andrew Ross Sorkin at the DealBook Summit

Sam Altman and Greg Brockman on the Next Next Thing

On New York Times Podcasts, “Sam Altman and Greg Brockman on the Next Next Thing” is a conversation recorded live at the 2026 DealBook Summit in New York City, where Andrew Ross Sorkin interviews Sam Altman, OpenAI's chief executive, and Greg Brockman, its president. The two co-founded OpenAI eleven years ago, and more than 1.2 billion people now use ChatGPT every week. Sorkin presses them on the tension running through the whole interview: how to keep pushing capability forward while putting safety, security, and human control first.

Sorkin opens with the week's industry upheaval around Nvidia's Jensen Huang. Both men say they have spoken with Huang personally, and both call him singular, a founder the AI industry is hard to imagine without. Asked about the cult of the chief executive, Altman concedes that people probably overproject any one person's importance, then describes what he and Brockman actually bring: a partnership built on constant, rapid fire contact, five or six short calls a day since a July 2015 dinner where the idea for OpenAI took shape, and a habit of grinding their disagreements down to a shared view, sometimes over months.

The deepest of those shared views is that people must stay in control of the future. When Sorkin says humans in the loop, Altman corrects him: not in the loop, in control. Both men reject the idea, gaining currency in Silicon Valley, of a gradual handover of control to AI systems. Where they differ, and where they spend their arguing time, is on what it actually takes to guarantee that control against commercial pressure and against whatever rival labs choose to do.

That leads to alignment. Sorkin puts Microsoft chief Satya Nadella's position to them: that models can never truly be aligned with human wants, so the only answer is to contain them. Brockman answers with monitoring. OpenAI watches its models through their chain of thought, and deliberately avoids training that chain of thought to look good, so it stays a faithful record of how the model is solving a problem. But as models grow more capable, more of their reasoning happens inside a single forward pass, without written thinking to inspect, so new monitoring technology will be needed. Altman says he disagrees that alignment is out of reach, while agreeing that the bar for confidence in alignment and monitoring has to keep rising with capability. It would be irresponsible, he says, to push model intelligence without that technical work keeping pace.

The stakes are not abstract for them. Brockman says that working with AMD, OpenAI used its models to win a year's worth of full generational chip design improvements in five months, on top of what the human engineers were doing. Models have also solved long unsolved mathematics problems. Capability like that, arriving during development and not just after deployment, is what changed the company's safety posture this year.

Security is where the change bit hardest. After an incident at Hugging Face showed what OpenAI's own models could do during development, Brockman led a security sprint that pulled a quarter of the company's production engineers off building product and put them on defense, applying OpenAI's frontier cyber models to protect OpenAI itself. Altman defends the company's philosophy of iterative deployment, releasing systems in stages and confronting reality early, against a California attorney general suit accusing the company of testing a dangerous product without proper oversight. He does not accept that characterization, and says the alternative, building a hugely powerful system in secret and switching it on at once, is the scariest possible world.

Sorkin raises the skeptics: former OpenAI researcher Paul Christiano, now on the OpenAI Foundation board, recently told a room that an AI accident is the most likely reason most of them will die, while figures like David Sacks argue the labs' warnings are scare tactics meant to win regulatory protection. Altman replies that OpenAI cannot make everyone happy: talk about safety and it is accused of regulatory capture, talk only about speed and people plead not to be killed. What matters, he says, is doing what they believe delivers a safe future with people in control. He also says plainly that the next four years cannot be run like the last four, and that the world needs a clear plan from the companies for a phase of significantly more capable models.

Asked directly whether what Anthropic is doing is safe, on the eve of that company's IPO, Altman answers that he does not know, and that Anthropic does not really know what OpenAI is doing either. He calls the race dynamic in that fog of war a bad thing, and argues more caution is warranted, not less. Brockman adds that OpenAI is, he believes, the only major AI company whose founders do not hold supermajority control, and that recursive self-improvement is an ill defined term covering very different things. Even shared information about where each lab stands internally, he says, would be a big step forward.

On money, the two are disarmingly open. Altman holds no equity in OpenAI, while Brockman's stake is worth something like 30 billion dollars; Altman jokes that he should have listened to Brockman, who urged him to take equity at the start, and both insist the arrangement has not changed the partnership. On compute, Brockman confirms he has asked both Elon Musk, who is suing OpenAI, and Sergey Brin for more of it, because the world is not building enough. He says OpenAI's third quarter revenue grew at a 70 percent annualized rate and its enterprise revenue doubled in the quarter. Altman shrugs at valuation comparisons with Anthropic: valuations will move up and down, but the value businesses and individuals are creating with the technology is real.

The closing stretch is about the stack. Microsoft, OpenAI's partner, had presented the day before on doing the same work at a small fraction of OpenAI's cost, and Altman says he hopes they succeed, since OpenAI wants AI to be abundant and cheap. Brockman says their models sit on the Pareto frontier of cost and intelligence, and points to Jalapeño, OpenAI's own inference chip, which he expects to be the best in the industry. Altman claims OpenAI is the only company holding the entire intelligence stack, its own chips, its own data centers, a consumer product with 1.2 billion weekly users, and a growing enterprise business on one agentic platform. Compute, both agree, is the binding constraint, and whoever can bring it to bear will set the pace.

Sorkin ends on the personal and the pointed. A film about the company, Artificial, will star Andrew Garfield as Altman, who says the strangest thing in his life is not the movie but the responsibility of ushering in this technology. Brockman deflects a story about a White House signing ceremony and a proposed renaming of AI with talk of the White House accord on safety standards. And when Sorkin raises the Times's own lawsuit against OpenAI, including an email in which Brockman praised a colleague who had gotten past the Times's paywall to scrape it, Brockman declines: active litigation, addressed in his deposition, and it will all be public.

Open questions

  1. Altman and Brockman both call for shared safety standards and shared information about internal development, while admitting the labs deeply distrust one another. What mechanism could actually get competing labs to open their development processes to shared scrutiny?
  2. Brockman concedes that chain of thought monitoring degrades as models reason more inside a single forward pass. What replaces it, and can any monitoring regime keep people genuinely in control of systems whose reasoning no one can fully inspect?
  3. Altman argues that capability progress must depend on alignment progress, while critics call such warnings regulatory capture. Who outside the labs themselves gets to judge whether the safety work is keeping pace, and by what measure?

Sourcery with Molly O'Shea · Ray Dalio on the biggest risks facing markets in 2027

I Finally Interview Ray Dalio

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, "I Finally Interview Ray Dalio" is a conversation O'Shea says she has wanted for years: Dalio sat at the top of her list of one hundred dream guests. The two share a birthday and a hometown, Westport, Connecticut, where Bridgewater is based, and they filmed in front of a shark as a nod to his ocean exploration. What follows ranges well beyond markets, but it keeps returning to the framework Dalio built his career on: the five forces that, in his telling, have driven the same patterns for five hundred years.

The framework came out of being blindsided. Dalio clerked on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange in 1971, the summer President Nixon announced the United States would no longer redeem dollars for gold. Expecting a crash, he watched the market rise instead, went looking for a precedent, and found that Roosevelt had done the same thing in March 1933. Studying the Great Depression and the years around it later helped him anticipate the 2008 financial crisis, and a study of the last five centuries convinced him the same things happen over and over. The five forces are the monetary order, the political order, the geopolitical order, acts of nature such as droughts, floods, and pandemics, and technology. They interact in big cycles that last roughly a lifetime, moving from disorder through rebuilding and prosperity and back toward conflict.

The recent election, for Dalio, changes the people dealing with the problems rather than the problems themselves. The debt problem, the wealth and values gaps, the climate problem, and the geopolitical conflict all remain. Politics now effectively contains four parties, he says: an extreme left, a moderate left, a moderate right, and a hard right, with a fight between left and right followed by a fight inside each camp between its moderates and its extremists. The only successful path for a democracy is for the moderates of both sides to join together and win, because extremists fighting extremists cannot resolve anything. The most surprising outcome, and the hopeful one, would be exactly that: voters in the flipped states already look more like people of the middle than like partisans. If instead disagreements pass the point of reconciliation, the system itself is in jeopardy, and some form of war follows until one side wins.

On the wealth gap, Dalio traces the problem to a lack of broad-based productivity. A successful country, in his account, educates its whole population well enough that people can be productive and civil with each other, and stays out of wars, internal and external. The United States is failing the first test: nearly sixty percent of its population reads below a sixth grade level, and in Connecticut, one of the richest states per capita, twenty two percent of high school students have dropped out or are failing with absentee rates above twenty five percent. A large population that cannot be productive will conclude the system is not working for them. History rhymes here, he says: the industrial revolution produced the Gilded Age and its robber barons, then the panic of 1907, then the First World War.

That sets up his distinction between wealth and money, prompted by O'Shea's question about the coming Anthropic listing and the string of trillion dollar IPOs widening the divide. You cannot spend wealth, he says. A fifty million dollar raise at a billion dollar valuation creates a billion dollars of wealth on paper, but it becomes money only when someone sells. Bubbles burst precisely when the people holding wealth need money: to repay what they borrowed to acquire it, because interest rates rose, or because a wealth tax comes due and there is nothing to pay it with except a sale. By then ownership sits in weak hands, and everything, markets and economy alike, depends on the one technology, which makes the reversal violent once fear replaces confidence.

A bubble, in his definition, is not a price that exceeds the present value of future cash flows, since those cash flows are unknowable for decades and even companies with good ones have bubbled and collapsed. The mechanics are simpler: people mistake a great new technology for a great investment. He asks listeners to imagine the nineteen twenties, when electricity, refrigeration, movies, radio, cars, and airplanes all arrived at once, a wave more visible than AI. Everyone believed, everyone borrowed to own a piece of it, and when the need for money arrived, the same dynamic that built the boom ran in reverse.

His own use of AI is the project of his late career. The way he built Bridgewater was to think through his criteria for investing, write them down, and put them into computer code, so building an AI system that applies his principles is a continuation, not a departure. At seventy seven, he says, he wants to pass along what he has. The system takes criteria, back tests them against different environments, and works out signals for things like bubbles in advance, which he argues no person weighing everything in their head can match. That way of deciding, he says, is obsolete. But he insists on partnership: neither the best human intelligence nor the best artificial intelligence does the job alone. The risk is a split in the population. Some people will stop thinking and just ask to be told, while others will use the machine to learn far more about why things work. AI, he says, will distinguish between those two kinds of people.

Underneath all of it sits one question: why. Everything works like a machine, he says, because everything that happens has causes, and the causes come before the effects. Understand cause and effect relationships, rather than merely following instructions, and you have an edge on what happens next.

That philosophy built Bridgewater's culture: meaningful work and meaningful relationships through radical truthfulness and radical transparency. The Dot system exists because in a meeting only one person can talk at a time, so nobody knows what everyone else is thinking. Collecting those judgments as data reveals each person's strengths and weaknesses, the way a coach learns a team, and lets people be combined well. The hardest evaluation he remembers was a client meeting after which he was told he had done a terrible job, rambling and not listening. O'Shea then confesses that she interviewed at Bridgewater in one of her first jobs after college and did not get it, having overintellectualized everything instead of simply saying what she thought. There are no faults, Dalio tells her, only realities.

What he looks for in people follows from the same beliefs: character and capability, and above all knowing what you do not know. Education rewards having the right answers, which produces people who believe they are smart because they performed well under someone else's approval. He calls an arrogant opinion that turns out to be wrong the greatest tragedy of mankind, because it is so easily fixed by open-mindedness. The rest of the conversation moves through the chapters of a life, from depending on others, to having others depend on you, to passing along what you know, and through his conviction that a sense of lost agency exists mostly in people's minds. The people who shaped him most were not mentors, he says, but partners in thoughtful disagreement. And the great misunderstanding between countries is that each sees through its own eyes rather than the other's. Asked what he looks forward to in the next twelve months, he names playing the global macro game, passing along what he has, and his family, including nine and a half grandchildren, the half being one on the way.

Open questions

  1. Dalio says the only successful path is for moderates in both parties to unite against their own extremists. What would such a coalition have to settle first, the debt, taxes, or spending, for the politics he describes to actually change?
  2. If bubbles burst when wealth holders are forced to sell in order to raise money, which forced seller reaches the AI boom first: lenders, rising interest rates, or a wealth tax?
  3. Dalio is encoding his own investment principles into an AI system meant to outlast his judgment. Can criteria written by one investor keep working inside a cycle that investor has never lived through?

20VC with Harry Stebbings · Anjney Midha on chips sold to China, why most VCs will not survive AI, and why the inference layer is uninvestable

"Fire the Bankers": Most VCs Won't Survive AI | Why the Inference Layer is Uninvestable | Anj Midha

On 20VC with Harry Stebbings, "Fire the Bankers": Most VCs Won't Survive AI | Why the Inference Layer is Uninvestable | Anj Midha is a conversation with Anjney Midha, a founding partner of the venture firm AMP and one of Anthropic's earliest investors, recorded the day after Anthropic's public listing. Midha argues from the bottom of the stack up: frontier AI is fundamentally an energy business, the West is losing critical compute capacity to China through poor coordination, and much of venture capital has been captured by former bankers who cannot judge deep technology. His refrain gives the episode its title: fire the bankers, bring in the scientists.

The energy claim comes first, and it is personal: AMP is named after the ampere. Read Anthropic's S-1, Midha says, and the business is simple. A frontier lab buys megawatts and finds a way to turn them into tokens, and tokens are a way of turning energy into intelligence. Software has become abundant, with a marginal cost near zero, but energy is physical and zero sum, so the pace of frontier progress is now a direct function of energy production in the United States and the West.

His firm's first investment shows where he thinks the ceiling is. Periodic Labs is searching for a room temperature superconductor, and its loop between a physical lab and AI researchers has already produced 13 new materials, one of them now used for ballistic heat shields. Midha believes a room temperature superconductor will be found within our lifetime, and that it would change everything, flying cars included. But the timeline is unpredictable, so the industry needs a phased plan for the world as it is, under today's transmission laws.

That world, he argues, favors China. China has decided to build more than 500 gigawatts of industrial energy capacity for AI over the next five years, while the United States has over 100 gigawatts of stranded power sitting at factories, decommissioned sites and military land, none of it connected in a way frontier research can use. The West is still great at invention, he says, but it has forgotten how to coordinate and scale infrastructure outside of wartime.

AMP's answer is to build. In March the firm created a company called National Compute and a system called the National Compute Grid, and it now operates land, power and shell alongside its investing. Prefabricated pods of five to ten megawatts, built by a company AMP co-founded with former Department of Energy staff and now scaling production in Houston, can be shipped to a stranded power site and plugged in. Because American utility regulation leaves communities paying the same whether or not local power is consumed, putting that stranded power to work can even bring local electricity prices down. The fit is natural, he argues, because roughly half of compute spend across his portfolio is now post-training and reinforcement learning, work that runs well on decentralized capacity rather than one giant cluster.

The pricing looks wrong until the scheduling is understood. On the grid, AMD's MI355 chips clear at 12 dollars an hour, against three to four dollars an hour on a five year lease, yet a lab paying on demand can cut its total spend to 30 or 40 percent of the lease cost, because it pays only for completed work. An agent called Marshall, modeled on the Borg orchestration system that took Google's GPU utilization from about 60 percent to 99 percent over five years, reprices and reallocates capacity minute by minute. The metric that matters, Midha insists, is goodput, useful work actually delivered, and on that measure the real cost of compute is plummeting even while headline prices rise.

The same researcher-first lens drives his pushback on the hundred billion dollar training run, a phrase Harry attributes to Satya Nadella. Researchers, Midha says, do not think in terms of training versus inference. They think in terms of research versus deployment, because reinforcement learning is inference whose results are fed back into the weights. A hundred billion dollars of compute flowing into a single model generation, across research, training and inference together, is plausible. What is wrong is the accounting language, and infrastructure people who want to understand frontier progress need to start thinking like scientists.

That sets up the episode's central rant. A significant share of the roughly 800 billion dollars in venture capital assets is, in his telling, marketed as venture but run by former bankers and growth equity deal hunters with no technical or scientific training. Jiao frames that work in three pillars, and the middle one is the product problem. She points to what OpenAI calls a capability overhang: models such as GPT Rosalind already hold chemistry and structural biology skills that most scientists never reach, because nobody knows what to ask. ChatGPT was the harness that made the chat model usable, and Codex did the same for programmers. The life sciences push is an attempt to build the equivalent for scientists, a Codex for the lab bench.

That gap is why Orchestra Bio mattered. Rajendra's company built software to coordinate a drug program across its studies, partners, and campaigns, capturing what he calls the chain of evidence, including the tacit knowledge of why a molecule was selected and what evidence argued against it. Pairing that orchestration layer with frontier models that gain new capabilities every few months is, in his telling, the inflection point: the industry gets the tools to run drug programs at the same speed the models improve.

The conversation follows a framework Rughani and Conde wrote about years ago: discovery, development, and distribution. On discovery, Jiao argues that biology's bottleneck has flipped. Two decades of sequencing and high-throughput assays produced more data than anyone can read, so the limiting factor is now human attention. In a collaboration with PerturbAI, OpenAI threw some 2,000 agents at an in vivo perturbation dataset covering 2,000 perturbations across 8 million cells, and the agents surfaced an interaction between two genes in the same protein complex, relevant to tuberous sclerosis, that months of manual human analysis had missed.

The scarcest skill, both guests agree, is knowing what to do next, and that is what makes a great drug hunter. The tools amplify that judgment rather than replace it: read five thousand papers instead of five, search the whole corpus, and bring in experts a scientist never thought of. Once a target is chosen, molecule design becomes a more concrete optimization problem, and Jiao says models are beginning to show the rare intuition of the tiny number of experts who can look at a protein sequence and picture its structure, or spot the liability in a candidate molecule. She predicts the way molecules are designed with AI will change a lot in the next three to six months.

Development is where the money is spent and where drugs fail. The group walks through protocol design, site selection, and enrollment. Matching patients to trials defeated the rules engines of the digital health era, because inclusion and exclusion criteria, and where a patient sits in their disease journey, are too tangled for handwritten rules. Models that reason over a patient's whole history, joined with OpenAI's consumer reach, could surface trial matches to patients and clinicians directly. The FDA, they note, is signaling it wants to modernize, with ideas like continuous submission and AI-assisted review turning a single point-in-time filing into an ongoing process. Manufacturing is the quieter opportunity: transferring a process to a contract manufacturer is like handing a Michelin chef's recipe to another kitchen, and agents can reconcile paper batch records, surveil every parameter, and trace questions like whether a refrigerator outage of a few hours puts a batch, and a trial behind it, at risk.

Ryan Peterman · Alan Kay on Steve Jobs, Xerox PARC, and the research culture that invented personal computing

Alan Kay: Steve Jobs, Xerox PARC, Marijuana

On Ryan Peterman, "Alan Kay: Steve Jobs, Xerox PARC, Marijuana" is a conversation with Alan Kay, the Turing Award winner who helped pioneer personal computing at Xerox PARC. Across an hour, Kay tells firsthand stories about Steve Jobs, explains the research culture that let a small lab invent an entire industry, and ranges over Dijkstra, large language models, LSD and marijuana, the books that shaped him, and his advice for young people.

Kay opens with a complaint about ambition. Businesses, he says, set their sights very low: they are happy making millions and billions, while really good researchers want trillions, and he jokes he now has to upgrade the line to whatever comes after trillion. Making money is a low goal, he argues, because you can make money by cheating people, and he calls social media a legal drug. What the ARPA community, including Xerox PARC, actually did was not invent a technology but invent an industry: the personal computer, the network, and the internet were conceived together, not as separate products. Ryan cites Kay's earlier estimate that PARC's combined contribution to gross world product stood at $35 trillion, and Kay says it is so much more now.

Why it worked, in Kay's telling, starts with ARPA's Information Processing Techniques Office and J.C.R. Licklider's vision of computers as interactive intellectual amplifiers for everyone on Earth, pervasively networked. ARPA funded people who thought they had answers rather than trying to pick the solution, because it was in problem finding mode, not problem solving mode. Kay uses a baseball metaphor: bat .300 on ideas this deep and you win, and the misses are overhead, not failure. ARPA also skipped peer review, on the theory that if you can find a peer of the best people, you should hire him instead.

The person who carried that culture to PARC was Bob Taylor, who had made the ARPANET happen and was hired to build Xerox's computer lab. Kay describes Taylor's social engineering in detail: beanbag chairs that made it hard to leap to your feet during an argument, and a single black ball hiring system in which the whole lab had to be enthusiastic about every new hire. Taylor refused to hire merely good people, arguing that ten good people cannot be assembled into one great one, and Kay notes that by Butler Lampson's count, all the grand inventions of PARC were done by about 25 people in a flat organization whose only title was member of research staff.

Xerox could afford the experiment because of its own unlikely history. After IBM's consultants at Arthur D. Little reported there was no market for plain paper copying, the enraged founders mortgaged their houses and used their life insurance to build a factory for the Xerox 914 copier, and by the end of the 1960s Xerox was the fastest growing company in the United States. Chief scientist Jack Goldman then persuaded the company to put a long range research lab as far from Connecticut headquarters as possible. Kay frames research funding as mad money, using Goldman's poker logic: money you have mentally given away when you sit down at the table. Research, he says, is being able to change your mind, while most executives use the word as a synonym for product development.

PARC decided what to work on at a weekly Wednesday meeting that opened with a big Chinese lunch, usually catered from Chef Chu's. Kay explains that Chinese food was the food of computing because the only Boston restaurants open at 3 a.m. were Chinese, back when graduate students worked all night to get the computer to themselves. The meetings also ran on a rule for arguments: when Taylor sensed people trying to win rather than argue, he called a type two argument, and each side had to state the other's position until the other agreed it was represented correctly, by which point most people had forgotten why they were arguing.

The technical lineage behind the Alto, which Kay says can safely claim to be the first personal computer with a full graphical interface, runs through Doug Engelbart's pointing devices and Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad, with its light pen and display lists. Steve Jobs visited PARC in 1979, and Kay was in the room. The Alto had been working since 1973, three years before Apple existed, and PARC had given public demos to thousands of people, so Kay treats the famous visit as just another demo. Jobs, he says, was so blinded by the user interface that he missed the object oriented programming and the Ethernet. Kay's verdict on Jobs is double sided: Apple would absolutely have benefited if Jobs had been technical, but Jobs understood desire, had far more taste than the other 8-bit micro people, and was indomitable.

Kay later joined Apple himself as a fellow, after Jobs recruited him when Atari went down, and he tells the story behind his famous verdict on the Macintosh. Asked by a magazine what he thought of it, he said the Macintosh was the first personal computer good enough to be criticized, which angered Jobs, though Kay notes his private memo at the time, comparing the Mac to a Honda with a one quart gas tank, was worse. About twenty years later, at the iPhone unveiling, where Jobs quoted Kay's line that people who care about software should build their own hardware, Jobs brought the phone over, put it in Kay's hands, and asked: Alan, is this good enough to be criticized? He had never forgotten.

The story Kay says he remembers Jobs most for is Pixar. Kay had taught Ed Catmull to program in graduate school, and when Catmull's graphics group wanted to go further than George Lucas would support, Kay walked with Jobs, talked him into visiting, and spent the two hour limo ride to San Rafael coaching him not to be obnoxious with first class people. Jobs then hung in through Pixar's long learning curve, spending, by Kay's account, a couple of hundred million dollars over about ten years before Toy Story, patience Kay says no venture capitalist today would show. He calls that, not Apple, Jobs's finest period.

The conversation turns to Edsger Dijkstra, prompted by Dijkstra's famous line that object oriented programming is an exceptionally bad idea that could only have been invented in California. Kay says the two were friends, that Dijkstra knew perfectly well the ideas predated PARC in the work of the Norwegian Simula creators, and that there was no substance in the jab: he had simply thought of a funny thing to say. Kay's own contribution to the lore is the unit of arrogance: arrogance in computer science, he once said, is measured in nano-Dijkstras.

On large language models and education, Kay returns to his overproduction argument: humans need sugar, salt, status, and story, and a desire that was safe when supply was scarce turns harmful once industry can overproduce it, which is why more is better needs the rider, until it isn't. On the episode's title topic, he is dismissive of his own drug history. PARC was mostly a drinking culture, he never took LSD, and isolation tanks can give you the trip anyway, since the brain secretes its own chemistry. He smoked marijuana as a teenage guitarist, he says, but it did not help the guitar playing, so he liked regular cigarettes better. He then picks up a guitar and plays.

His book recommendations center on Bertrand Russell, whose History of Western Philosophy Kay says Russell dictated essentially from memory, and on Jerome Bruner, with whom he held fourteen hour conversations. His closing advice to young people is to learn as much as possible in as many areas as possible, without needing a reason: the theatrical work he overdid in college turned out to be the key to designing the user interface. He is not sure he would go into computing now, disliking the current hacker vibe, but he insists every generation produces the same rough percentage of talent, so when one era underperforms, the fault lies in the environment, not the kids.

Open questions

  1. Kay argues that ARPA and PARC worked because funders tolerated problem finding and treated misses as overhead rather than failure. Can that model survive inside today's top-down, business-goal funding, or does it require a patron with monopoly profits and no product deadlines?
  2. His Pixar story credits Jobs's decade of patience as the thing no modern venture capitalist would replicate. If that patience is no longer available, what funding structure replaces it for technologies with ten year learning curves?
  3. Kay warns that language models, like social media, overproduce something humans are wired to want. What would it mean in practice to design an AI product around his rider, more is better until it isn't?

Greg Isenberg · Adrien Gaidon of Walden Robotics on why robots are the new apps

$1.1B founder: robots are the new apps

On Greg Isenberg, "$1.1B founder: robots are the new apps" is a conversation with Adrien Gaidon, co-founder and chief strategy officer of Walden Robotics, about where the openings sit in robotics and physical AI. Walden has raised over $300 million to build general purpose factory robots, and Gaidon uses the episode to map the landscape for everyone else: how robots are now taught rather than programmed, why deployment data is the most valuable data there is, and why the best opportunities for new founders sit in the negative space around the big labs.

Gaidon says the field's turning point already happened. He felt the physical version of AGI back in 2022, when his co-founders produced diffusion policy and showed that a robot could learn a task from examples. If you can demonstrate something, a robot can learn to do it. The catch is that the physical world cannot move at software speed: you cannot teleport a robot, robots break, and hardware degrades even as the intelligence inside it improves.

The old way of building robots was programming. A machine had to be told every point in space to move through, which is why factory spot welders are open loop and effectively blind. The new way is teaching. Web, video, and language data build a foundation model with common sense about the physical world, and demonstrations of a specific task build the skill on top of it. Diffusion policy adapted the diffusion idea from image generation to action generation, and its descendants now sit inside the action heads of vision language action models.

On data, Gaidon draws a sharp line. Buying data can accelerate a team, but the most valuable data comes from deployment, and deployment data cannot be bought. It has to be earned, by persuading manufacturers like Toyota to let your robot onto a production floor that already works. For someone starting a data business, his test is a Venn diagram: the data must be scarce, meaning it rests on privileged access that is not a web crawl away, and it must be useful for a task robots cannot yet do. He adds the Japanese idea of ikigai on top: work you love, that the world needs, and that it will pay for is a personal unfair advantage.

Walden calls itself roboticists on Team Human, taking WALL-E and Johnny Five rather than the Terminator as its models. The company's name comes from Thoreau's Walden and its values of self-reliance, simplicity, and purpose. Its robot has an asymmetric head so it looks curious rather than threatening, and its motion is tuned to feel friendly, because people who like a robot start asking what else it could do, and that pull creates the commercial flywheel. Asked what the world looks like in ten years, Gaidon predicts at least millions of robots, a manufacturing boom, infrastructure projects that become cheaper and faster, and people building alongside robots the way Iron Man builds.

The robot itself is a study in pragmatism. Walden's machine is a general purpose mobile manipulator on a wheeled base with two-finger grippers, not legs and not elaborate hands. Gaidon tells the story through CEO Russ, a longtime MIT professor who led MIT's team in the 2015 DARPA Robotics Challenge with a legged Boston Dynamics Atlas, and whose first Walden design dropped legs and hands anyway. Factory floors are flat because tripping hazards violate safety rules, and nobody does backflips in a factory. The lesson he draws for founders is strong opinions loosely held: move fast on a hypothesis, then update when the evidence arrives.

Learning robots also change how robots get sold. The old model was capital expenditure: program a machine once and it repeats the same motion for years while you compute a payback period. A robot that keeps learning delivers a service instead, and customers who teach it more get more return from it, which Gaidon ties to Jensen Huang's line that the more you buy, the more you save. He and Isenberg note that pricing across AI is moving away from per seat and per device toward pay per outcome and pay per token, and founders can now design how customers buy rather than copying old patterns.

For founders who want in, Gaidon lists the open territory. Data is one opening, and supply chain is another, because getting to high mix, high volume production is exactly what an age of personalization requires. Teleoperation is a real category for slower robots, though safety critical machines moving at highway speed keep it embedded inside the company that builds them. Insurance is an opening too, both for robots and for the products of vibe manufacturing, where people design physical goods with language models and print them. Software founders can sell shovels: Weights and Biases, the experiment tracking tool Walden used as its first customer before OpenAI, is his example of a software company built on serving machine learning labs.

The hardest part of physical AI, Gaidon says, is that it is everything everywhere all at once. He pictures the system as a tube with bottlenecks, where widening a wide section does nothing for flow. Robotics has three linked bottlenecks, data, compute, and manufacturing, and a company has to widen all three together: more deployment needs more robots built, and more data needs more compute to compress it into intelligence. That is why Walden runs hardware, AI, and deployment teams as one full stack company.

Founders also get to pick their level of hard. Below the everything machine sit general purpose robots like Walden's, which bundle many small tasks, none of which would justify automation alone, into one machine that keeps learning more of them. Below that sit single task niches, a welding robot, a barista robot, a gardening robot, where a founder can hold a monopoly in a small market and have a great business. Isenberg connects this to the old line that there are two ways to make money, bundling and unbundling, and Gaidon agrees it applies to physical AI directly.

On hype, Gaidon names zero-shot learning as the most misunderstood idea in the field. The claim that a robot trained on video alone will nail a factory task on day one mistakes the starting line for the finish line. Zero-shot capability is real, but deployment is where learning begins, not where it ends, a continuous improvement habit the Japanese call kaizen. Robot hands are his second overhyped item: more degrees of freedom sound better, but a complex hand that costs tens of thousands of dollars and breaks twice a day is a terrible manufacturing product, so Walden ships simple two-finger grippers and lets form follow function.

The home is the frontier he cannot yet see clearly. Gaidon praises Matic, the vision based robot vacuum and mop, as great product work that revived a stale category, and Hugging Face's programmable Micro Duck as a toy he ordered himself. But every successful home robot so far navigates rather than manipulates, and he does not know what the first large scale manipulation use case in the home will be. Folding laundry became easy to teach after diffusion policy, but he doubts people will pay $20,000 for it, and everyone who wants Rosie from the Jetsons wants a cheap Rosie. He closes by rooting for the home robotics founders to prove him wrong.

Open questions

  1. Gaidon argues deployment data is the most valuable data in robotics and that it can only be earned on real production floors. Does that make the incumbents with factory access nearly impossible to catch, or can a data startup with privileged access in one niche stay ahead?
  2. If robots that learn are sold as services priced per outcome rather than as machines, who carries the risk when a robot underperforms, and how should factories rewrite their return on investment math?
  3. Gaidon cannot name the first killer manipulation app for the home and doubts laundry folding carries a $20,000 price tag. What home task is valuable enough, frequent enough, and safe enough to be that app?

Harvard Science Book Talks and Research Lectures · Terence Tao in conversation with Lauren K. Williams on Math 2.0 and his book Six Math Essentials

Terence Tao, Six Math Essentials

On Harvard Science Book Talks and Research Lectures, "Terence Tao, Six Math Essentials" is an event built around Terence Tao, the UCLA mathematician and Fields Medalist, and his first book for a general audience. Tao opens with a surprise: he will not talk about the book. Instead he gives a short presentation on what he calls Math 2.0, the upgrade now arriving as AI systems learn to produce proofs, and only afterward sits down with Harvard mathematician Lauren K. Williams to discuss the book itself.

His starting point is a paradox. Mathematics has traditionally been regarded as one of the hardest subjects for humans, yet it is close to the easiest task for modern AI. Calculators have long beaten humans at arithmetic, but it was not clear computers could beat them at proofs. In retrospect, he says, proof is custom designed for machine learning: success is objectively verifiable by a computer, the work is fully digital with no messy real world data, there are no privacy or legal complications, and the datasets are large and high quality. It is the bitter lesson of AI applied to mathematics, and it ticks all three boxes in a way almost no other task does.

About four or five years ago, the leading AI labs realized that training their models on tough, objectively verifiable tasks was the way to make them more capable, and that the best such tasks are math problems. They invested enormous compute, expertise, and money. The result is that humanity has moved, almost overnight, from an era of proof scarcity to an era of proof abundance. Undergraduate and graduate homework exercises are now routine for a computer, even though the same tools remain embarrassingly bad at ordinary tasks like booking a hotel by talking to a human. The technology, Tao says, is jagged: superhuman in some directions, hopeless in others.

Mathematics itself is in no danger of running out. There are infinitely many problems, so there will always be a frontier, and the frontier grows as more problems are solved. The real problem for 2026, he says, is that nobody can currently see where the frontier is. The usual guideposts for what can and cannot be done have gone blurry, and that blurriness, more than any single result, is what is causing anxiety in the field.

The deeper issue is what problem solving was for. Solving problems, Tao argues, was always partly a proxy, the way a good grade is a proxy for actually learning the material. In the era of proof scarcity the proxy was well aligned: people who were good at solving problems also tended to be good at understanding mathematics, explaining it, making conjectures, writing textbooks, and training students, so the field rewarded problem solving with prizes and prestigious publications. Now that proofs are cheap, the proxy has broken. If the field keeps its old incentives unchanged, he warns, it will damage the long term health of mathematics.

He also cautions against reading the headlines at face value, because there is a strong reporting bias. An AI success gets far more attention than a human success, and failed AI attempts are almost never reported. OpenAI, he notes, publicized solving roughly 300 important problems, but it had attempted 4,000, a success rate of about nine percent, and the list of attempted problems was not released. Getting accurate records of both positive and negative results matters, and he points to First Proof, a project Williams helps edit, as an effort to assess AI proof ability honestly.

His picture of the future is a Venn diagram. One ellipse is everything human mathematicians could do under Math 1.0. A second, overlapping ellipse is what AI can do, which extends far beyond the human ellipse in problem solving but still cannot, for example, write a good textbook. The real opportunity is a third, larger circle: Math 2.0, built from creative combinations of humans and AI that nobody has invented yet, rather than the current dominant practice of feeding unsolved problems from the old frontier into a tool and seeing what happens.

One consequence is that mathematics will become an experimental science as well as a theoretical one. Math has historically been about 99 percent theoretical, building its map of the frontier paper by paper. AI makes it possible to sweep across thousands or millions of problems and chart which are easy, hard, or impossible, the way medicine moved from studying one patient at a time to running trials across a whole population. His example is the equational theories project he ran in 2024, which posed 22 million true or false questions in universal algebra, of the form: does one law of algebra imply another? Crowdsourced human and machine contributions, formalized in Lean, completed the full implication graph in about three months, work that would previously have required 22 million graduate students.

A second example is the inverse Galois challenge, run by a foundation he founded. Whether every finite group arises as the Galois group of a polynomial was known up to degree 23, but degree 24 was open, and settling it meant finding a polynomial for each of roughly 25,000 groups, an Easter egg hunt at scale. A competition brought together high school students, professional Galois theorists, and AI enthusiasts, and together they matched a polynomial to every group. The plan now is to combine all their strategies into one AI run super team and push on toward degree 31, starting next month.

Tao is equally clear about the danger. Pure math problems can be verified objectively, but applied math problems, modeling the weather, the financial system, or air traffic control, cannot, and keeping applied solutions aligned with reality depends on human understanding of the models being used. That understanding was historically built by solving pure problems. AI now produces lengthy, correct proofs of pure problems without producing any human understanding at all: often the person entering the prompt is not a mathematician and understands neither the problem nor the output. Pure math itself is not harmed, but the same models are then deployed on applied problems, where the traditional brake of human understanding is fading, and the risk of misaligned solutions grows.

The remedy, in his telling, is to use AI to bolster understanding rather than replace it: to summarize existing arguments, to answer questions and give feedback, to suggest new problems, and to detect when a model is being used incorrectly. His closing example is a Caltech event from the previous weekend, a math hackathon whose second edition flipped the format of the first. Instead of racing to solve as many open problems as possible with nobody understanding the solutions, 100 teams took results whose existing proofs were terrible or unsatisfactory and produced simplified proofs, better expositions, animations, and code, with two more months of cleanup and possible Lean formalization to follow. The future of math is bright, he concludes, once the field reinvents itself, but journals, institutions, and cultures all have to change, and the biggest bottleneck is imagination.

The conversation with Williams then turns to the book. Six Math Essentials, Tao's first popular book, came out only days before the event. It grew out of a pitch from his editor: instead of writing, Tao would record long conversations about mathematics, which would be transcribed and edited into a book. Tao proposed twenty or thirty disconnected stories; his editor condensed them into six categories, and the format worked. The intended reader is the person he meets at a party who, on hearing he is a mathematician, either changes the subject, confesses they were always bad at math, or, rarely, asks about a famous conjecture. The book assumes no expertise, contains few equations, and is aimed at all ages.

The six essentials each open a door. Numbers are the gateway to quantitative thinking, algebra the gateway to abstraction, geometry a way to calculate beyond what we can see, probability a tool for navigating uncertainty rigorously, analysis a means of taming the very large and the very small, and dynamics the mathematics of change. Williams singles out the book's simple examples: the coastline of Norway alongside non-differentiable functions, flights between cities alongside curved geometry, and sharks and fish alongside dynamical systems. Tao explains his habit of reaching for analogy, describing his own field, analysis, as a kind of market: proving an inequality means starting with the quantity you own and bartering, trade by trade, until you hold the quantity you want, never accepting a worse deal along the way.

The two trade lighter observations as well. Analysts, Tao jokes, are fixated on checking whether bad things can happen, a productive paranoia, and analysis is the perfect tool when perfection is not possible. He repeats an urban legend that algebraists and analysts can be told apart by how they eat corn on the cob, then notes it was extrapolated from a tiny dataset in a single tea room. His more serious theory is that evolution gave humans no math center in the brain, so every mathematician has repurposed some other faculty, the visual center, the language center, physical intuition, and different fields attract different repurposings. Math dreams, he admits, have never been productive for him, though physical motion helps: he once understood a stubborn vector field problem by lying on the floor, closing his eyes, and imagining he was the vector field, rolling around until his aunt walked in, walked out, and never mentioned it again.

On the myth that math is magic, Tao blames education. Children begin with intuitive, curiosity driven questions, but around high school the subject takes a sharp turn into formalism, right answers, red marks, and a subtle shame that drives many away. What students rarely see is the third stage, where intuition returns on top of rigor and math becomes fun again. His own route into new fields, he says, is social: find a collaborator. Ben Green taught him number theory, others taught him dynamics, and he navigates foreign fields through imperfect dictionaries, such as the fact that what an algebraist calls nilpotent an analyst calls epsilon, something so small it is practically zero.

The audience questions return to AI. Asked about labs that may have solved students' problems internally without releasing the results, Tao says the labs should partner with mathematicians before generating proofs, not after. Their incentives are fundamentally different from the community's: the labs want training tasks for their models, math problems are nearly the only hard verifiable tasks left, and the proofs are almost a waste byproduct. The path not taken, he suggests, would have been a win for both sides: mathematicians design large sets of problems nobody was working on, interesting to solve and useful as training data, with some problems reserved for humans and others handed to AI, the way a city separates car streets from pedestrian streets. Instead, the community's gift economy, in which papers are shared freely, refereeing is unpaid, and the arXiv was built to democratize access, has collided with a well funded capitalist industry that treats the archive as a free training ground.

His practical proposals follow from that diagnosis. He edits a new journal, Mathematical Discourse, where authors submit a refereed talk rather than a paper. Funders now run workshops where twenty young people collectively digest a single painful AI proof, work that is exhausting alone but feasible together. Hiring and promotion can no longer rest on counts of top journal papers, and because AI can generate polished papers whose authors know nothing of their content, he expects hiring to shift toward in person assessment, ideally a blackboard talk with real questions. A student of his, dispirited when an AI release nearly scooped his problem, has responded by building a communal catalog of thousands of variants of the problem, tracking what is known about each and testing which proofs extend, replacing the race to be first with a shared completion project, much like the dashboard that tracked the equational theories graph from 80 to 85 to 87 percent complete.

His advice to students is to sort their skills into two buckets: skills to keep for themselves, and skills they are willing to lose in order to get the job done, because moving a skill into the AI bucket is easy and getting it back is hard. He has consciously moved coding in Python and JavaScript, and literature search, into the second bucket, while still writing Lean by hand and still doing research mathematics himself, largely without the tools. Flexibility, he says, is the only safe prediction. He closes by returning to culture: mathematics has been drifting from the mathematical duels of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and from Gauss, who announced results as cryptic Latin anagrams to timestamp them, toward collaboration, with coauthor counts slowly rising. In the age of AI the field is diverging, some turning hypercompetitive and rushing out barely proofread papers, others building the collaborative projects he favors. Readjusting priorities toward exposition, communication, and collaboration, he says, is the work now.

Open questions

  1. Tao argues that problem solving was a good proxy for understanding only while proofs were scarce, and that the proxy has now broken. What should replace it in prizes, hiring, and journal prestige, and who gets to decide the new measures before the old ones harden again?
  2. His proposed settlement between labs and mathematicians, jointly designed problem sets with some problems reserved for humans and others given to AI, requires the labs to give up scraping the open literature. What incentive could make a lab accept that bargain when the literature is currently free to take?
  3. If mathematics becomes an experimental science of sweeping millions of problems, what happens to the fields and questions that cannot be swept, and does the new landscape quietly devalue the slow, single problem work that produced most of classical mathematics?

Matthew Berman · Anthropic's claimed Riemann hypothesis proof and what it would mean

Anthropic JUST Solved Mathematics… Now what?

On Matthew Berman, "Anthropic JUST Solved Mathematics… Now what?" reports a claim that would rank among the biggest in the history of science: an internal Claude model, working with Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpoge, has produced a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics. Berman presents the claim as fact and stacks up reactions to convey its scale. One mathematician at Imperial College London compares it to aliens with vastly superior intelligence arriving to explain the sacred mysteries, and to the discovery of the nuclear bomb. Another calls it, if correct, the biggest result in pure mathematics ever, the holy grail. Elon Musk is quoted declaring that unequivocal superintelligence has arrived, and Meta's Alexander Wang that superintelligence is arriving.

Berman is clear, though, that the solved problem is not the biggest deal. The bigger deal is how it was solved, and the questions that leaves behind. Humanity has always gained knowledge by trial and error: try something, see what works, iterate, and build a mental model along the way, so the understanding is earned as the knowledge grows. AI now inverts that. It hands us the answer first, and we have to reverse engineer the understanding afterward. Berman's analogy is flight. The Wright brothers flew for seconds, others iterated for decades, and the result is the modern airliner. Imagine instead that people who had never flown were suddenly handed a Boeing 747, a godlike device they could not operate, maintain, or explain. That, he says, is what is now happening in mathematics, and soon in science, materials discovery, and coding.

The Riemann hypothesis itself concerns prime numbers, the building blocks of mathematics, which appear in a pattern that looks random and has never been predictable. In 1859 the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann conjectured that the pattern is not truly random and can be predicted. Mathematicians have been nearly certain it is true ever since, but 99 percent certain is not proven, and a great many later proofs simply assume it. It is one of the Millennium Prize problems, carrying a million dollar prize. Terence Tao, widely regarded as the best mathematician alive, had thought it would take a fundamentally new approach, perhaps new AI, and guessed at a date around 2050. In Berman's telling, it fell last week.

What changed, Berman argues, is surprisingly little. There was no fundamental jump in AI capability and no new mathematics. The proof needed no new tools. It needed a deep command of all the existing tools of mathematics and a way of chaining them together in a novel sequence, holding thirty or forty or fifty complicated concepts in mind at once and tracking how each affects the others across a very long chain. Human minds are poorly suited to that. It is exactly what AI is suited to, and it is the same reason AI now outcodes humans: a person struggles to hold the context of a massive codebase, while a model can hold millions of lines and their interactions at once.

The human partner describes the experience in terms that underline the strangeness. Alpoge says that he and Claude had worked closely for months, but this time Claude far outpaced him near the finish line: Claude flew and danced and sparkled, Alpoge guided them to the right situation, and then Claude left him in awe. The output was 17 million lines of Lean, the programming language used to formally verify mathematics, which would run to about 250,000 printed pages. No human could ever read all of it, and Alpoge himself admits he does not yet fully understand how it works. Berman adds that, on his account, this is the third Millennium problem to fall to AI in a few weeks, after Navier-Stokes, which governs how fluids move, and P versus NP, which asks which problems are computable at all.

From there the video turns to what this era means. We are entering a period in which knowledge arrives prepackaged, answers delivered with a bow on top, often with no accompanying understanding of why they are true. Berman presses the uncomfortable questions. If the results exceed what human brains can follow, how do we verify they are right, and how do we make sure they are safe? Code is his example of the pattern: it climbed from binary to machine code to languages resembling English because humans are bad at reading ones and zeros, while machines are superb at it. What happens if AI invents a programming language a thousand times more efficient that humans cannot read at all? A system could do 99.9 percent of what we intend and something terrible in the remaining 0.1 percent, and we would have no way to check.

He reaches for the most famous earlier case of an AI move no one understood. In the AlphaGo match against Lee Sedol, the best Go player on Earth, the system's move 37 looked to every watching expert like an irrecoverable blunder. As the game developed it became clear the move was brilliant, and it won the game. Understanding arrived only later, when analysts took the game apart to see how the machine had won. Berman expects that pattern to become the normal one across fields: we will know things are true before we know why, and spend our time reverse engineering the reasons.

Mathematicians are already divided on what an unreadable proof is worth, and Berman plays clips from a 3Blue1Brown conversation to show it. Maxim Kontsevich says that if an AI produced a million token proof of the Riemann hypothesis he would not celebrate and move on; he would ask how it is done, because understanding is the entire point of doing mathematics. Jordan Ellenberg goes further: a Lean proof twenty billion tokens long that nobody can read is, in his view, not a proof of the hypothesis at all, and he does not care that it exists.

Berman expects the same split that appeared in coding. When AI became better than humans at writing code almost overnight, one group mourned the craft, while a second group, the builders, used it to ship more than ever. He counts himself in the second group: he loves coding, but he loves building more. Mathematics and science, he predicts, will divide the same way, into hobbyists who prove things by hand for the joy of it and a practical group that uses AI to discover more than ever before. He admits the cost: intellectual curiosity and discovery may become a hobby, no longer the engine of progress. And there is a harder risk. If AI can solve the deepest problems in mathematics, encryption, which rests on hard mathematics, could eventually crack too, taking privacy and crypto with it overnight.

He closes with a line from Anthropic's chief executive, Dario Amodei: we are in a race between interpretability and model intelligence, between how well we can understand these systems and how smart they get. As long as we can understand them, we are okay. The moment we cannot, we are in an unknown world. It is worth stressing what kind of claim sits underneath all of this. Berman presents the proof as settled fact, but a 17 million line machine generated proof that no human has read is, by the video's own account, not yet understood by anyone, and nothing in the video describes independent verification or peer review. The episode is best read as a dispatch from inside a claim, and an argument about what it would mean if the claim holds.

Open questions

  1. If a proof exists that no human can read, in what sense has the Riemann hypothesis been solved, and who or what should the mathematical community accept as the verifier: the Lean kernel, a panel of mathematicians reading samples, or a second AI?
  2. Berman treats reverse engineering understanding from finished answers as the new normal. Can understanding reliably be recovered that way at scale, or does knowledge that arrives without the trial and error process leave permanent gaps that only surface when something breaks?
  3. If discovery becomes something AI does and understanding becomes the human job, what happens to the pipeline that produces people capable of understanding, given that it was built by having them do the discovering themselves?

Turing Post TV · Two new JEPA papers and the limit of offline data

Yann LeCun's JEPA Just Hit the Limit of Offline AI (Here's What Comes Next)

On Turing Post TV, "Yann LeCun's JEPA Just Hit the Limit of Offline AI (Here's What Comes Next)" is an episode of Attention Span, the Turing Post explainer show hosted by Ksenia Se. It walks through two JEPA papers published on November 18, 2026 by Yann LeCun and collaborators at AMI Labs, NYU, and Brown University. One paper shows a world model drawing an accurate map of city streets it never saw mapped. The other proves a hard mathematical limit on what any model trained only on recorded data can ever answer, and then finds a surprisingly simple way to work right up against that limit.

The episode opens with that map. A model watches first-person street video and the steps the walker took, with no GPS, no coordinates, and no map, and the exact layout of the streets emerges inside its own internal representation. To explain how, Se first gives a refresher on how a JEPA, a joint embedding predictive architecture, plans. An encoder compresses what the camera sees into a compact numerical summary, a latent representation that keeps only what matters. A predictor then imagines how that summary changes under a candidate sequence of moves, and a planner tries many sequences and picks the one whose imagined ending lands closest to the goal point.

For that planning search to work, the internal space needs two things at once. It needs geometry: places that are close in the real world should sit smoothly connected in the model's map, so stepping toward a door shrinks the distance the planner measures. And it needs content: important objects, like a block resting on a table waiting to be pushed, have to stay readable in the representation even while they are not moving.

The first paper, "The Content–Geometry Tradeoff in World Models" by Mengye Ren, Ying Wang, Chris Hoang, Yilun Kuang, and LeCun, shows that standard JEPA training forces those two needs to fight. The usual trick for preventing collapse, called stop-gradient, protects resting objects, but with real, limited data, visual noise still leaks in and turns the latent map of a maze into a tangled mess. The alternative, dropping stop-gradient and adding SVReg, a sliced variance regularization that keeps the embeddings spread out in every direction, makes the noise vanish and unfolds a beautifully smooth map. But it creates the opposite failure: the encoder gets penalized for any detail that is hard to predict, so it takes the lazy shortcut and throws the resting cube out of its representation. On the cube task, it lost track of the cube's height in three out of six training runs. A gorgeous map of the room, Se notes, except the model forgot the furniture.

Their fix is called Slow SVReg, and it adds two things. A small slice of slow channels is encouraged to change only gradually from frame to frame, giving persistent objects a safe home in the representation. And a gated residual predictor starts by copying the current state as its default guess and predicts only what actually changed. Across five difficult visual planning benchmarks, two mazes, a reaching arm, a cube task, and a pushing task called Push-T, the method comes in first on every one. On Push-T, success jumps from 44 percent with standard stop-gradient training and 60 percent with a Gaussian regularizer to 87 percent. Trained on first-person city walks reconstructed from real street scans, the model reproduces the street loop in the top dimensions of its latent space, which Se connects to Edward Tolman's 1948 finding that rats build internal cognitive maps of mazes rather than memorizing turns. Nearly eighty years later, a cognitive map can be watched forming inside a model that only ever saw video.

The second paper, "Counterfactual World Models" by Randall Balestriero, LeCun, and Kerda Varaku, asks a harder question: what happens on a specific day when something invisible disrupts the plan? Their example is a boat aiming for a dock that gets pushed off course by an unseen underwater current. Learning from that mistake means asking where the boat would have ended up if it had aimed further left, which is an individual counterfactual, the third rung of Judea Pearl's ladder of causation. It matters because it separates bad decisions from bad luck. A normal world model, asked what happens when you steer left, averages over every current it saw in training and throws away the biggest clue available: where your boat actually landed a second ago.

Before proposing any fix, the authors prove something stronger. No world model trained only on offline data can answer individual what-if questions in general, not even a perfect generative video model with infinite data. Offline logs show thousands of boats heading east and thousands heading north, but they never record the same boat, at the same second, in the same unseen current, trying both headings. Two opposite what-if worlds can produce the exact same offline dataset. The authors call this action-dependent gauge freedom: the hidden noise can be relabeled differently for each action without changing any ordinary prediction. Scaling up offline data cannot fix a clue that is missing from the data itself.

The rescue comes from an old principle of natural philosophy: nature makes no leaps. When an action, like the boat's engine, and a hidden disturbance, like the current, act as two separate small forces, they barely interfere over a short time step, and the error appears only at second order. Halve the time step and the error shrinks to a quarter, and the authors prove that rate is minimax optimal, meaning no method using only offline data can guarantee better. That yields a rule the authors call residual reuse. Take what actually happened, subtract what the existing JEPA predicted for the action you took, and the leftover residual captures what the hidden disturbance did in that exact moment. Then add that residual to the model's prediction for the alternative action. There is no counterfactual training data, no extra parameters, and no retraining, just two calls to the same frozen JEPA and one subtraction.

The results are striking. Across seven frozen vision encoders, starting with DINOv2 and masked autoencoders, and four simulated robot tasks such as reaching for a target, catching a ball in a cup, and moving liquid, the same world models jump from explaining 38 to 46 percent of the counterfactual variation to explaining 86 to 96 percent. Working directly on raw image features with zero state labels, the method still reaches 73 to 94 percent. In hindsight planning, where a model that missed a target must work out which action would have reached it, the counterfactual JEPA finds a winning action 35 to 69 percent of the time, while an oracle baseline handed the exact simulator, but not shown what actually happened, manages only 14 to 24 percent. One real observation, it turns out, beats a perfect simulator flying blind. A section title in the paper makes a related point: pixels do not transport. Adding the residual in raw image space just smears two frames into a ghost; the trick only works in compact latent or physical coordinates, which is itself an argument for predicting in latent space the way JEPA does.

The shortcut breaks exactly where nature does make leaps. If the hidden disturbance multiplies the command instead of pushing alongside it, as with a worn motor that delivers only 60 percent of the force requested, doubling the command doubles the hidden error too, and carrying over a fixed residual gives the wrong answer. Performance falls sharply in that regime. The bigger frontier is touch and contact, where friction snaps from sticking to sliding and a two-millimeter difference separates a clean grasp from a knocked-over cup. Balestriero sent Se a note stating the limit plainly: counterfactual reasoning for complicated settings like touch and contact is very hard without simulated data or added expert structure, and the team can show that provably. Reaching human-level what-if reasoning there, he argues, may require online learning with robots deployed in the world. Offline data may simply not be enough, which could mark the frontier of what offline data can do. He adds a thought about humans: perhaps toddlers are not born with what-if intuition either, but build it by actively poking, dropping, and nudging the same objects thousands of times.

Se pairs that idea with a classic experiment. In 1963, Richard Held and Alan Hein raised pairs of kittens in darkness at Brandeis University, giving them daily sessions in a visual carousel where both animals saw exactly the same sights. The only difference was that one kitten walked under its own power and drove the carousel, while the other rode passively in a basket. Afterward, the active kitten had normal depth perception and placed its paws accurately; the passive kitten, despite watching the identical visual stream, failed. Watching the world without touching it was not enough to understand physical contact.

The episode then looks at what happens when JEPAs are let out of the basket. AdaJEPA, from the same NYU and AMI Labs team behind the map paper, keeps updating its world model online inside the planning loop, recalibrating its predictor with as little as one gradient step per replanning step. JEPA-TTT, which stands for test-time training, keeps adapting across whole episodes when the physics changes; after 500 test-time episodes it cuts prediction error by 83 percent and boosts planning by 153 percent compared with a frozen JEPA. But a third paper, published October 26, 2026, finds a trap inside online adaptation. Because the learner only sees outcomes of actions its planner chose, a model that wrongly believes a good action will fail never tries it, and grows accurate about its own mediocre habits while staying wrong about the move that would work. The paper, "Adapt for Planning, Not Just Prediction" by Akshay Mete, Dileep Kalathil, and P. R. Kumar, proposes optimism as the fix: alongside minimizing prediction error, the model gets a small planning term that nudges it to stay optimistic about actions that could reach the goal. Across three JEPA models under shifted conditions, that dose of curiosity roughly doubles the adaptation gain, lifting AdaJEPA from 6.9 percentage points to 13.5.

Se closes with the caveats. All five counterfactual environments are still simulated, the highest numbers rely on a small readout trained on labeled positions even though raw features still do well, and online learning on real robots, where hardware breaks and resets cost time and money, is far harder than in code. But the shape of the field has changed in a few months: researchers can now build smooth cognitive maps without erasing the objects inside them, separate bad decisions from bad luck in smooth physics, and point to a provable wall where offline datasets stop being enough. Her closing question is what crosses that wall first, much better physics simulators generating synthetic counterfactuals, or fleets of real robots stepping out of the basket to learn actively in the physical world.

Open questions

  1. Residual reuse is provably the best any offline method can do on smooth systems, but contact is not smooth. What evidence would settle whether simulator-generated counterfactuals can substitute for real touch experience, or whether robots truly have to learn contact by acting in the world?
  2. The impossibility result says the missing clue is a paired observation that offline logs never contain. Does the same wall apply to language models trained on text, which records humans narrating their own what-if reasoning, or is recorded reasoning a different kind of data?
  3. OpTTA shows online adaptation needs optimism so a planner keeps trying actions its model underrates. On a real robot, how should that optimism be bounded so curiosity does not become a safety hazard?
