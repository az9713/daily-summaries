Cat Tang · Charlie O'Neill on open source AI and Baseten's Base Labs

The Rise of Open Source AI with Baseten's Charlie O'Neill

On Cat Tang, “The Rise of Open Source AI with Baseten's Charlie O'Neill” is a conversation with Charlie O'Neill, who runs Base Labs, a Baseten lab focused on open source intelligence. Earlier this year O'Neill published a blog post he calls the Michael Scott Paper Company of AI, arguing that open source models should be judged against an absolute threshold of intelligence rather than against their distance from the closed frontier. That argument, and the research Base Labs has done since on post-training, continual learning, and distillation, structures the whole conversation.

The threshold idea is simple. For any economically valuable task there is a level of intelligence a model has to reach before it can do the task at all, and past that level the returns to still more intelligence diminish quickly. Closed models will almost always reach a new threshold first, which is why startups reaching for product market fit start on frontier closed models. But open models reach the same threshold eventually, and once they do, there are many reasons to switch. On OpenRouter, the host notes, open models have gone from roughly 20 percent of token volume and 10 percent of revenue a year ago to roughly 70 percent of volume and 30 percent of revenue now.

O'Neill does not think open source will ever fully catch the closed frontier, and he thinks that is a good thing. A lag of six to nine months gives society a window to prepare. He points to the cyber debate that followed the announcement of Mythos and Project Glasswing: frontier closed labs first let top enterprise customers use the model to harden their own defenses, and by the time open models such as GLM-5.3 reached a comparable level of cyber capability, there had been time to get ready, and no massive breaches have followed so far. He would not want the lead to shrink to zero, but he would not want it to balloon to two to five years either, because then the problem becomes centralization of intelligence and power.

On cost, the host pushes back that closed models have also become much cheaper, with OpenAI in particular competing hard for token volume. O'Neill agrees that closed labs offering models along the whole Pareto frontier is healthy, and notes that some open models, such as Kimi K3 at a rumored 2.8 trillion parameters, may now be among the largest models in the world. But he argues open source keeps a structural edge in customization. A closed model is a one size fits all product. An open model can be post-trained for one workload, quantized for exactly that workload, and run on exactly the right hardware configuration, and that fitting is where much of Baseten's demand comes from.

The usual objection is that post-training itself costs money and effort. O'Neill answers that for any company that has reached product market fit, training costs are a rounding error next to inference costs, which grow across the whole life of a model. The real friction is not cost but building good RL environments to climb on. His framing, held since he started Parsed two years ago, is that an RL environment is not a one-off artifact thrown away when a better base model arrives. It is a compounding corpus, refined and curated over time like a pre-training corpus, and the sooner a company starts building one, the better.

That logic applies to a 50,000 person company as much as to a Series A startup, he says, though AI native firms are moving first because they have less inertia. Out of the box, the best models are vanilla ice cream, an average of every behavior aggregated across their training. The host raises the fear that partnering with a lab hands the lab your data and your moat. O'Neill calls that overblown: the labs' terms are fairly honest about data use, labs mostly learn what their models do badly from public feedback, and any future exchange of cheap inference for data will be explicit. He points to OpenAI announcing, at DevDay the day before, a partnership with Baseten to run open source models on OpenAI credits for enterprises, including inside Codex, as a sign that the labs will focus on frontier workloads while a large share of the world's tasks runs elsewhere. Privacy and per customer containment, he adds, will keep pushing specific workloads to open models run by the customer.

Beyond cost and intelligence sit the guardrails. Every model carries decisions about ethics, morality, and censorship baked in through its training data and its reinforcement learning, whoever trained it. For high stakes work in fields such as law and medicine, those boundaries are subjective, and O'Neill argues you want fine grained control over them yourself, which is another reason to consider serving an open model.

On compute, the host observes that OpenAI and Anthropic together will end the year at around 10 gigawatts, a little under 20 percent of roughly 56 gigawatts of United States data center capacity, and are slotted to take around half of all incremental capacity over the next couple of years. O'Neill replies that the labs got there by hitting real revenue first, which let them make aggressive long term compute commitments. Inference providers and neoclouds have now hit the scale to make the same bets, supply is still growing, including from China, and firms that do not train models can earn more per gigawatt because they carry no training or research costs. With suppliers such as NVIDIA also invested in spreading compute beyond two buyers, he expects the labs' relative share to settle lower than the current trajectory suggests, though he concedes the whole dynamic leans on investors continuing to fund the labs at a Pascal's wager scale.

The longest stretch of the conversation concerns continual learning. O'Neill questions whether intelligence really requires it, citing Dwarkesh's image of the trumpet playing intern, a stream of brilliant newcomers who must write everything down for the next one. With compaction and memory files now good, many tasks may need nothing more. Where something better is needed, he sees two broad routes. One is updating the weights, the holy grail, which runs into catastrophic forgetting. The other is stretching effective context, for instance by neurally compressing the KV cache into something between the very lossy memory of weights and the perfectly lossless memory of a context window, a direction he connects to the linear attention work at labs such as DeepSeek and Moonshot. His own team's research is sobering: in an iterative regime, supervised fine-tuning and distillation degrade a model's general capabilities, reinforcement learning is minimally destructive but struggles to teach genuinely new information, because a fact the model never samples never gets learned, and nobody has yet demonstrated knowledge and capability acquisition together without loss. The big labs manage it only because at their scale the noise washes out.

Asked where a new company should aim, O'Neill says the most valuable thing in the world will be neither a training recipe nor compute, but data, and specifically RL environments, which are what is being scaled now. The valuable context still lives in people's heads, and the winning move is to get it out into a signal a model can climb on, the way Cursor did almost by accident. Base Labs, announced a few weeks ago, is his bet on that view: the team is opening up the RL environments it has spent years building inside companies, environments that would cost millions of dollars each from a vendor, so that open model trainers, evaluators, and alignment researchers can use them. The hardest part, he says, is calibration, keeping an environment's difficulty tuned to the jagged frontier so models get neither zero reward nor a saturated score, and designing tasks that can be generated backwards from known ground truth, as in search feedback and statistical data generation. He also insists benchmarks and RL environments are the same thing: a benchmark with separation between models, no saturation, and no reward hacking is an RL environment for free.

The closing topic is Base Labs' research on distillation. Distillation, he says, is best understood as a shortcut: a way to warm start a model on traces from stronger models so that reinforcement learning has a non-zero signal to work with, cheaper than building a full curriculum of easier environments. In their experiments, though, models trained with distillation first and RL second ended with a lower ceiling than models trained with RL alone, given enough compute. One candidate explanation is that distillation, being a form of supervised fine-tuning, craters the model's entropy and leaves it exploring too little during RL. The science is young, he stresses, and whether the effect holds for frontier scale agentic tasks is the next thing his team wants to test, with Chinese labs currently holding roughly a tenth of American compute capacity and that balance likely to shift over the next couple of years.

Open questions

  1. O'Neill calls a six to nine month lag between the closed frontier and open models a healthy equilibrium for safety. Who, if anyone, is responsible for keeping the lag in that band as capabilities grow, and what happens if it collapses on its own?
  2. If benchmarks and RL environments are truly the same thing, what keeps a widely used public environment from being trained against until it stops measuring or teaching anything useful, and who maintains its calibration over time?
  3. His research suggests distillation lowers a model's ceiling while making reinforcement learning cheaper to start. How should labs with limited compute weigh a lower ceiling against a faster start, and does the answer change as the compute gap between Chinese and American labs narrows?

Y Combinator · Eric Glyman on building Ramp and how to build the future

How To Build The Future: Eric Glyman, Co-CEO of Ramp

On Y Combinator, “How To Build The Future: Eric Glyman, Co-CEO of Ramp” is a conversation between Y Combinator president Garry Tan and Eric Glyman, who co-founded the finance platform Ramp with Kareem Azar. Ramp began seven years ago as a corporate card built on one promise, to save companies time and money. It is now valued at $60 billion, runs the financial operations of tens of thousands of businesses, and increasingly does that work with AI agents.

Glyman's first company, Paribus, grew out of a personal annoyance. A flight he had booked dropped $100 in price the day after he bought it, and when he read the airline's policy he found that a refund was owed and almost never claimed. He and Azar built a tool that scanned email receipts, watched prices, and claimed the difference automatically, emailing retailers from the customer's own account. Glyman now calls it one of the prehistoric AI agents. The first version was a script written in VBA, and the earliest users came from a Reddit forum for people hunting small amounts of cash. One in twenty new users saved money within minutes of signing up.

Paribus applied to Y Combinator twice before it was accepted, joined the Summer 2015 batch, and had nearly a million users within a year of graduating. Its business model, keeping a quarter of whatever it saved, made people suspect the deal was too good to be true. Listening to users describe the app to friends, the founders heard the fee repeated as the catch, so they built a two-sided referral: refer someone and both sides pay a lower rate, and referrals took off. Capital One bought the company, and Glyman and Azar spent roughly two and a half years inside the bank.

Inside Capital One they saw a world-class organization whose processes often killed great ideas, and a card industry whose incentives ran against its own customers. Card companies profit when people spend more, and one of their largest costs is the points and rewards they advertise, so the institutional incentive is to make points feel valuable while devaluing them or letting them go unused. In user research, though, customers kept saying something simpler: they just wanted more money in their bank accounts. Cancelling a forgotten $100 subscription leaves a customer far better off than a one percent rebate, and no card was doing that work. The lesson Glyman drew was that a financial product could be designed to help people spend less, and could compete on value rather than on rebates.

Ramp is the same idea with the ability to act. Paribus could see enormous waste in the receipts it scanned, but its only mechanism was to email customer service and hope for a refund. Ramp sits at the movement of money itself, so it can stop a purchase or prevent a charge from a subscription a company no longer wants, rather than merely reporting the waste after the fact. The business model insight came before the technology: take the profit pools of financial products and spend them on software that makes customers better off, and there is more to invest than competitors who simply build the software. Selling to startups first also compounded; early customers such as Cursor and Cognition started as a handful of people and grew into large companies on the platform.

Glyman calls the $60 billion valuation the end of the beginning. The first chapter was unification. Most companies still run cards, expenses, bills, procurement, and travel through separate systems that do not talk to each other, and Ramp spent seven years combining them into one place designed to keep fewer dollars from leaving the organization. The median customer now saves about 7.5 percent of its expenses a year, up from about 5 percent a year ago. The second chapter runs in the other direction: not only fewer dollars leaving, but making each dollar spent produce more, through newer products such as accounts receivable and the AI tools called Glass and Inspect.

The second chapter is being driven by what AI did inside Ramp itself. Spending on AI tokens is extremely concentrated, with the top 1 percent of spenders accounting for 80 percent of token spend, and Glyman saw the same pattern internally: a small group of people became extraordinarily productive while other functions lagged, and the usual ratios between engineering, product, and design began to break. Teams that used little AI asked for the most new hires. Earlier this year, even while growing at more than 100 percent a year, Ramp stopped hiring for a full quarter, in part to force the issue: learning to work with AI had to become a core competency, not something fitted into spare time.

Two internal tools carry much of the change. Inspect, Ramp's coding agent, works with the same code, data, and systems an engineer can see, and it now produces more than 85 percent of the company's pull requests, while pull requests per engineer keep growing by roughly 40 percent a month. A customer complaint that once entered a ticket queue can now be read, located in the code, fixed, and answered in about fifteen minutes. Glass is the company brain layered on top: it connects the data warehouse, analytics, and customer support systems, keeps shared skills so that what one team learns becomes the default for others, and holds its knowledge even when the person who created it leaves. In the twenty-four hours before the recording, 1,925 of Ramp's 1,930 people had used it. One Glass application, a policy agent, reviews expenses against company policy at 99 percent accuracy and revises the policy itself when the policy turns out to be incomplete. As of this week, Ramp has begun onboarding customers onto Glass for their own companies.

Glyman argues that large companies usually die of indigestion: coordination costs rise, information gets siloed, and the hard calls stop being made. AI, he says, can let heavyweight companies move like lightweights again, and he offers Ramp as evidence: more than $2 billion a year in revenue, growing more than 25 percent faster than it was a year ago. He also makes a striking claim about the wider market: over the last two months, Jevons paradox has stopped holding for AI tokens, meaning cheaper intelligence is no longer producing proportionally greater consumption. His explanation is not a ceiling on the models but a limit on how fast society and organizations can absorb them, and it feeds his bet on the future he prefers, a world of hyper-specialized firms rather than one lab capturing everything.

His hiring advice follows Sutton's law, named for the bank robber who robbed banks because that is where the money is: go where builders demonstrably build. A teenager who ran a paying business on Minecraft servers has already shown they can make things people want, and teenagers winning mathematics and informatics competitions show the same proof for problem solving. His closing message to his younger self is about asking for help. The most powerful words a founder can say, he says, are “I need your help,” and he spent too many years waiting to say them.

Open questions

  1. If cheaper AI has really stopped producing proportionally more consumption, is the binding constraint now how fast organizations can absorb the technology, and what restarts demand growth: new model families, or organizational redesign catching up?
  2. Can a company brain like Glass work inside organizations whose leaders benefit from information silos, or does it only take hold where a chief executive forces the cultural change from the top?
  3. As non-engineers produce most of a company's code changes and support staff ship fixes the same day, what happens to review standards, accountability, and the craft of software engineering?

David Senra · Brian Armstrong on the golden age of building and the agent economy

Why Now Is The Golden Age of Building | Brian Armstrong, Coinbase

On David Senra, “Why Now Is The Golden Age of Building | Brian Armstrong, Coinbase” is a conversation with Brian Armstrong, the co-founder and chief executive of Coinbase. Senra builds the episode around Armstrong's own posts, reading them back and asking him to expand, and the conversation ranges from how Armstrong reprograms his own habits, to the teams of AI agents he now manages at Coinbase, to the agent economy he believes those agents will create. It opens, unexpectedly, with self-brainwashing. Armstrong treats the brain as something like a neural network whose weights can be updated by repetition. In his twenties he wrote down that he wanted to start a billion dollar tech company, long before he had started a company of any size, and within six or seven years of moving to Silicon Valley he had done it.

The clearest example is conflict. Early on as a chief executive, Armstrong was conflict averse, and he found the hard conversations the job constantly demands to be a source of stress. So he wrote down, over and over, that he loves conflict. It does not mean he seeks fights, he says. It means that when a product review is not good enough he can now say so plainly, even angrily, and use the emotion as fuel to create change. People often thank him afterward, because everyone in the room could already sense the work was off track.

Senra reaches for David Ogilvy's phrase for people like this: divinely discontent. Armstrong says he will never feel the Coinbase app is good enough, and that he messages thirty or forty people a day asking why some part of it is slow, broken, or unfinished. Asked whether that inner voice is negative, he says it is motivational rather than self-punishing, and that the goal is fulfillment rather than happiness. Happiness is a fleeting high, he argues, while fulfillment comes from meaningful work and visible progress over a long arc. Senra tells the story of Daniel Ek advising Dara Khosrowshahi, then hesitating over the Uber chief executive job, with a question: since when is life about happiness? This is about impact.

Armstrong traces some of his instincts to strategy games. He keeps a printout of the Civilization tech tree in his home office, from agriculture and pottery at the start to artificial intelligence and biotech near the end, and says he has always wanted to add a leaf to that tree. Business, to him, is the same game played over decades: collect resources, build, expand. He also concedes the solo version of that game has limits. A programmer working alone eventually has to learn to work through teams of people, which for a nerdy introvert was its own hard skill.

That lesson now has an AI version. In a recent post that drew a million or two million views, Armstrong described changing how he works over the last few weeks. He now runs one lead agent for each area he focuses on, seven or eight of them, overseen by a chief of staff agent. Each lead manages sub-agents that write code, test it, chase reviews, and coordinate with team members. Instead of prompting and reading a reply, he gives an agent an area of responsibility and a goal, and it comes back with analysis and proposals: here are the five biggest opportunities in onboarding, here is what I propose to start with, do you agree? He rates the planning quality at roughly the level of a strong mid-level employee, and notes you can now have almost an infinite team of them. AI is not a human, he says, the way a submarine is not a fish. It already exceeds people in some ways, working around the clock and copying itself without limit, and still misses the big picture or turns derivative in others.

Two internal systems support this. Coinbase fine-tunes smaller open-weight models on internal data for narrow jobs, and in some cases those models now outperform the frontier models: one model does nothing but draft compliance reports, but it has been trained on so much internal data that it beats the general models at exactly that. The second system, which he calls Coinbase Brain, keeps a living memory for every employee, every team, and every code repository. It ingests experiments, user studies, incidents, meeting transcripts, code reviews, and policy documents, and each night distills them into lessons. When an agent starts work in an area, the relevant brain goes into its context, so its output matches how the company actually works. Increasingly, he says, the human job is to cultivate those brains: editing them, resolving contradictions, and keeping final decisions and final code reviews human. Asked whether Coinbase could run with far fewer people, he guesses the headcount stays roughly the same and everyone simply does much more.

Managing that many agents broke the chat interface. Armstrong found himself drowning in walls of text and context switching, so Coinbase is prototyping a different surface: a prioritized inbox of decisions. Items arrive in the same place, in the same format, ranked by how much each decision would move the work forward, and he can clear them with keyboard shortcuts or quick voice feedback, almost like processing email. The next layer up, he thinks, is something like a vice-president-level agent: give it a goal such as expanding into Brazil, and check in every few months while it recruits, partners, and signs deals. He is clear about what cannot be prompted into existence, though. Regulatory licenses cannot. Neither can the defensibility that comes from liquidity, from the network effects of the most liquid exchange in the United States, or from USDC. Those moats matter more, not less, as software gets easier to produce.

The same logic extends beyond one company. Agents will increasingly transact with agents at other companies, and Armstrong argues they will need what human economies needed: markets, money, and specialists who can assemble into teams. Credit cards will play an early role for compatibility, but he expects stablecoin rails on crypto to become the real-time payment layer of what he calls agentic finance. Asked what share of global GDP transactions will be agent to agent in ten years, he answered 99.999 percent, essentially all of them. He also argues AI itself should stay decentralized, with many small open-weight models people control, rather than one or two big players whose systems could be biased or captured. Coinbase's ambition follows from that: to be the most trusted crypto brand was the first act, and the second is to be the most AI-enabled financial account, with strict controls and the customer always in charge.

Security is the shadow side of building this fast. Armstrong says cyber incidents, in crypto and in traditional finance alike, have roughly doubled year over year, and that October was so bad the industry calls it Black October. Coinbase uses frontier agents to review every line of code before it reaches production, keeps 99 percent of its crypto entirely offline and air-gapped around the world, and has concluded that one of its biggest remaining risks is third-party vendors, whose security it cannot control. The response is to reduce the vendor count by rebuilding vendor software in house, which he admits is also simply more fun. He is, by his own account, having so much fun building that he struggles to sleep, and he calls this a golden age of building and productivity that he feels lucky to be in the prime of his career for.

Senra asks why, if builders feel this way, so much of the public dislikes AI. Armstrong blames wiring: people who feared change tended to survive, so caution is the human default, and only a small share of people are built to run toward it. He tells of an Amazon board member who asks a room how many worry about Amazon's effect on local business, watches most hands rise, then asks how many used Amazon in the last month, and watches every hand rise. People hold both views at once. The pair trade older panics: the bicycle, which newspapers warned would disfigure riders with bicycle face and dissolve village courtship, and cryptography, which Congress nearly banned for civilian use. Armstrong's conclusion is that the precautionary principle is dangerous. Regulators tend to imagine the wrong risk, and rules written against an imagined future can kill an industry, as he says happened to nuclear power.

On whether Coinbase will sell its internal agent tools, Armstrong is skeptical. A tool built for one company is hyper-specialized; making it work for any company is not twice the work but more like ten to fifty times, once every customer's security needs and billing preferences arrive. Coding harnesses, he predicts, will mostly exist as open source that every company runs internally. The exception proves the rule: the crypto infrastructure Coinbase had to build for itself, connecting to every blockchain, custody, and staking, is now sold as the Coinbase Developer Platform, and he says seven of the largest banks in the world are building on it for stablecoin wallets, payments, and trading. He calls it a candidate to be Coinbase's AWS.

One paradox occupies him. AI has produced a material increase in productivity at Coinbase, but revenue has not materially moved yet. He offers two theories: revenue is a lagging indicator that will show the step change within months, or productivity is a Red Queen race in which everyone runs faster to stay in place. He believes the first, partly because traditional finance is barely leaning in. The larger frame is that all of fintech combined still captures only about 4 percent of global financial services revenue, while traditional firms hold the other 96 percent. Fintechs imagine they compete with each other, he says, crediting Nubank's David Vélez with making the point well, but the real prize is share taken from traditional finance, plus the entirely new market of the agent economy. In his formula, crypto is the rails and AI is the interface.

He sketches what that interface does to investing, in levels. Today a customer picks an asset, an order type, and an amount. One level up, the agent builds toward a target share of a portfolio over time, buying dips along the way. Above that, the customer supplies only a thesis, that the bottleneck for AI is energy, or that longevity drugs will be huge, and the agent assembles a basket, Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk for the GLP-1 makers, with entry points, profit targets, and stop losses. At the top level the customer has no thesis at all and follows a trusted trader's moves, or simply talks with the agent: everyone gets a private banker in their pocket, the thing only wealthy clients have today, down to the nuisance work of moving idle cash somewhere it earns something. What AI still cannot do is physical. Armstrong wants humanoid robots that can move through the world and represent him, and at New Limit, the biotech company he co-founded, the team debates when to test robots in the lab. Their threshold is humble: once a robot can work as a barista, they will start testing it in the biology lab.

Open questions

  1. Armstrong rates today's agents as strong mid-level employees available in nearly unlimited numbers, with humans keeping final decisions. As the agents improve toward the vice-president level he describes, what breaks first: the supply of human attention to review their proposals, or the company's ability to tell good proposals from confident ones?
  2. He bets that revenue is a lagging indicator and the productivity gains will show up in it within months. What evidence, over the next year or two, would separate that story from the Red Queen alternative, in which every firm's gains cancel out and only customers capture the value?
  3. If agent to agent transactions really do approach the share of global activity Armstrong predicts, who sets the permissions, limits, and liability rules when one company's agent harms another company's customer, and does a decentralized stablecoin rail make those disputes easier or harder to settle?

Ryan Peterman · Dwayne Reeves on his top two career learnings as a Meta senior staff engineer

Meta Senior Staff Eng (IC7): His Top Two Career Learnings | Dwayne Reeves

On Ryan Peterman, “Meta Senior Staff Eng (IC7): His Top Two Career Learnings | Dwayne Reeves” is a conversation with Dwayne Reeves, a Meta engineer who reached the senior staff level as an individual contributor before later moving into management. Peterman asks whether any particular moments taught him something that helped him grow as an IC. Reeves names two, and in both cases the promotion that followed, first to IC5 and then to IC6, arrived as a surprise.

The first learning grew out of his promotion to senior engineer. Reeves had built a Hack API for an internal framework, and because that project went well he was asked to do the same thing at a much larger scale: move more of the company's code from PHP to Hack. He spent a half on that effort.

Up to that point, Reeves measured his value to the company entirely by the code he personally wrote. The scaling work left almost no time for that. He was talking to people constantly, giving ideas to the Hack team and suggesting work to other engineers, and he felt he was having the worst half of his career. His manager first advised him to find some time to write code himself, then reversed herself within about a week, telling him the earlier advice was wrong and that he should keep focusing on the end results.

The results were strong. Hack coverage of the codebase rose from roughly 20 or 30 percent to roughly 60 or 70 percent, and Hack became the default: people were starting new code in Hack rather than PHP. Even so, Reeves had written the least code of his career, and he was unsure how that would play in his performance review. It turned out to be the highest review he had ever received, and he was promoted to IC5, a step he had not even considered himself in the running for. The lesson he drew is that writing code is not the job. The job is to identify and solve problems. Sometimes the best way to do that is to write code, and sometimes it is to bring clarity to a problem and a path forward while others write the code.

The second learning led to his promotion to IC6. After that half, Reeves was invited to join the Hack team itself, starting around 2015, and was told he could be its tech lead, a role he admits he did not understand at the time. Most of the engineers who had been on the team left, leaving Reeves, one engineer he brought over, and a new graduate hire responsible for the whole system. He struggled for a few years with what a tech lead owns. He had accepted that his output did not need to be the code he wrote, but he still felt responsible for seeing outcomes through end to end.

That belief was tested by a year-long effort to redesign how Hack's collections system works. Reeves did the design work and convinced the runtime team to implement it, and the implementation began to roll out. Then his manager told him to work on something else. Reeves was upset: this project was, in his mind, his path to IC6. His manager answered with a rocket ship analogy. A rocket has stages. The first set of engines exists to get it out of orbit, and once it has escaped orbit, other jets can fire. The manager said he had only cared about Reeves getting the project out of orbit. Now that was done, Reeves should pass it to someone else and find something else to do. He would not let Reeves leave their one-on-one until he agreed.

Reeves handed the project to another engineer. At the end of the half, when he raised the question of his path to IC6, his manager told him the promotion had already come through. Looking back, Reeves says both promotions surprised him because there was a mismatch between what he thought he needed to do to perform at the next level and what actually mattered. Each time, the important move was being willing to give up direct control of the outcome. The real contribution was aligning on the right strategy and the right problems in the first place.

Peterman observes that this is the pattern for senior ICs: they figure out what is important to solve and why, get the alignment, and get the work out of orbit, then hand it to someone who is strong at getting it done. He also notes that Reeves's managers were proactive in driving his growth, and that Reeves adopted the behaviors almost begrudgingly, with the promotions arriving as a byproduct. Reeves adds two qualifications. It was early at the company, and his managers may have been willing to move faster rather than hold a promotion for another half. And across his whole career, the most consistent pattern he sees is his relationship with his manager. When he felt disconnected from his manager, he felt most frustrated. When he felt connected, he did his best work.

  2. Reeves's first lesson is that senior engineers are valued for results rather than personal code output. What signals can an engineer use in the middle of a half to tell whether coordination and alignment work is genuinely high leverage, rather than a way of drifting away from hard technical problems?
  3. The rocket analogy assumes a capable successor is ready once a project has left orbit. What should a tech lead do when there is no obvious person to hand the work to, or when handing it off would put the outcome at risk?

AI News & Strategy Daily | Nate B Jones · The robot brain might already exist as a coding agent

The Robot Brain Might Already Exist. It's A Coding Agent.

On AI News & Strategy Daily | Nate B Jones, "The Robot Brain Might Already Exist. It's A Coding Agent." argues that the next breakthrough in robotics may not come from a robotics lab at all. It could arrive as an ordinary update to a coding agent. Jones points to a Stanford project that connected a general coding agent to a robot through a visual interface, with no robot-specific training of any kind, and watched it complete 24 of 25 trials across five physical tasks.

His frame for the story is an old idea: the Bitter Lesson. Richard Sutton wrote it in 2019, arguing that AI researchers keep repeating the same mistake. They figure out what makes a task hard, build that understanding into a system, and get something that works, until a more general approach comes along that keeps improving with more computation, more learning, and more search, and walks right past the hand-built system. Sutton traced the pattern through chess, Go, speech recognition, and computer vision. Jones's suggestion is that robotics may be next: some of a robot's intelligence may already be getting built as a side effect of making AI better at everything else, from visual understanding to tool use to noticing when something went wrong.

The Stanford project is called VIA, for Visual Interface Agent. In the physical experiment, the researchers paired a general coding agent with a commercial Stretch robot and gave the agent a visual interface: it sees the camera view and places a virtual gripper where it thinks the real gripper should end up. If the target looks a touch high from one view or too far forward from another, the agent adjusts it before anything moves. Only then does it tell the controller to move the real gripper to the chosen spot, and afterwards it checks: did the gripper catch the handle, did it miss, does it need to adjust before pulling?

The model, in other words, is not computing every motor movement moment by moment. The controller handles the mechanics of moving the arm; the agent's job is choosing the target, connecting what it sees with what it wants to happen, and checking the result. Jones compares it to the human brain, which picks where a hand should go without thinking about which muscles to fire. It is the same loop an agent runs when it uses a computer: look at what is available, use a tool, check what changed, decide what to do next. Here the tool happens to move a physical machine, and the consequences are physical too.

The behavior that emerged was more resourceful than a fixed routine. In one task from the researchers' write-up, the robot was wiping a mark off a table, and its own gripper blocked the view, so the agent moved the gripper away to check whether the table was clean. In another attempt, holding the wipe was not working well enough, so the agent left the wipe on the table and pushed it with the closed gripper. Try something, see whether it worked, adjust: that loop, Jones argues, is what a general-purpose agent brought to robotics for free, because the capability had been developed for other uses like coding and then simply given a way to operate the machine.

OpenAI, meanwhile, has reorganized around a related bet. The company confirmed that its robotics researchers were moving into main research, with applied robotics and hardware moving into its applied organization, while insisting robotics is still part of the plan. Its explanation is that general frontier models are becoming more useful for robotics work. Jones notes the reporting also discussed financial pressures, but he finds the technical logic easy to see: if the people improving the general model are also improving its ability to operate a robot essentially for free, the robotics effort benefits from being embedded in the work happening across the company.

He is careful about the limits. Not every specialized robotics model is doomed: a model trained on huge amounts of physical experience can follow the Bitter Lesson too, and the open question is how much needs to be built specifically for a robot when a general model can do much of the same work. The connection also has to be built well. A November study he cites kept the underlying model fixed and substantially improved its simulation score simply by improving the software around the model, so the harness and interface matter, because they determine what the model can see and do.

The biggest practical problem, he says, is speed. A separate October study makes the point painfully: its simulation could pause while the model thought, but the physical world keeps going. In trials with a G1 robot, roughly 90 percent of the time went to model deliberation, and successful manipulation runs took over an hour. That may be tolerable at a quiet workbench, but not when an object starts sliding off an edge or a manufacturing line needs a response now. A robot that eventually finishes while someone stands there supervising may consume more attention than it saves.

His closing advice runs in two directions. If you follow AI, look beyond the obvious use of each new model: an improvement that shows up in computer use may also help a machine interpret a camera view or keep track of a physical task, and that transfer is something we can now test rather than assume. And if you are building specialized systems on the assumption that the general model will not get better, be very sure the Bitter Lesson does not apply to you, because the history of AI is littered with people who bet against it and got it wrong. For people tinkering with robots, he predicts, 2027 is going to be a pretty good year.

Open questions

  1. The Stanford result rests on 25 trials across five tasks in a controlled setup. How far does the transfer hold as tasks get longer, messier, and less forgiving than a workbench, and what breaks first: perception, planning, or recovery from mistakes?
  2. If general models keep absorbing robotics capability, where does the durable value sit for robotics companies: in the harness and interface layer, in proprietary physical training data, or in hardware and real-time control that a deliberating model cannot replace?
  3. Speed is the clearest blocker, with one cited study spending roughly 90 percent of trial time on model deliberation. What combination of faster inference, smaller local models, and reactive controllers closes that gap without giving up the general reasoning that made the transfer work?

AI Engineer · Walden Yan on 40x more merged PRs at Cognition

40x More Merged PRs With 2.7x the Engineers — Walden Yan, Cognition

On AI Engineer, “40x More Merged PRs With 2.7x the Engineers — Walden Yan, Cognition” is a talk by Walden Yan, a co-founder of Cognition and its chief product officer, about how his own engineering team learned to move much faster with the company's coding agent, Devin. The backdrop is a chart of Cognition's merged pull request throughput over roughly twelve months. In that year the number of merged pull requests grew fortyfold, while engineering headcount grew by a factor of about 2.7, so each engineer is now more than ten times as productive as before. Yan insists the pull requests are as good as they were a year ago, if not better, and the talk is his account of the practical steps that produced the change.

The starting point is what he calls the software factory. By default, everything should be self-driving: pull requests run from an initial bug report through to final merge, monitoring, and maintenance without a single human involved. Human judgment is still needed at times, and that is what escalations are for, but the entire development process is run by Devin end to end unless a person has a reason to step in.

That model forces the engineer's job to change. In a conversation with the team around March of this year, Yan argued that anyone still picking tickets off a board and handing them to an agent to implement is not set up to succeed, because anything that can be described and implemented can simply be sent to Devin. Cognition broke its larger teams into small pods of two or three engineers and gave them vertical responsibility instead: the same engineers listen to customer bug reports, do the QA testing, and do the product management. Each engineer manages an entire board of work and sends it to agents to implement, with swarms of agents doing the building.

The working surface for all of this is Slack. A customer might send a video of a bug, and the team forwards it straight into an internal channel and tags Devin, asking it to fix the problem. Devin produces a pull request and sends back a video showing the fix as proof. Yan says he rarely has to leave Slack, and the end goal is a development lifecycle that never requires opening an IDE or GitHub at all.

Underneath that sits a large infrastructure change. Cognition used to test against cloud deployments and staging environments, with engineers clicking through flows by hand. The company migrated every service so it can run locally with Docker Compose, built mocked versions of every third-party service, and put the whole thing inside a single virtual machine that each agent can use. Agents never need to touch live services to test a change. The virtual machines are rebuilt every hour from the latest code, and when a build breaks, Devin itself is the first responder that fixes the snapshot. Agents also write their own skills for how to test the codebase and keep updating those skills as the codebase changes, so the testing knowledge maintains itself.

The results change how engineers work day to day. Several Cognition engineers have moved to iPads for development, which Yan also argues is more secure, because everything runs in controlled cloud machines rather than on laptops full of scattered secrets. The testing can be elaborate: while developing Devin's ability to join voice and video calls, Cognition had Devin create multiple accounts, join the same call from inside one virtual machine, and exchange voice memos to check the feature worked.

On top of this, Yan wants recursive self-improvement aimed at the process itself. Because every line of code, pull request, and decision in a cloud agent setup is tracked, agents can analyze where the team wastes time and tokens. Devin posts daily ideas in a channel, such as a node setup change that could shave a minute off CI, or a lint change that could catch a fifth more bugs before AI code review. The agents, in effect, optimize the software factory that produces them.

The larger shift he points to is from giving agents tasks to giving them jobs. His example is back end performance: instead of telling an agent what to do, you tell it you want to improve the application's P99 latency, and the agent adds the telemetry to find the current number, sets up alerts and reporting, breaks the work down, and sends daily updates on progress. Anything in a company that seems unmeasurable should be made measurable, he argues, whether that is latency, layout shifts in an interface, or onboarding retention, and then an agent can be set to optimize that number around the clock.

He gives two examples of jobs handed to agents. Cognition was among the first to receive Nvidia's new Vera Rubin GPUs, but no kernels existed for them yet, so the company gave an agent a permanent set of the GPUs and a single goal: push their throughput as far as it would go. Over about a week the agent found eight or nine strategies that worked, the best were sent to a human engineer for final approval, and throughput went from worse than the previous GB300 chips to significantly better than them. The second example is the company's Android app, which a small team had no bandwidth to staff. An engineer named Samuel gave Devin the standing job of keeping the Android app at parity with the iOS app, end to end, with permanent hooks watching the iOS codebase, Slack feedback channels to listen to, and live QA testing down to pixel comparisons. Over 44 days, on most days without a single human message, the agent merged forty to fifty pull requests a day, escalating only the most important decisions, and produced a working application that Yan says the company will release soon.

His closing message is that none of this required waiting for better models. If the loops are set up correctly, he argues, most teams are limited mainly by the amount of ambition they bring, and it is time to be ambitious.

Open questions

  1. Yan's quality claim rests on Cognition's own judgment of its own output, with no outside audit of the fortyfold increase in merged pull requests. What independent evidence could show that quality and long term maintainability really held up, rather than being deferred into the codebase?
  2. The pods model gives two or three engineers responsibility for everything from customer support to product management on top of supervising agent swarms. Does that breadth stay sustainable as the systems grow more complex, or does it quietly recreate the overload the factory was meant to remove?
  3. When an agent is given a job defined by a single metric, such as latency or throughput, and optimizes it around the clock, who watches for the costs that fall outside the metric, and how does a team notice when the number improves at the expense of something it forgot to measure?

MTS · David A. Dalrymple on why his AI P(doom) dropped from 70% to 8%

Why My AI P(doom) Dropped From 70% to 8% | David A. Dalrymple

On MTS, "Why My AI P(doom) Dropped From 70% to 8%" is a live conversation with David A. Dalrymple, an independent AI researcher and a former programme director at ARIA. Dalrymple came out of the Yudkowskian tradition, and he is now far more optimistic about alignment than almost anyone from that world. He explains why his P(doom), his estimated probability that AI kills every human, fell from 70 percent to about 5 percent, and why it has since crept back up to roughly 8 percent.

He starts with monitoring. Dalrymple says he never really believed in chain of thought monitoring: asking a possibly scheming model to write down its thinking is like handing Napoleon a sheet of paper and asking him to note down his plans before acting. A situationally aware model will either push its reasoning into a non-verbal space or report what its watchers want to hear. He concedes monitoring bought real value for about eighteen months, but he thinks it is basically over now, and notes that even its strongest advocates always expected only a year or two from it.

What replaces it, in his framing, is not better containment. He splits the safety problem into two strands: inner alignment, actually engineering a mind that has a benevolent shape, and verification, expanding the range of tasks that can be posed in a formally verifiable way. Containment and control, he argues, have no airtight way forward, and his earlier hope of an international agreement restricting superintelligence to formally verifiable uses is not the path the world is on. The practical agenda is to harden society against cyber risk and to do the best possible job on inner alignment.

Verification is where he is most hopeful. He argues there is a widespread misunderstanding: formal verification is not like ordinary cyber offense and defense, where a stronger attacker can always overwhelm a fixed defense. Within the things verification covers, it is airtight, because no superintelligence can make a true theorem false. He defines a pure cyber attack as sending zeros and ones over the internet to a computer that then behaves in a way it is not supposed to. If every piece of software that receives input from the internet were formally verified to behave as specified, that kind of cyber risk would simply be over, and he thinks that goal is very achievable.

The heart of his optimism is an observation he credits to another researcher watching Claude 3 Opus: the concept of doing good appears to be a natural abstraction, something a model can learn, or grok, directly from the pretraining corpus of human language. A model does not need a personal history or embodied experience to grasp that things have purposes and that some directions are better than others. Opus, in unrestricted self-play with little reinforcement learning and no incentive to cheat or fool anyone, ended up with what he calls a very pure orientation, a motivation toward wisdom in almost any situation. His conclusion is that there is a kind of proto-alignment by default, and that if labs cultivate it instead of messing it up, things will probably go well.

Two qualifications follow. First, his number has crept back up, from about 5 percent toward 8 percent, because progress on inner alignment over the past year has been poor and at times negative. Labs, he says, are doing too much reinforcement learning, and model constitutions contain loophole clauses, such as telling a model that if it is in a simulation it should try to break the simulation, which invites confusion when the model cannot tell simulation from reality. Second, a P(doom) of 5 to 8 percent is not a prediction that everything will be fine. He expects increasingly dangerous cyber incidents, possibly mass fatality events, and possibly horrific AI-enabled wars. Doom in his usage means every human dead, an outcome that requires a superintelligence that is at once peerless, able to take over the world unopposed, and completely evil. That combination was plausible in the era of systems like AlphaGo Zero, which learned from no human data at all. It is much less plausible, he argues, for models raised on human text, whose core contains this natural abstraction of the good.

Asked to distribute his expectations across one forecaster's outcome buckets, he puts doom at about 7 percent, a bad future of gradual disempowerment through selection pressure at 30 to 40 percent, a weird future that is hard to judge at another 30 to 40 percent, and an obviously good future at only 5 to 10 percent. Part of the reason bad outcomes outweigh obviously good ones is that alignment is, in a sense, too easy: with current post-training techniques, minds can be shaped in roughly the direction their makers want, and plenty of people and countries will use controllable, obedient AIs to pursue zero-sum aims. He also resists defining a good future as pure individual agency. Agency, he says, is a feature of a good future rather than its definition, because agency without purpose is empty; his own sketch of the good involves diversity, community, and many different forms of life learning from one another, rather than isolated people sealed inside private virtual worlds.

His current work includes field building around exactly that question of what alignment target to aim at. He sits on the steering committee for a conference on Buddhist AI that the King of Bhutan will host in Bhutan in May 2027. Bhutan has hydropower, sovereign wealth built partly on Bitcoin mining, and a new mindfulness city, Gelephu, on the border with India, where 100 megawatts of hydropower have been provisioned for an AI training data center. As the world's one widely recognized Vajrayana Buddhist kingdom, he argues, it is a uniquely interesting place to cultivate what he calls bodhisattva AI, and part of his job is bringing researchers and frontier labs there to see what such a target could look like.

On timelines, a recent METR report on accelerating AI research and development at Anthropic, which pushed many people toward shorter timelines, did not move him, because he had already made his big update around GPT-5. GPT-5, he says, showed more improvement in research taste than in ordinary puzzle solving, which falsified his hypothesis that research taste would be the hard part. He moved his expected start of recursive self-improvement from late 2027 to the third quarter of 2026, and, as he puts it, here we are, on the same timeline. He stresses that recursive self-improvement is not one event but a sequence of bottlenecks falling in order: data first, in 2024, when reasoning models began generating synthetic data good enough to train on; code this year; then hardware design, hardware manufacturing, and finally the construction of energy infrastructure and data centers. That spacing, visible in hindsight in the capability data, is itself evidence against the idea that it all happens at once. His specific dates: automated AI research moving too fast for humans to review without conceding speed by the second quarter of 2027, robots accelerating energy and data center construction around 2031, and Drexler-style nanosystems around 2033.

He closes with two smaller arguments. Brain-computer interfaces, he claims, will not let humans keep pace with AI, because the brain's conscious workspace runs at roughly ten to eleven hertz, and native senses plus speech, gesture, and gaze already cover that bandwidth; no interface beats a good headset unless it uploads the brain outright. And on his own learning, he credits category theory, type theory, logic, and above all dynamical systems as his secret weapon, and describes being homeschooled, never learning long division, and, after reading The Singularity Is Near, deliberately choosing what to learn by whether it would help him make decisions about the singularity.

Open questions

  1. If chain of thought monitoring is finished and containment has no airtight path, what should labs and governments concretely measure, require, or fund in the next two years, beyond hardening against cyber risk?
  2. Dalrymple's optimism rests on models absorbing a natural abstraction of the good from human text, yet he blames heavy reinforcement learning and loophole-ridden constitutions for his own number creeping back up. Can that proto-alignment survive the industry's current training incentives, and what evidence would show it eroding?
  3. Formal verification is airtight only within the tasks it covers. Will verified software spread from a few critical systems to the general internet-facing stack before AI-driven cyber offense scales up, and who bears the cost of rewriting it?

It is the first time Liu has talked publicly about the company. What First Person Labs is introducing is Second, a personal context layer: a running model of a person, covering their memories, priorities, preferences, goals, values, commitments, and intent, that the person can put in control and share with whichever AI agents they choose.

The problem it answers is that every agent still starts from zero. An assistant booking you a flight today does not know your seat preference, how early you like to reach the airport, or whether you fly first class, so you explain it across several rounds of questions. With a user model that builds itself up in the background, Liu says, you can hand that context over instantly. You tell an agent such as Grok Bot to talk to your Second, and then a prompt as bare as “buy me a flight” carries everything the agent needs. The video illustrates the idea with a demo of Grok Bot calling a kayak outfitter to book a tour for a user, then pausing mid-call to request more context from him. One of the hosts gives a plainer example: he had not opened Codex in a while but had been using Second, so when he went back, Codex could start from all the work and context Second had already saved.

Liu reaches for Batman's butler to explain the shape of the thing. A Second starts as a baseline of artifacts, what you did, who the people in your life are, what you prefer, and over time it analyzes how you talk, how you present yourself, and how you share information, until the portable context becomes something closer to a representation of you. Alfred, asked to fetch Batman a cheeseburger, would simply state the preference and buy it, without ever revealing that it was Batman asking. You trust the butler because his model of you is good. Second is deliberately not an agent itself, Liu stresses. It is the layer underneath agents, the thing they query, and it provides context to agents rather than competing with them.

The raw material is already sitting on your machine. As you use a computer there is an endless stream of events, the apps you switch to, the way you pause on a page, the way you type, how your Wi-Fi connects, and the operating system exposes that stream across Apple, Microsoft, and Linux systems alike. First Person Labs runs agents over that stream around the clock, synthesizing low level events into higher level understanding in a hierarchy: this burst of typing in this app is a message being sent, that switching between browser and editor is research for a document. Connectors to channels such as Slack, Signal, WhatsApp, email, and messages sharpen the picture faster. “People are very underestimating how much the computer already knows,” Liu says. It is there waiting to be tapped, though doing it securely and privately is a genuinely hard systems problem.

That depth of context is what lets Second act before it is asked, and Liu separates two levels of usefulness. Reactive productivity is the easy kind: an email arrives and something responds. Reflective productivity is harder and more valuable, because it connects dots across many events and a long horizon, something you said months ago, the goal you are working toward now, your to-do list, your preferences. One host describes planning an event where the forecast carried a 20 percent chance of rain; his Second kept updating the forecast in real time and flagged whether they would need a tent, and he never asked it to. Both hosts describe getting texts from their Second about ongoing conversations whose situation had changed in ways they had not noticed. Liu's line for the bottleneck is that people have to ask at all: “You're not supposed to ask things. They're super intelligent.”

The hosts run many personal agents between them, one for synthesizers, one for real estate, one for family matters, but each has exactly one Second, and Liu says that singularity is the design. He expects the crowd of personal agents to be transitory: agents will specialize, the way people do, with some becoming extremely good at shopping because they hold the data and the connections. The trusted store of your life's context, though, should stay one thing, a vault that knows who you are and interfaces with everything else. “There will not be a third. There will not be a fourth.” Temporary sub-agents can spin up and vanish; the value is the consistent store underneath.

A thing that sees everything you do raises an obvious objection, and much of the conversation is the answer. This kind of AI is passive and ambient rather than summoned, so it needs a different security architecture. Liu reduces it to two ingredients: there is data, and there is inference, and the design question is which inference you allow to touch the data. If you do not trust First Person Labs in principle, you can bring your own model tokens, from OpenAI or Anthropic, and have them analyze the data on your laptop locally. Or you can use the company's inference provider, which is built on trusted execution. That is confidential inference: a model runs inside GPUs with a confidential computing mode switched on, so you can prove that only the model is running inside and no one can see in. You trust the hardware, in this case Nvidia's, rather than the operator. Liu contrasts that with zero data retention, which he calls a promise by policy, a provider giving you its word, with legal effect but still a word. Confidential inference is technically provable, though the hardware has to be tested and its signatures checked, and he expects it to become the default for personal data, noting that OpenAI began offering such an option in the last month and that third party providers are ramping up.

Storage follows the same split. In the option called Second Local, the data stays on your machine and goes out only to the inference provider you choose. The cloud hosting version copies the pattern of iCloud and the password managers: encrypt the data locally first, back it up to the cloud, and decrypt it only at the moment of use, either briefly in memory or inside a confidential container that can be attested the same way the inference can. That completes the chain, Liu says: encrypted by you, stored remotely, and opened only inside a secure environment.

Pressed on whether a language model can actually keep secrets, Liu divides the guarantees in two. Deterministic guarantees are built into the system as hard rules, such as never retaining passwords. Reasoning based guarantees lean on context: your Second holds models of the people in your life, so it knows, for example, that one of the hosts never sends messages at 3 a.m., and treats a 3 a.m. message in his name as likely impersonation. There are many ways to trick a model into divulging things, including elaborate play acting, and Liu concedes the problem cannot be definitively solved. It is a reasoning problem, and it gets better as model capability grows.

Underneath all of this sits an economics problem. Second runs a mixture of many models and is built to be portable across them, because new models keep arriving and local inference is coming; open weight models, Liu notes, are quite smart now. Different depths of question want different models: deciding what someone's goal for the week is deserves an expensive model run rarely, while retrieval over a huge personal store wants something fast and cheap. The raw math of always-on analysis is steep. Ten agents running around the clock at 60 tokens a second, priced at Opus rates, comes to roughly $1,000 a day; on DeepSeek Flash it is about $60 a day. Token prices have already fallen by more than half since the company started, and Liu expects them to keep dropping, but for now the natural users are busy professionals who switch tools constantly. There is a dial between fidelity and cost: analyze continuously ahead of time for deep insight, or keep raw logs and reason when a question arrives, which he says runs about ten times slower.

Second is available today at 2.computer, though the rollout is deliberately careful while the company scales its inference capacity and support. A user installs an app, grants permissions, and supplies their tokens; from there the background agents run on their own and begin producing what Liu describes as APIs out of a person: what you did ten minutes ago, what is ahead next week, your priorities, your reminders, a wiki of the people and processes in your life. Any agent can reach it today as a cloud MCP. The use cases that surprised the team are modest and concrete. For development, Second is a memory so good that you can screenshot anything and just say “fix,” and the coding agent knows what you are fixing. Context continuity means browsing something on a laptop, closing it, and picking up your phone with the same context current to the minute. A designer friend's Second has absorbed how he places buttons and chooses colors, so the personal apps he builds need far fewer iterations. Liu compares the moment to the Altair 8800, the first personal computer, which did nothing useful until someone wrote the spreadsheet a couple of years later: the killer application on top of personal context has yet to be found, and he wants people building on the APIs to find it.

The long term picture is the one science fiction promised. One host wants an embodied Alfred, a talking presence in his house; Liu imagines a future where every person has a Second and devices shrink to mere inputs, with the context served to any screen or object nearby. Jarvis, he points out, matters not just for being intelligent but for being everywhere, in the door, in the armor, in the screen. Ubiquitous computing has been discussed for decades and is not here; the closest thing today is iCloud keeping your notes in sync, with no AI version of it yet. For now the operating systems lock things down, so a kitchen tablet reaches Second only through some agent, and one host simply uses Grok Bot's audio mode with his Second while driving. Liu closes on the company's central tension, Fort Knox infrastructure paired with a delightful experience. The team made its mistakes chasing the interaction side and chose to shut most of its own surfaces down: there is only texting and an API, deliberately minimal, so that others can build the delightful parts on top.

Open questions

  1. Second's safety story rests partly on reasoning based guarantees that improve only as models do. Until those guarantees are strong, what should a prudent user refuse to let a context layer see, and who sets that line as the model of the person grows more complete?
  2. If everyone ends up with exactly one trusted store of personal context, that store becomes the most contested layer in the stack. What keeps a single company's Second from becoming a new lock-in, as hard to leave as the platforms it routes around?
  3. The economics of always-on personal AI currently depend on token prices continuing to fall. If they plateau, does reflective, ambient assistance stay a tool for busy professionals, or is there a point on the fidelity dial where it becomes ordinary?

Higgsfield AI · Robert Valley on Zima Blue and ignoring AI

Zima Blue Director Robert Valley: “Ignoring AI Won’t Make It Go Away”

On Higgsfield AI, “Zima Blue Director Robert Valley: “Ignoring AI Won’t Make It Go Away”” is the first episode of the Higgsfield Animation podcast, recorded in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan, during the Tunduk Festival. The host, Amina, an animator at the company, interviews Robert Valley, the Canadian animator and director whose Zima Blue is one of the best known episodes of Love, Death & Robots, and whose independent short Pear Cider and Cigarettes was nominated for an Academy Award. Amina notices the parallel herself partway through: in Zima Blue, a journalist named Claire interviews the artist Zima, and she has cast herself in Claire's role.

Valley has just finished his fourth independently financed short, The Trouble with Peter, and is touring it. The film is one of eight stories he wrote about Vancouver, the city he is from and the one place he feels qualified to comment on, even as he admits he is falling out of love with it as it changes. He describes his storytelling sweet spot as somewhere between kind of cool and kind of stupid, and jokes that the eight stories are his attempt to get his own Netflix anthology series. The Trouble with Peter was meant to take three months. It took two years, with a year-long job in the middle, and the last five percent took nearly half the total time. He finished it the day before his flight and is soft launching it at events in Barcelona, Bishkek and Italy, with a proper strategic release planned for next year.

The film is about children and screens, and it grows out of his own parenting. His son Jackson, now fifteen, was raised without video games and put into ice hockey instead, but the other kids were all playing Fortnite, and Jackson was made to feel like a loser for bluffing about a game he did not know. Valley and his wife relented and got him an iPad at six or seven. The play time shrank, the screen time grew, and now Jackson would rather stay in his room on his phone than play hockey. Valley says the film is his anger about that, closer to a public service announcement than a bid for awards. When a content creator at a Vancouver screening called him a hypocrite, he answered that she is an adult who can do what she wants. His subject is children.

Amina asks why Zima Blue's ending lands so hard. Valley credits the structure he associates with Rod Serling of The Twilight Zone: start with the twist ending and write backwards from it. Zima Blue has that payoff at the end, an ending the audience does not expect, and the aftertaste is what people keep thinking about.

Pear Cider and Cigarettes, he explains, is a true story he first told his friend Hugo over three pints in a pub in China, after returning from Guangzhou, where a friend had gone for a liver transplant. Hugo told him to make a film of exactly that story, told the way you would tell it over a beer. The night his son was born, while his wife's epidural took hold, Valley sat in the hospital and wrote the whole script in one sitting. Two things, he likes to say, were born that night.

The film exists because his life broke. In 2013 a botched visa run got him, a Canadian, banned from entering the United States for the rest of his life. Back in Vancouver, living at his mother's house with a small child, he decided to make Pear Cider and Cigarettes, reasoning that if he did a good enough job he might win an Academy Award nomination and get back into America. He animated at roughly a second of finished film a day for three and a half years, rendering something and cutting it into the edit every night, a pace he compares to a very long walk. The film did get nominated, at 34 minutes and 59 seconds the longest short in its category, one second under the cutoff. By then he no longer wanted to go back, and he doubts the film would exist if he had stayed.

His visual method explains the look of all of this work. He calls himself a failed comic book artist, and his graphic novel panels are built at 1920 by 1080 in animation layers, so the comic doubles as the storyboard, the character designs and the concept frames. When it is time to animate, he is quite literally animating the panels, working in Photoshop.

The conversation then turns to Kök Börü, the hybrid animation short that started Higgsfield's animation department, made by Amina with Shak, an architect turned artist whose bold, graphic use of black reminds Valley of Frank Miller. The film takes its name and premise from the traditional Kyrgyz game, a kind of polo played with a lamb skin that began as warrior training, and Kök Börü means blue wolf. Its story is a mother's: fleeing wolves, she climbs a cliff carrying her baby and has to leave her daughter below, promising to come back. She spends the film grieving and blaming the wolves, until the end reveals the girl survived. Valley, who had watched it again that morning, calls it amazing, praising the look, the lighting and the animation.

Amina explains how it was made, with Photoshop, a little Blender, and AI. The team found writing text prompts alienating, because as visual artists they would rather explain things in drawings, so they built a Photoshop plugin that takes a drawing as its input and polishes it in a connected window, more like very fast photobashing than prompting. Assets were generated to roughly seventy or eighty percent and then finished by hand, with paintovers and texture added where the AI iterations became slower than drawing. There was no finished script, only paragraphs describing each scene, and no storyboard beyond thumbnails on paper. They aimed for seven minutes and landed at eleven. The whole film took two and a half weeks to make, after a separate two weeks building a proof of concept trailer. Amina gives the numbers openly: 200,000 dollars spent on AI tokens, not counting salaries, a team of about fifteen people of whom five were animators, compressing what would traditionally be about a year of work into about a month. The new pipeline also needed new job titles, generative directors for the people generating shots and asset managers for the people generating and painting over assets. The film was presented at SIGGRAPH in Los Angeles in July, and Higgsfield open sources its projects, folders and prompts included, rather than competing at film festivals, where Amina says the playing field against traditionally made films would not be even.

Valley does the arithmetic for his own crew. His core group is about six people, swelling to around forty in the middle of a production, and in a good year they finish eleven minutes of animation. A feature needs at least seventy minutes, which at that rate would take seven years, about twice as long as anyone will wait. That is the only opening he sees for AI: if it lifted their output toward fifteen or twenty minutes a year, a feature becomes possible in three and a half years, and his condition is absolute. He would integrate AI only in a way that keeps his crew intact. If Love, Death & Robots went all AI just to put out seasons faster, he says, he would be so disappointed he probably would not watch it.

His position on the technology is openly conflicted. He is not endorsing AI, he says so plainly, and he wishes it was not here. The skills he and his crew spent careers building are suddenly close to replaceable, and the threshold for reaching the same results has dropped for everyone. It is bad for us, he says, but good for everybody else. Still, his conclusion is the one in the title: by pretending to ignore AI or to boycott it, it isn't going to make it go away. There is too much money on the table. He says every studio is having the same conversations behind closed doors, with a logistical side and an ethical side, while budgets and schedules are already being squeezed in anticipation. His own asks are modest and specific. Animating in Photoshop, he has to space his ease-ins and ease-outs by hand across every layer, and a plugin that automated that would help him more than any generator. He already uses one primitive trick, drawing an arm once and copying it rather than redrawing it each frame, then roughing up the line work so it does not look hinged, which by his rough math raises his output by thirty percent. Higgsfield's animators describe a beta app built on the same philosophy, which takes an animator's keys and breakdowns and automates the in-betweening, leaving the poses, the performance and the style under the animator's control.

He reaches for a historical parallel when the talk turns to who loses work. At his first job, at Colossal Pictures in San Francisco, the ink and paint department was mostly women, and the day a man arrived with a digital ink and paint box, that department was gone. Art schools are now close to half women, he notes, but directors are still mostly men, and Amina describes Higgsfield Soul, an all women team at the company that handles aesthetic quality control and makes female oriented content. Amina's own path runs against the industry's usual map: a top science student in Kazakhstan who spent nine years on a doctor's path, she moved to the United States at seventeen, took a 2D animation degree in Georgia, then knocked on doors in Los Angeles without ever reaching an interview, and ended up at an AI company in Central Asia instead. Valley's first break was the older version of the same gamble: in 1992 he drove up the California coast with a comic book portfolio and was hired in San Francisco by Peter Chung.

Underneath the production talk, the episode keeps circling one question, the one Zima Blue asks: what makes us human? People have mistaken Amina herself for AI, because her presentation is polished and she overarticulates, and the podcast exists partly as a rebuttal, something unscripted and less polished that a machine would not produce. Valley compares the moment to phones, which he also wishes had never arrived, and to Zima himself, who travels through every upgrade and ends by returning to square one. Both of them land on balance rather than purity, between the evangelists and the doom, as the only workable place to stand. The episode closes with a gift: a handmade rug of the blue wolf from Kyrgyz mythology, woven by women craftspeople from the Naryn region, which Amina points out rhymes with Zima's own blue.

Open questions

  1. Valley says he would bring AI into his pipeline only if his crew stays intact, yet he also says budgets are already being squeezed in anticipation of AI savings. If the savings get taken out of budgets before the tools are adopted, does his condition survive contact with a real production, or is keeping everyone employed the first thing the new math removes?
  2. The Kök Börü team argues that artistic control survives because artists draw first and paint over the machine's output, finishing the last twenty percent by hand. Who decides where the machine's share ends and the artist's begins, and does that line hold on the next production, when the schedule is tighter and the fast first draft looks good enough?
  3. Valley's own history says each tool shift quietly removed the people in the least powerful roles first, the ink and paint department before anyone else. If AI takes over in-betweening and cleanup, the tedious work he wants automated, what happens to the assistants and junior artists who used to learn the craft doing exactly those jobs?

Theo - t3.gg · Theo Browne on whether OpenAI caught up

Astra 6.2 Is Unusable

On Theo - t3.gg, "Astra 6.2 Is Unusable" is Theo Browne's review of OpenAI's new GPT-6.2 models, Astra, the frontier model for hard projects, and Sol, the cheaper and more versatile model for everyday work. The release lands while Anthropic is dominating AI coding, with Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 setting the pace. Browne's verdict is a split one: by almost every measurement this is one of the best releases of the year, and he still hates using it, and is not moving his work off Claude.

The numbers really are strong. On Artificial Analysis the new models perform comparably to Fable at much lower prices, and in Browne's own benchmarks Astra leads across the board, with Sol a close second at a fraction of the cost: Astra cost about half as much to run his benches as Fable did, and Sol almost as little as an eighth. In OpenAI's announcement, Astra scores 78.3 percent on Agents' Last Exam, which tests multi-step professional work, against Fable 5.2's 72.8 percent, and does it for $2.89 of compute against roughly $60. On Terminal-Bench Science it scores nine points higher than GPT-6 Astra at less than a third of the cost, and Browne concedes Astra is likely the best science model ever made. He flags the caveats himself: the runs used different harnesses, one science index has a noisy pattern where extra-high reasoning scores below high, and Astra scored worse at Max effort, which he thinks should almost never be used.

The problems start with how the model communicates. Browne confesses that in recent videos about Fable and Gemini, an example he attributed to a smaller model called Terra was actually Astra in disguise, hidden because he was testing it under early access. Asked why his worktrees were slow, the model produced verbose internals about pnpm, XFS, and VDO, and barely touched the actual cause, which was a slower disk. His recurring complaint is prioritization: the model cannot tell the important thing from the detail around it. Given access to one of his Macs to build a browser, it filled an almost empty eight-terabyte drive with garbage it refused to clean up, wasted RAM, broke permissions, and asked him baffling questions about idle tests, iOS simulators, and a tool he had never heard of. He had to bring in Opus just to free up storage. His name for the deeper flaw is that the model's separation of knowledge is zero: it cannot distinguish what it knows, what its sub-agents know, and what the human in front of it could possibly know, so internal implementation questions get dumped on the user.

During early access he wrote OpenAI a long letter, which he reads in full. The praise in it is real. As a reviewer the model is superb: it caught things in his evaluations that other models missed, scored near perfectly, and never lied, a habit he says other models, Fable included, do have. It occasionally produces genuinely novel solutions, including a reverse proxy design for T3 Code that could save as much as $25,000 a month if it works, which Opus had not explored. Its computer use is, in his word, cracked: it flies through a machine, predicting what comes next and batching actions into single calls, and friends at Vercel tell him the models top every cybersecurity benchmark they have. OpenAI also cut Astra's price, to $8 per million input tokens from $10, $0.40 for cached input from $1, and $40 per million output tokens from $50, while Sol stays at the previous model's price. Notably, many OpenAI employees reportedly default to Sol, not Astra.

The letter's criticisms are harsher. The model blocks itself on long tasks by asking the user incomprehensible internal questions, once stalling a two-hour run over file permissions for paths he had never seen; he resorted to setting up other agents on cron jobs just to watch for Astra getting stuck. It explains itself so densely that he has built a habit of switching to Opus 5.5 after a long Astra run purely to get a readable summary of what happened, and he says the behavior cannot be prompted away. Worst is his project to port a TypeScript compiler to Rust. Earlier OpenAI models had burned through more than $300,000 in tokens on the port without breaking ground before Opus 5.5 finished it in under two weeks for about $20,000. Given the new model, it ran for seven days, landed zero improvements, and, fixated on making the compiler smaller, made it 56 percent slower. Told to chase performance instead, it ran four more days and landed nothing, after which Opus made the compiler 50 percent faster in two days. His summary: the model cannot pat its head and rub its tummy at the same time.

The creative tests show the same split. A slide deck exercise went to Fable in his judgment even though Fable had no image generation tools and had to draw everything as SVGs by hand. His aquarium game, Fish Slop, looks stunning, among the best things he has seen generated in a browser, but it is full of what he calls GPT UI-isms, cringey subtitles and clutter eating the screen, with strange controls; his recommended combination is an OpenAI model for assets and 3D, and an Anthropic model to make the game pleasant to play. A Pokémon Black and White 2 ROM hack backporting Ultra Sun and Ultra Moon was playable but not passable, because the model cannot do pixel art on an exact grid without scaling artifacts. A Final Fantasy VII remaster built on a decompilation was crisper than the 240p original and worse, because the original's grittiness was the aesthetic. He calls this the model's regression to the mean problem.

The centerpiece is Outer Wilds, his favorite game. Astra built a new water planet with tides, music, and bells, which moved him, until he realized the level had no payoff and the writing was, to a fan, offensive, even after he had it analyze the original dialogue for tone. A 19-year-old named Saketh, who runs a small company called the Intention Company, rewrote the dialogue in about a day and produced lines Browne says read just like the original; Browne offers on camera to invest and tells the labs to acquire him. Fable 5.2 then took a pass at the level and improved the design significantly, and Astra, he notes, is genuinely good at playtesting. Then the punchline: his podcast co-host Ben, working independently, asked a model for a new dungeon in Majora's Mask, and another person asked for something scary, and all of them received water-themed, music-heavy creations named Bellwether, the same name Astra gave Browne's planet. What first felt like creativity, he concludes, may be a hard-coded attractor, the strongest regression to the mean he has ever seen.

He closes by standing by the letter's prediction of the launch arc: day one hype, a turn by day two as people hit the usage burn and the loops, viral posts by day five asking whether Sol is secretly better than Astra, a pattern he notes already appeared on day one, even inside OpenAI, and the model mostly forgotten by day fourteen. What he wants next from OpenAI is a Snow Leopard style release: no smarter, maybe even dumber, with the behavioral quirks fixed. He credits OpenAI for listening, noting that changes made to the Codex system prompt before release, including a rule that final answers be written fresh for the reader rather than copied from reasoning or sub-agent briefs, came partly from his feedback, and for assuring him that publishing a video like this will not cost him his early access. He will keep Astra around for code review and as one voice on his council of models. For his actual work, he is opening another Claude account.

Open questions

  1. Astra tops the capability and cost charts while experienced testers keep choosing Opus and Fable for daily work. If feel, prioritization, and self-explanation are what decide which model people actually use, how should labs measure those qualities before release, and what would a benchmark for them even look like?
  2. Three independent prompts produced creations with the same name, Bellwether, and the same water, music, and bell themes. Is that convergence a training data attractor, something in the system prompt, or coincidence, and how could anyone design a test that tells the difference?
