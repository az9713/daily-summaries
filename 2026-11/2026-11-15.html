Turing and Turing Now - Science & Tech News · Gennady Korotkevich, the competitive programmer AI cannot beat

Even AI Can't Beat This Man at Programming

On Turing, “Even AI Can't Beat This Man at Programming” tells the story of Gennady Korotkevich, the Belarusian competitive programmer known online as “tourist,” whose dominance of his sport grew so complete that its biggest platform had to invent a new rank and name it after him. The AI angle in the title only arrives near the end. Most of the video is the record itself, and the record is the argument.

Competitive programming is a sport in which contestants solve hard programming and mathematics problems as fast as possible. Its biggest platform, Codeforces, rates players with a system similar to chess. A rating of 3,000 is considered extraordinary: millions of programmers exist, fewer than 100 have ever reached it, and those who do earn the rank of Legendary Grandmaster. On August 30, 2024, Korotkevich became the first person to break 4,000, reaching 4,009. Codeforces created a new rank above Legendary Grandmaster for scores over 4,000 and called it “Tourist,” with a badge that reverses the usual colors, a red first letter and the rest in black.

Korotkevich was born on September 25, 1994, in Gomel, in southeastern Belarus. His parents, Vladimir and Lyudmila Korotkevich, worked as programmers in the mathematics department at Francysk Skaryna Gomel State University. He started coding at about six, after watching his parents at their computers and asking how the text on the screen controlled the machine. By eight he had outgrown what they could teach him, and they turned to their colleague Mikhail Dolinsky, who had started the regional school of informatics. Dolinsky gave him a small computing book, heard nothing for two months, and then was shown a notebook full of code: the boy had spent the summer finishing every assignment. In second grade he entered a national programming contest meant for much older students and placed in the top 50. At about nine, needing a username, he saw the word “tourist” on his skis and took it.

The International Olympiad in Informatics, the top contest for high school students, normally crowns eighteen-year-olds. Korotkevich made the Belarusian team in 2006 at only 11, breaking the age record by a wide margin, and finished 26th in the world with a silver medal. He was disappointed, having expected gold. He won his first gold in 2007 at 12, another in 2008, and finished first overall in 2009, 2010, and 2011. At the 2011 event in Thailand he solved every problem for a perfect score. He closed his school career in 2012 with one more gold and a second-place finish, for six golds and one silver in all. No one else has matched that record. Asked about the secret of his success, he said: “I try various strategies, and one of them is the right one,” and “I am no genius. I am simply good at it.” He also kept his practice to no more than three to four hours a day and made time for football and table tennis.

In the fall of 2012 he moved to St. Petersburg to study at ITMO University, where coach Andrey Stankevich had built a program that treated competitive programming with the seriousness of professional sport. The collegiate contest, the ICPC, is a team event: three students share one computer for five hours and work through about a dozen problems, so communication and planning matter as much as coding speed. In 2013 his team, with Mikhail Kever and Niyaz Nigmatullin, beat schools including Shanghai Jiao Tong University and the University of Tokyo to win the world championship. In 2015, in Marrakesh, he won again with Artem Vasilyev and Borys Minaiev, and that ITMO team solved all 13 problems.

The open division has no age limits, and its tournaments are run by major tech firms under very different rules. Google Code Jam rewards algorithmic optimization, Meta Hacker Cup tests work with huge datasets, and Topcoder splits into 90-minute Algorithm sprints and multi-day Marathon matches. In 2014 Korotkevich completed a Grand Slam, winning Code Jam, Hacker Cup, Yandex Algorithm, the Russian Code Cup, and the Topcoder Open Algorithm championship in a single year. He won Code Jam eight times, including an unbroken streak from 2014 to 2020, a sixth place in 2021, and the final edition in 2022. His Hacker Cup titles came in 2014, 2015, 2019, 2020, 2023, and 2025, and he also won the Topcoder Open Marathon matches in 2018 and 2019. His longtime rival was Petr Mitrichev; later challengers included Andrew He of the United States and Kevin Sun of Canada.

The Codeforces ladder helps explain why 4,000 stood untouched for so long. Ratings are relative: your score moves according to how you perform against the field, so near the top, where everyone already knows the standard algorithms, gaining even a few points means finishing near the top every time, and a single mistake can wipe out months of effort. Since Korotkevich crossed the line, only one person has followed him. In January 2025, the Chinese prodigy Lingyu “jiangly” Jiang also passed 4,000, reaching 4,039.

His method, as competitors describe it, is subtraction. Reading a problem, he ignores the decorative story, the farmers dividing fields or the spaceships crossing asteroid fields, and goes straight to the core mathematics. Like a chess grandmaster, he draws on a large mental library of patterns and recognizes in moments what a beginner might need an hour to see. Andrew He has studied footage of him competing: Korotkevich works entirely in a terminal, using the text-based Far Manager, a sparse C++ template, and rapid keystrokes, with no elaborate interface or heavy debugging tools. At the 2014 Meta Hacker Cup finals, Mark Gordon finished coding well ahead of him, only for Korotkevich to submit in the closing minutes, glance at the updated scoreboard, and flash Gordon a small smirk. Two of Gordon's submissions turned out to have bugs, and he did not even finish second. Egor Suvorov, who has competed alongside Korotkevich, credits his calm under time pressure, which lets him debug faster and find simpler, shorter solutions.

Offstage he is unassuming. Winning matters to him, he has said, because contest results are completely objective: a program either runs correctly inside the time limit or it fails, with no committee deciding. He travels simply, often with one backpack, collected fridge magnets from host cities for himself and his mother, and for years chose long train rides between St. Petersburg and Gomel over flying. In 2017 he confirmed that he had turned down highly lucrative recruitment offers from both Google and Yandex, living comfortably on prize money of $10,000 to $20,000 per win and keeping his independence. Alongside the sport he finished a master's degree and began a PhD under Alexey Sergushichev in bioinformatics, co-authoring a highly cited 2019 paper on FGSEA, a fast method for gene set enrichment analysis.

That is where the title's AI thread finally enters. In 2023 he briefly joined Ramp, a financial technology company, as a software engineer, using fine-tuned large language models to automate tasks such as organizing receipt data with optical character recognition. In December 2024 he joined Cognition AI in San Francisco. Cognition's founders, Scott Wu, Steven Hao, and Walden Yan, are all IOI gold medalists, and the company built Devin, an autonomous AI software engineer meant to plan, write, run, debug, and deploy whole projects. Korotkevich was hired to provide the best possible human benchmark: he studies how AI models reason, improves their debugging methods, and trains the networks to handle tough problems the way he does. The video names the irony plainly. The man who spent two decades marking the upper limits of human cognition is now building the machinery designed to make human coding obsolete. It has not cost him his edge. In 2025, while working on Devin's intelligence, he took a break, returned to competition, and won the Meta Hacker Cup again. Just like that.

Open questions

  1. Lingyu Jiang has already followed Korotkevich past 4,000, reaching 4,039 in January 2025. Does the “Tourist” rank stay a one-person honor, or does it quietly become a tier, and what happens to its meaning if a third and fourth programmer cross the line?
  2. Korotkevich now spends his working days at Cognition training AI systems to reason and debug the way he does. Can a model actually absorb the pattern recognition and calm decomposition that made him dominant, or does his edge rest on intuition that does not transfer into training data?
  3. He protected his independence for two decades, turning down Google and Yandex to live on prize money. As the sport's top champions move into AI labs, does competitive programming keep producing singular figures like him, or does the talent pipeline now end at a job offer?

Every · Dan Shipper on the future of great human work after automation

A New Vision for Human Work After Automation

On Every, "A New Vision for Human Work After Automation" is Dan Shipper's Thesis 27 talk, his closing address to Every's own conference, built around one question: what is the future of great human work after automation? Shipper, Every's co-founder and chief executive, argues that the answer depends on how you try to predict the future. There are two methods, he says. The aggregate gathers lots of data and extrapolates a trend line. The intimate zooms in on one real case, with all its messiness, and extrapolates from there. Our culture emphasizes the aggregate, because it feels objective and quantitative, while the intimate can look anecdotal. But the aggregate, he argues, misses things, and you can only see what is missing up close.

The aggregate case looks grim for human work. He points to GDPval, an OpenAI benchmark that measures how well models perform economically valuable tasks drawn from real jobs. Experts turn their own tasks into detailed prompts, a model and a human working without AI each produce an answer, and blind experts pick the better one. In 2024, with GPT-4o, the AI won 12.4 percent of the time. In 2025 it won 47.6 percent. In 2026, with GPT-5.4, it won about 83 percent of the time. Shipper says he can see the same shift in his own week. In the cracks of his calendar he analyzed the company's quarterly customer survey in ways that once required a data scientist, built a live video game for the team all-hands, spotted an opportunity to sell Every to teams, and vibe coded his own CRM to run outreach. In 2020 terms, he says, that is a data scientist, a game developer, a product marketer, an engineer, and a business development rep, perhaps two hundred thousand dollars of work, and he got it as part of his Claude subscription. He calls the feeling this produces an agency rupture: we identify ourselves with the work we do, and watching a model do that work strikes at our sense of self.

Then he turns to the intimate. If the aggregate view were the whole story, he asks, why does Every still employ thirty people? He zooms in on that same customer survey. Everyone on the team had access to the same data and the same Claude, yet different people found very different things. Yash, the go-to-market engineer, who is a monomaniac about growth, found that many first-time subscribers churn after the first month because Every sends too much email, and wrote the code change to fix it. Douglas, the head of marketing, found something else entirely: many readers feel lonely, surrounded by colleagues who think their enthusiasm for AI is strange, and come to Every as the one place that takes it seriously. Jack, a senior editor, turned the results into a claymation family feud game show. Ask Claude the default question, what do you see in the data, and it answers that the writing is the business, the bundle is what makes people stick, and the risk is too much volume. All true, Shipper says, but everyone now has access to that answer, so it is cheap, and not one person on his team submitted anything like it.

His name for the underlying shift is that generation has become cheap while ranking stays hard. He cites an experiment by Mike Taylor, Every's head of evals, who tried to predict what Shipper would say in Slack. Claude could do it, in a sense: given four hundred tries, one of its answers matched what Shipper actually said 93 percent of the time. But it could not rank its own answers. Asked to put the real one at the top, it landed in the top ten percent only about 60 percent of the time, close to a coin flip. A human on a team, by contrast, carries a responsibility for one particular area of the world, which narrows where they look, and a perspective, which lights up the few things in that area worth acting on. Millions of paths run through any dataset. Responsibility narrows the search, perspective does the ranking, and the person picks one path and takes ownership of it. A team multiplies that: each member explores and ranks a different part of the possibility space, which is far more powerful than any default answer.

Shipper then takes on the obvious objection: models will get better at ranking, so give it a year and the humans are redundant again. The comparison, he says, is rigged on both sides. Pitting the 2026 version of Yash against a 2027 model freezes Yash in place, when in reality the 2027 Yash will be using the 2027 model, with a year of new experience behind him. And the supposedly unaided model on the other side had to be set up by someone: there is no immaculate conception of an agent. Put the model on both sides of the equation and it cancels out. What the comparison really asks is whether you would rather have a person with a responsibility and a perspective using the model, or the model's default prompt. He picks the person every time.

That is the core of what he calls the allocation economy, an idea he first wrote about in 2024: you will not be judged on how much you know, but on how well you can allocate and manage the resources of intelligence, assigning work to models and making sure it gets done well. Because the model is distributed to everyone, its default capability raises the floor for all, and default answers, however impressive they look today, become the baseline rather than an advantage. The scarce thing, the alpha, is the perspective and responsibility a person uses to push the model somewhere non-obvious. That advantage gets absorbed into tomorrow's floor as capabilities rise, but the person is not frozen in place either: they are using the new model too.

This flips how AI progress should be measured, he argues. Today we measure models by consensus: performance on problems everyone agrees are worth solving, like math and programming, precisely because those answers can be validated. But consensus capability is exactly what gets commoditized and handed to everyone. The value now sits in non-consensus perspective, so the better question is how good a model is at doing your work, from your perspective. The aggregate has to freeze humans at some fixed year to make its comparisons, which is why its charts make human work look headed for zero. The intimate shows something else: the same rising line, with human perspective riding on top of it. The audience, he closes, did not come to the conference because a capability chart told them everything they needed to know. They came to get close to the work. Now, he says, it is their job to spread that understanding to the rest of the world.

Open questions

  1. If ranking is the scarce human skill today, what happens to Shipper's argument in the specific domains where models do learn to rank as well as the responsible expert, and how would anyone notice that threshold being crossed from inside a team?
  2. The allocation economy rewards perspective and responsibility, but those are unevenly distributed and hard to hire for: does this future widen the gap between people with a developed point of view and everyone else, and what closes it?
  3. If AI should be measured by how well it does one person's work from that person's perspective, what would such a benchmark even look like, and could it avoid collapsing back into the consensus measures it is meant to replace?

The Rest Is Science · Hannah Fry and Michael Stevens with Michael's sealed puzzle

The Secrets Michael Keeps From Himself

On The Rest Is Science, “The Secrets Michael Keeps From Himself” is an episode of Field Notes, the show and tell series hosted by Professor Hannah Fry and Michael Stevens of Vsauce. The theme is things Michael owns but has never experienced. Each star attraction stays firmly sealed, and Fry spends the episode trying, and failing, to get him to open one.

The first item is a three color pentomino puzzle made by Raymond Hou of Physics Fun. A pentomino is a flat shape made of five squares, and the pieces, in yellow, green, and blue, have to fit into a rectangular frame with no two pieces of the same color touching. The catch is that the puzzle is sold already solved, sealed in transparent wrapping with the solution facing out. Michael has kept it upside down on his desk for years, because one glance would lodge in his memory and spoil the challenge forever. He turns it over with his eyes closed so the camera can see what he never has. Fry points out that he could simply tip the pieces into a bag and store them loose. He concedes the point, blames a mental trap he calls ironic process theory, the harder you tell yourself not to remember something, the more permanently you remember it, and floats the idea of finally solving it on a flight to Los Angeles that night.

The second item is not visual at all. It is a blister pack labeled Comet Vomit, holding a small vial of chemicals blended to reproduce the smell of a comet's tail, the gases and ices blown off a comet by the sun, whose composition scientists worked out after landing instruments on comets. Michael bought it at a museum, the Freaky But True Peculiarium. He bought two. He opened and smelled the first one on camera for his D!NG channel, and this second vial stays sealed precisely because the experience is spent: once opened, it outgasses and never works again. He cannot even remember what the first one smelled like, and he is content not to know. He might let his daughter open this one someday.

The third item is a can of vegetables that was used on screen in the film Wet Hot American Summer, the movie whose talking can of vegetables was voiced by H. Jon Benjamin. Someone who worked on the films and the Netflix series gave it to Michael, and it is the only screen used movie prop he owns. It has never been opened. Its best by date reads December 2015, and it has changed in his hands: it feels strangely light now, it no longer sloshes, and there is corrosion and what looks like a small hole in the lid. He smells the hole for Fry and reports rust and metal, like blood. Fry jokes that ants may have been dining inside. Michael says he might open it on his deathbed, or perhaps film its destruction in 2035, when it turns twenty. Then he makes one concession. If a comment on this video reaches five million likes, more than the most liked comment on YouTube, he will open the can on camera and eat some of it.

Fry counters with a sealed thing of her own: a good bottle of rum she was given for her twenty first birthday, which she decided on the spot to save until she turns fifty. The pair reflect that what counts as a special bottle changes with age, and that Fry, unlike Michael, at least agreed terms with herself in advance. His objects just sit there, unused and tempting, with no occasion attached.

The episode then turns to listener questions. Nora writes that she woke from spine surgery having memorized the dialogue of a two and a half hour film she had seen once, the night before, and asks whether people can wake from anesthesia with abilities they did not have. Fry is skeptical but intrigued. Her explanation is that the brain records far more than it lets through, and anesthetic does not switch the brain off and on in one step: different systems come back online at different speeds, a state doctors call emergence delirium, so buried material can briefly surface unfiltered. She cites foreign language syndrome, a documented phenomenon with only about nine cases in the medical literature, including a seventeen year old Dutch boy who woke from a knee operation in 2022 unable to speak Dutch, speaking only English and convinced he was in America, with his Dutch returning after about a day, and a man in his seventies who, asked to count to thirty after an anesthetic, switched halfway into Hindi he had picked up during the Second World War. The consistent point is that nobody learns anything new under anesthesia. The information was already in there.

The last question, from Annabel, asks which everyday item has stayed roughly the same for the longest time. Michael's answer is the container. Working from Ursula K. Le Guin's carrier bag theory of fiction, which he has not read but knows through Marilyn French's book on Ulysses, he argues that before the spear and the sharp edge came gathering, and gathering needs something to carry things in: a leaf, twigs and vines, a hide, a bucket. Carrying more than you can hold meant food could be brought home and stored, which freed hours of the day, and free hours are where society, laws, currency, and everything else came from. Archaeologists, he notes, have found evidence of containers in use half a million years ago, and because early containers were made of perishable materials, the true starting point is probably unknowable. Fry adds a running joke from her household, dating events to somewhere between the invention of the bucket and Lizzo's second album, and Michael happily declares that era closed: we are contained within the next one.

Open questions

  1. Will a comment on the video ever reach five million likes, and if the can is opened on camera, will what is inside settle whether anything edible was ever in it?
  2. If emergence delirium can briefly surface buried memories and languages, could waking patients be studied systematically in that short window, or is the phenomenon too rare and too brief to ever be more than case reports?
  3. If the container really predates the tools archaeology can recover, is there any evidence that could ever date its invention, or will the oldest technology remain the one that left nothing behind?
