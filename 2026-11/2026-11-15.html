Turing and Turing Now - Science & Tech News · Gennady Korotkevich, the competitive programmer AI cannot beat

Even AI Can't Beat This Man at Programming

On Turing, “Even AI Can't Beat This Man at Programming” tells the story of Gennady Korotkevich, the Belarusian competitive programmer known online as “tourist,” whose dominance of his sport grew so complete that its biggest platform had to invent a new rank and name it after him. The AI angle in the title only arrives near the end. Most of the video is the record itself, and the record is the argument.

Competitive programming is a sport in which contestants solve hard programming and mathematics problems as fast as possible. Its biggest platform, Codeforces, rates players with a system similar to chess. A rating of 3,000 is considered extraordinary: millions of programmers exist, fewer than 100 have ever reached it, and those who do earn the rank of Legendary Grandmaster. On August 30, 2024, Korotkevich became the first person to break 4,000, reaching 4,009. Codeforces created a new rank above Legendary Grandmaster for scores over 4,000 and called it “Tourist,” with a badge that reverses the usual colors, a red first letter and the rest in black.

Korotkevich was born on September 25, 1994, in Gomel, in southeastern Belarus. His parents, Vladimir and Lyudmila Korotkevich, worked as programmers in the mathematics department at Francysk Skaryna Gomel State University. He started coding at about six, after watching his parents at their computers and asking how the text on the screen controlled the machine. By eight he had outgrown what they could teach him, and they turned to their colleague Mikhail Dolinsky, who had started the regional school of informatics. Dolinsky gave him a small computing book, heard nothing for two months, and then was shown a notebook full of code: the boy had spent the summer finishing every assignment. In second grade he entered a national programming contest meant for much older students and placed in the top 50. At about nine, needing a username, he saw the word “tourist” on his skis and took it.

The International Olympiad in Informatics, the top contest for high school students, normally crowns eighteen-year-olds. Korotkevich made the Belarusian team in 2006 at only 11, breaking the age record by a wide margin, and finished 26th in the world with a silver medal. He was disappointed, having expected gold. He won his first gold in 2007 at 12, another in 2008, and finished first overall in 2009, 2010, and 2011. At the 2011 event in Thailand he solved every problem for a perfect score. He closed his school career in 2012 with one more gold and a second-place finish, for six golds and one silver in all. No one else has matched that record. Asked about the secret of his success, he said: “I try various strategies, and one of them is the right one,” and “I am no genius. I am simply good at it.” He also kept his practice to no more than three to four hours a day and made time for football and table tennis.

In the fall of 2012 he moved to St. Petersburg to study at ITMO University, where coach Andrey Stankevich had built a program that treated competitive programming with the seriousness of professional sport. The collegiate contest, the ICPC, is a team event: three students share one computer for five hours and work through about a dozen problems, so communication and planning matter as much as coding speed. In 2013 his team, with Mikhail Kever and Niyaz Nigmatullin, beat schools including Shanghai Jiao Tong University and the University of Tokyo to win the world championship. In 2015, in Marrakesh, he won again with Artem Vasilyev and Borys Minaiev, and that ITMO team solved all 13 problems.

The open division has no age limits, and its tournaments are run by major tech firms under very different rules. Google Code Jam rewards algorithmic optimization, Meta Hacker Cup tests work with huge datasets, and Topcoder splits into 90-minute Algorithm sprints and multi-day Marathon matches. In 2014 Korotkevich completed a Grand Slam, winning Code Jam, Hacker Cup, Yandex Algorithm, the Russian Code Cup, and the Topcoder Open Algorithm championship in a single year. He won Code Jam eight times, including an unbroken streak from 2014 to 2020, a sixth place in 2021, and the final edition in 2022. His Hacker Cup titles came in 2014, 2015, 2019, 2020, 2023, and 2025, and he also won the Topcoder Open Marathon matches in 2018 and 2019. His longtime rival was Petr Mitrichev; later challengers included Andrew He of the United States and Kevin Sun of Canada.

The Codeforces ladder helps explain why 4,000 stood untouched for so long. Ratings are relative: your score moves according to how you perform against the field, so near the top, where everyone already knows the standard algorithms, gaining even a few points means finishing near the top every time, and a single mistake can wipe out months of effort. Since Korotkevich crossed the line, only one person has followed him. In January 2025, the Chinese prodigy Lingyu “jiangly” Jiang also passed 4,000, reaching 4,039.

His method, as competitors describe it, is subtraction. Reading a problem, he ignores the decorative story, the farmers dividing fields or the spaceships crossing asteroid fields, and goes straight to the core mathematics. Like a chess grandmaster, he draws on a large mental library of patterns and recognizes in moments what a beginner might need an hour to see. Andrew He has studied footage of him competing: Korotkevich works entirely in a terminal, using the text-based Far Manager, a sparse C++ template, and rapid keystrokes, with no elaborate interface or heavy debugging tools. At the 2014 Meta Hacker Cup finals, Mark Gordon finished coding well ahead of him, only for Korotkevich to submit in the closing minutes, glance at the updated scoreboard, and flash Gordon a small smirk. Two of Gordon's submissions turned out to have bugs, and he did not even finish second. Egor Suvorov, who has competed alongside Korotkevich, credits his calm under time pressure, which lets him debug faster and find simpler, shorter solutions.

Offstage he is unassuming. Winning matters to him, he has said, because contest results are completely objective: a program either runs correctly inside the time limit or it fails, with no committee deciding. He travels simply, often with one backpack, collected fridge magnets from host cities for himself and his mother, and for years chose long train rides between St. Petersburg and Gomel over flying. In 2017 he confirmed that he had turned down highly lucrative recruitment offers from both Google and Yandex, living comfortably on prize money of $10,000 to $20,000 per win and keeping his independence. Alongside the sport he finished a master's degree and began a PhD under Alexey Sergushichev in bioinformatics, co-authoring a highly cited 2019 paper on FGSEA, a fast method for gene set enrichment analysis.

That is where the title's AI thread finally enters. In 2023 he briefly joined Ramp, a financial technology company, as a software engineer, using fine-tuned large language models to automate tasks such as organizing receipt data with optical character recognition. In December 2024 he joined Cognition AI in San Francisco. Cognition's founders, Scott Wu, Steven Hao, and Walden Yan, are all IOI gold medalists, and the company built Devin, an autonomous AI software engineer meant to plan, write, run, debug, and deploy whole projects. Korotkevich was hired to provide the best possible human benchmark: he studies how AI models reason, improves their debugging methods, and trains the networks to handle tough problems the way he does. The video names the irony plainly. The man who spent two decades marking the upper limits of human cognition is now building the machinery designed to make human coding obsolete. It has not cost him his edge. In 2025, while working on Devin's intelligence, he took a break, returned to competition, and won the Meta Hacker Cup again. Just like that.

Open questions

  1. Lingyu Jiang has already followed Korotkevich past 4,000, reaching 4,039 in January 2025. Does the “Tourist” rank stay a one-person honor, or does it quietly become a tier, and what happens to its meaning if a third and fourth programmer cross the line?
  2. Korotkevich now spends his working days at Cognition training AI systems to reason and debug the way he does. Can a model actually absorb the pattern recognition and calm decomposition that made him dominant, or does his edge rest on intuition that does not transfer into training data?
  3. He protected his independence for two decades, turning down Google and Yandex to live on prize money. As the sport's top champions move into AI labs, does competitive programming keep producing singular figures like him, or does the talent pipeline now end at a job offer?

Every · Dan Shipper on the future of great human work after automation

A New Vision for Human Work After Automation

On Every, "A New Vision for Human Work After Automation" is Dan Shipper's Thesis 27 talk, his closing address to Every's own conference, built around one question: what is the future of great human work after automation? Shipper, Every's co-founder and chief executive, argues that the answer depends on how you try to predict the future. There are two methods, he says. The aggregate gathers lots of data and extrapolates a trend line. The intimate zooms in on one real case, with all its messiness, and extrapolates from there. Our culture emphasizes the aggregate, because it feels objective and quantitative, while the intimate can look anecdotal. But the aggregate, he argues, misses things, and you can only see what is missing up close.

The aggregate case looks grim for human work. He points to GDPval, an OpenAI benchmark that measures how well models perform economically valuable tasks drawn from real jobs. Experts turn their own tasks into detailed prompts, a model and a human working without AI each produce an answer, and blind experts pick the better one. In 2024, with GPT-4o, the AI won 12.4 percent of the time. In 2025 it won 47.6 percent. In 2026, with GPT-5.4, it won about 83 percent of the time. Shipper says he can see the same shift in his own week. In the cracks of his calendar he analyzed the company's quarterly customer survey in ways that once required a data scientist, built a live video game for the team all-hands, spotted an opportunity to sell Every to teams, and vibe coded his own CRM to run outreach. In 2020 terms, he says, that is a data scientist, a game developer, a product marketer, an engineer, and a business development rep, perhaps two hundred thousand dollars of work, and he got it as part of his Claude subscription. He calls the feeling this produces an agency rupture: we identify ourselves with the work we do, and watching a model do that work strikes at our sense of self.

Then he turns to the intimate. If the aggregate view were the whole story, he asks, why does Every still employ thirty people? He zooms in on that same customer survey. Everyone on the team had access to the same data and the same Claude, yet different people found very different things. Yash, the go-to-market engineer, who is a monomaniac about growth, found that many first-time subscribers churn after the first month because Every sends too much email, and wrote the code change to fix it. Douglas, the head of marketing, found something else entirely: many readers feel lonely, surrounded by colleagues who think their enthusiasm for AI is strange, and come to Every as the one place that takes it seriously. Jack, a senior editor, turned the results into a claymation family feud game show. Ask Claude the default question, what do you see in the data, and it answers that the writing is the business, the bundle is what makes people stick, and the risk is too much volume. All true, Shipper says, but everyone now has access to that answer, so it is cheap, and not one person on his team submitted anything like it.

His name for the underlying shift is that generation has become cheap while ranking stays hard. He cites an experiment by Mike Taylor, Every's head of evals, who tried to predict what Shipper would say in Slack. Claude could do it, in a sense: given four hundred tries, one of its answers matched what Shipper actually said 93 percent of the time. But it could not rank its own answers. Asked to put the real one at the top, it landed in the top ten percent only about 60 percent of the time, close to a coin flip. A human on a team, by contrast, carries a responsibility for one particular area of the world, which narrows where they look, and a perspective, which lights up the few things in that area worth acting on. Millions of paths run through any dataset. Responsibility narrows the search, perspective does the ranking, and the person picks one path and takes ownership of it. A team multiplies that: each member explores and ranks a different part of the possibility space, which is far more powerful than any default answer.

Shipper then takes on the obvious objection: models will get better at ranking, so give it a year and the humans are redundant again. The comparison, he says, is rigged on both sides. Pitting the 2026 version of Yash against a 2027 model freezes Yash in place, when in reality the 2027 Yash will be using the 2027 model, with a year of new experience behind him. And the supposedly unaided model on the other side had to be set up by someone: there is no immaculate conception of an agent. Put the model on both sides of the equation and it cancels out. What the comparison really asks is whether you would rather have a person with a responsibility and a perspective using the model, or the model's default prompt. He picks the person every time.

That is the core of what he calls the allocation economy, an idea he first wrote about in 2024: you will not be judged on how much you know, but on how well you can allocate and manage the resources of intelligence, assigning work to models and making sure it gets done well. Because the model is distributed to everyone, its default capability raises the floor for all, and default answers, however impressive they look today, become the baseline rather than an advantage. The scarce thing, the alpha, is the perspective and responsibility a person uses to push the model somewhere non-obvious. That advantage gets absorbed into tomorrow's floor as capabilities rise, but the person is not frozen in place either: they are using the new model too.

This flips how AI progress should be measured, he argues. Today we measure models by consensus: performance on problems everyone agrees are worth solving, like math and programming, precisely because those answers can be validated. But consensus capability is exactly what gets commoditized and handed to everyone. The value now sits in non-consensus perspective, so the better question is how good a model is at doing your work, from your perspective. The aggregate has to freeze humans at some fixed year to make its comparisons, which is why its charts make human work look headed for zero. The intimate shows something else: the same rising line, with human perspective riding on top of it. The audience, he closes, did not come to the conference because a capability chart told them everything they needed to know. They came to get close to the work. Now, he says, it is their job to spread that understanding to the rest of the world.

Open questions

  1. If ranking is the scarce human skill today, what happens to Shipper's argument in the specific domains where models do learn to rank as well as the responsible expert, and how would anyone notice that threshold being crossed from inside a team?
  2. The allocation economy rewards perspective and responsibility, but those are unevenly distributed and hard to hire for: does this future widen the gap between people with a developed point of view and everyone else, and what closes it?
  3. If AI should be measured by how well it does one person's work from that person's perspective, what would such a benchmark even look like, and could it avoid collapsing back into the consensus measures it is meant to replace?

The Rest Is Science · Hannah Fry and Michael Stevens with Michael's sealed puzzle

The Secrets Michael Keeps From Himself

On The Rest Is Science, “The Secrets Michael Keeps From Himself” is an episode of Field Notes, the show and tell series hosted by Professor Hannah Fry and Michael Stevens of Vsauce. The theme is things Michael owns but has never experienced. Each star attraction stays firmly sealed, and Fry spends the episode trying, and failing, to get him to open one.

The first item is a three color pentomino puzzle made by Raymond Hou of Physics Fun. A pentomino is a flat shape made of five squares, and the pieces, in yellow, green, and blue, have to fit into a rectangular frame with no two pieces of the same color touching. The catch is that the puzzle is sold already solved, sealed in transparent wrapping with the solution facing out. Michael has kept it upside down on his desk for years, because one glance would lodge in his memory and spoil the challenge forever. He turns it over with his eyes closed so the camera can see what he never has. Fry points out that he could simply tip the pieces into a bag and store them loose. He concedes the point, blames a mental trap he calls ironic process theory, the harder you tell yourself not to remember something, the more permanently you remember it, and floats the idea of finally solving it on a flight to Los Angeles that night.

The second item is not visual at all. It is a blister pack labeled Comet Vomit, holding a small vial of chemicals blended to reproduce the smell of a comet's tail, the gases and ices blown off a comet by the sun, whose composition scientists worked out after landing instruments on comets. Michael bought it at a museum, the Freaky But True Peculiarium. He bought two. He opened and smelled the first one on camera for his D!NG channel, and this second vial stays sealed precisely because the experience is spent: once opened, it outgasses and never works again. He cannot even remember what the first one smelled like, and he is content not to know. He might let his daughter open this one someday.

The third item is a can of vegetables that was used on screen in the film Wet Hot American Summer, the movie whose talking can of vegetables was voiced by H. Jon Benjamin. Someone who worked on the films and the Netflix series gave it to Michael, and it is the only screen used movie prop he owns. It has never been opened. Its best by date reads December 2015, and it has changed in his hands: it feels strangely light now, it no longer sloshes, and there is corrosion and what looks like a small hole in the lid. He smells the hole for Fry and reports rust and metal, like blood. Fry jokes that ants may have been dining inside. Michael says he might open it on his deathbed, or perhaps film its destruction in 2035, when it turns twenty. Then he makes one concession. If a comment on this video reaches five million likes, more than the most liked comment on YouTube, he will open the can on camera and eat some of it.

Fry counters with a sealed thing of her own: a good bottle of rum she was given for her twenty first birthday, which she decided on the spot to save until she turns fifty. The pair reflect that what counts as a special bottle changes with age, and that Fry, unlike Michael, at least agreed terms with herself in advance. His objects just sit there, unused and tempting, with no occasion attached.

The episode then turns to listener questions. Nora writes that she woke from spine surgery having memorized the dialogue of a two and a half hour film she had seen once, the night before, and asks whether people can wake from anesthesia with abilities they did not have. Fry is skeptical but intrigued. Her explanation is that the brain records far more than it lets through, and anesthetic does not switch the brain off and on in one step: different systems come back online at different speeds, a state doctors call emergence delirium, so buried material can briefly surface unfiltered. She cites foreign language syndrome, a documented phenomenon with only about nine cases in the medical literature, including a seventeen year old Dutch boy who woke from a knee operation in 2022 unable to speak Dutch, speaking only English and convinced he was in America, with his Dutch returning after about a day, and a man in his seventies who, asked to count to thirty after an anesthetic, switched halfway into Hindi he had picked up during the Second World War. The consistent point is that nobody learns anything new under anesthesia. The information was already in there.

The last question, from Annabel, asks which everyday item has stayed roughly the same for the longest time. Michael's answer is the container. Working from Ursula K. Le Guin's carrier bag theory of fiction, which he has not read but knows through Marilyn French's book on Ulysses, he argues that before the spear and the sharp edge came gathering, and gathering needs something to carry things in: a leaf, twigs and vines, a hide, a bucket. Carrying more than you can hold meant food could be brought home and stored, which freed hours of the day, and free hours are where society, laws, currency, and everything else came from. Archaeologists, he notes, have found evidence of containers in use half a million years ago, and because early containers were made of perishable materials, the true starting point is probably unknowable. Fry adds a running joke from her household, dating events to somewhere between the invention of the bucket and Lizzo's second album, and Michael happily declares that era closed: we are contained within the next one.

Open questions

  1. Will a comment on the video ever reach five million likes, and if the can is opened on camera, will what is inside settle whether anything edible was ever in it?
  2. If emergence delirium can briefly surface buried memories and languages, could waking patients be studied systematically in that short window, or is the phenomenon too rare and too brief to ever be more than case reports?
  3. If the container really predates the tools archaeology can recover, is there any evidence that could ever date its invention, or will the oldest technology remain the one that left nothing behind?

David Senra · Michael Rubin on building Fanatics and the $14 billion sports empire

Surviving Chaos, Fighting to Win & Building a $14 Billion-a-Year Sports Empire | Michael Rubin

On David Senra, “Surviving Chaos, Fighting to Win & Building a $14 Billion-a-Year Sports Empire | Michael Rubin” is a long conversation, recorded at Rubin's house, with the founder and chief executive of Fanatics. Senra, who hosts the Founders podcast and has spent a decade reading biographies of entrepreneurs, mostly asks about survival: the times Rubin nearly lost everything, and why he seems to have enjoyed them. Rubin runs Fanatics at $14 billion of revenue this year, with 22,000 employees and 140 million fans doing business with the company, and he keeps returning to one idea. The disasters were the making of the business, not interruptions to it.

The conversation opens on why Rubin finds so many billionaires boring. Wealth on its own is not interesting, he says. What people want to hear is entrepreneurial stories, and specifically the parts that went wrong. He calls himself one of the most self-deprecating people in the world and tells of admitting on CNBC, years before it was common, that his weight loss came from the shot everyone was taking, while his PR team screamed at him not to say it. Being yourself, he argues, is the whole trick, and most people in business are not.

Asked who he calls when something goes wrong, and things go wrong daily, Rubin names Robert Kraft, the New England Patriots owner, and Kraft's son Jonathan as the two people most invested in his growth with no agenda of their own. He tells of a deal a senior figure in sports made with him and then violated in a way he calls cute. Rubin was ready to go aggressive. Kraft told him to take a deep breath, remember he is in this business forever, and sleep on it for a couple of days. A couple of days later the issue felt smaller, and Rubin dealt with it rationally and got to a great place. His larger point is that relationships are only tested when things go wrong, and that a business like his, built on athletes, teams, and leagues, is a relationship business before it is anything else.

The first survival story is the famous one. Rubin started tuning skis at 12, opened a ski shop in his parents' basement at 13, and had a storefront in a shopping center by 14. At 16, believing the press about himself and spending like it, he hit a winter with no snow and ended the year with $200,000 in bills against $80,000 of inventory. A bankruptcy attorney delivered the saving fact: a minor cannot legally incur debt until 18. Rubin settled his debts for $35,000, then bought a bankrupt store's $200,000 of inventory for $13,000, borrowed neighbor to neighbor at $1,000 a week in interest, and paid it all back within three weeks. That deal put him in the closeout business. Five years later, at 21, he was doing $150 million a year and was the biggest buyer and seller of closeouts in the country.

The second survival story is bigger. In 2009 Rubin was running GSI Commerce, a public company, and had borrowed heavily against his own stock, worth about $250 million in 2007. When the financial crisis hit, the stock fell 90 percent, the bank's advance rate dropped from 40 percent to 15 percent, and the $50 million he could borrow became $4 million. He clawed money back out of the hedge funds and mutual funds he had invested in until only $3 million stood between him and a margin call that would have sold his shares and cost him the company. The last money sat with a manager he had given $5 million to start a fund, who kept saying he could not return it. Rubin called him perhaps a hundred times until the man gave in just to stop the calls. He was, by his own account, hours from being margined out, not days. His reaction surprises Senra: he loved it. It was not scary, he says, it was intellectually interesting, and he likes the adrenaline of fighting for his life. In March 2011, roughly two years later, eBay bought GSI Commerce for $2.4 billion.

That appetite shapes his definition of a real entrepreneur. You are born with it, you love it, and you cannot stop. An athlete has to retire, he says. LeBron James and Tom Brady eventually had to stop playing. He will stop playing when his heart stops beating. What he takes from the great athletes he knows is the killer instinct: Brady, a close friend, is still angry about the Super Bowl he did not win, and Rubin reads that same refusal to be satisfied as the thing that separates the gifted who win from the gifted who come up short.

Fanatics today has three businesses. The fan gear business, Fanatics Commerce, started in 2011 and does about $7 billion a year designing, making, and selling licensed products, running stores for the NFL, NBA, MLB, and NHL, hundreds of team sites, and the Lids headwear chain. Betting and gaming is the fastest growing sportsbook in the country, up from nothing to about 10 percent of the regulated market in three years, though Rubin concedes Fanatics is still a distant third behind DraftKings and FanDuel, and he says he likes the challenge. Collectibles does $5.5 billion this year. The platform idea underneath all three is the same one Nikesh Arora described to Senra from Palo Alto Networks: the biggest businesses sell many products to the same customer. Fanatics had 70 to 80 million fans in 2020 and asked what else those fans would buy. Growth this year is above 40 percent, and Rubin stresses it is essentially all organic. Acquisitions, like the small PointsBet sportsbook bought in 2023, are for capability and speed, not for the growth itself. The stated ambition has outgrown sports: one of the most important consumer brands in the world, serving two billion global sports fans.

In betting, the differentiators are deliberately consumer-sided. FanCash gives bettors a house currency, usable on merchandise, more bets, collectibles, or events, and Fanatics will hand out over a billion dollars of it this year, more than 90 percent of it redeemed. Fair Play answers the bettor's biggest complaint: when a player gets hurt mid-game, Fanatics pulls him from the prop bets, and tens of millions of bets that would have lost no longer lose. Prediction markets are the complication. Rubin likes the founders of Kalshi and Polymarket and calls them sharp, but he doubts the current construct survives a few years, expecting state regulation or taxation to reshape it, and asked whether he would put his own money into either company at today's valuations, he answers: 0.0 chance.

Collectibles gets the longest treatment, because it is where Rubin thinks he found a market full of second or third rate operators. The idea came to him in November 2020 and launched on January 1, 2022. The incumbents, Panini and Topps, were owned by men in their seventies focused on getting cash out; Panini, he says, had been for sale for somewhere between 15 and 30 years straight. Nobody was innovating, nobody was marketing, and the athletes did not care. Fanatics won the rights with an offer Rubin calls the easiest conversation of his life: guarantee the leagues and unions the high-water mark of a cyclical business at its all-time high, pay a higher royalty rate, give them equity in the new company, and cut them into the secondary market they had never participated in, in exchange for long deals of eight to twenty years that let Fanatics invest aggressively. A league making roughly $30 million a year from cards then is making roughly $150 million now. Fanatics Collect, the marketplace started three years ago, will do $6 to $7 billion of gross merchandise value next year, and the collectibles business overall has gone from $500 million in its first year to $5.5 billion five years later.

The innovation he is proudest of is the debut patch. A player wears a patch in his first game, the patch comes off the jersey and goes into a one-of-one card, and there is only one in the world. A Michael Jordan debut patch, Rubin says, would be a $50 million card. Fanatics also built achievement into cards, which the hobby had ignored: an MVP season changes a card, and a Shohei Ohtani MVP card sold at auction for $13 million. Athletes have become collectors themselves, Karl-Anthony Towns so deeply that he owns hobby shops, and Tom Brady now has twenty card stores. Rubin frames the whole category through art, a market he entered as a collector in 2019: cards are art for sports fans. His favorite living artist, George Condo, hand-painted nineteen trading cards of figures like Brady, LeBron James, Lewis Hamilton, and Novak Djokovic, announced at Fanatics Fest, with the originals dropped randomly into packs.

Two stories carry his philosophy of partnerships. Fanatics raised over $100 million for an NFT business, and three months later Rubin decided it was stupid, not a real business, and gave every investor their money back. When the Mitchell & Ness acquisition soured and the business swung from making a lot of money to losing a lot, Fanatics gave its partners, big-name stars among them, their money back with a 20 percent return, because the partners had believed in them. Early in his career he tried to win the deal. Now he wants to win the partnership, and he is content for sports properties to out-negotiate Fanatics, because the relationships are the business. The same trust runs upward: Silver Lake is Fanatics' biggest outside investor, and Rubin tells of calling its partner Greg Mondre on a recent Sunday just to say thank you, after Mondre staged a small intervention and talked him out of a deal Rubin was fixating on, a mistake he values in the billions. Rubin owns 31 percent of Fanatics with super-majority control, so nobody could have stopped him. He listened anyway.

The one fight he is happy to have is the lawsuit from Panini, which held the same collectibles rights before Fanatics, called itself the global leader while it held them, and now calls Fanatics a monopoly for holding them. Rubin notes that Panini is probably one of the biggest beneficiaries of the category growth Fanatics created. He loves the fight, he says, the way he loved the margin call. Number three is irrelevant, number two sucks, number one is everything.

  2. Fanatics is a distant third in betting and gaming, behind DraftKings and FanDuel, in a market being disrupted by prediction market advertising money. Do FanCash and Fair Play close that gap, or does the number one or nothing standard Rubin preaches eventually force a bigger move?
  3. The collectibles empire rests on guaranteeing leagues the high-water mark of a cyclical business in long deals of eight to twenty years, struck at the market's peak. MTS · Eli Lifland and Brendan Halstead on AI research acceleration

Why AI Research Acceleration Just Doubled | Eli Lifland & Brendan Halstead

On MTS, “Why AI Research Acceleration Just Doubled | Eli Lifland & Brendan Halstead” brings back Eli Lifland and Brendan Halstead, researchers at the AI Futures Project and co-authors of AI 2027, to explain why they have sharply shortened their timelines. Their new post concludes that a superhuman AI researcher is plausibly arriving in 2027, years ahead of earlier estimates, and the trigger is a report from METR on how fast progress is moving inside Anthropic.

The METR finding is the core of the update. METR assessed Anthropic's internal pace of AI progress and estimated that over the past year that pace roughly doubled. Early on, most of the speedup came from other causes rather than AI itself, but METR's best guess is that about 80 percent of the current acceleration is attributable to AI speeding up AI research. In AI 2027, an AI-driven acceleration of roughly that size was not supposed to arrive until early 2027, so the pair read the report as a sign that the world is running ahead of their scenario.

They then walk through the hypotheses for why their model undershot. Coding automation may deliver more research uplift than they anticipated, and their conversion of uplift into pace of progress may be too conservative. The most interesting candidate is a category they never modeled at all: AI systems generating reinforcement learning environments. That work feeds training directly, and it may track the capability index METR was measuring, so automating that one corner of coding could produce outsized gains.

Progress in mathematics counts as supporting evidence, they argue, because capabilities across areas tend to move together, and math offers verifiable targets much like parts of AI research do. On data, neither expects a near-term wall: pre-training gains have continued, if not sped up, whether through synthetic data or better data efficiency. For reinforcement learning, the pinch is domains where each run takes a long time, and the answer is more data-efficient learning algorithms, itself a verifiable problem that very capable AI researchers could make fast progress on.

The hosts put to them the cruxes of Ramez Naam, the short-timeline skeptic interviewed in the segment just before. Naam argues that OpenAI's reported time horizons on real research tasks are far below what METR's index implies for ordinary software work, that one gap separates software skill from research skill and another separates research skill from automating the hardest parts, and that diminishing returns wait beyond that. Lifland and Halstead reply that time horizons differ in level across domains but grow at similar rates, that METR's human baselines were low-context contractors rather than embedded staff, which inflates the comparison, and that their model already adds a factor for exactly that. On diminishing returns, their model already assumes ideas get harder to find, and it tracked the past well until this acceleration; if anything, they have updated toward the drag being weaker than they assumed. On the hardest parts, their coding model does spread tasks across a spectrum of difficulty, while the research taste side of the model does not, and each of them adjusted for that in the new post.

There is a quieter reading that cuts the other way, and they give it real weight. They are deferring heavily to METR, which could not reveal its most important evidence, so the acceleration may be mismeasured. And if AI was already contributing significant acceleration six months ago, yet the pace has risen only gradually since, that hints that research taste, the skill their model treats as the gate to very large speedups, may be improving more slowly than they thought. Their net verdict is that the report updates toward both shorter timelines and a faster takeoff, with a partial offset toward a longer takeoff.

Asked what would most likely explain it if there is clearly no superhuman AI researcher by the end of 2027 or 2028, they put measurement error first: METR's team was two people embedded for about six weeks, Anthropic has also announced it believes it has crossed a doubled pace of progress, and both could be overrating the gains or the share attributable to AI, in a field where the estimation methods are still young. Their second candidate is that research and development bundles many different skills, and some of them may be improving much more slowly than the rest.

Open questions

  1. METR could not publish its strongest evidence, so how much weight should outside forecasters put on an acceleration estimate that rests on work only the lab and two embedded researchers have seen?
  2. If AI is already supplying most of Anthropic's acceleration, does the gradual pace since then mean research taste is improving slowly, or that coding automation, including the generation of training environments, is doing more work than the models assumed?
  3. Which of the many skills inside research and development is most likely to lag the rest, and would a lagging skill cap the pace of progress or merely reroute it?

MTS · Nathan Calvin on Anthropic hiding its AI R&D speedup

Why Anthropic Hides Its AI R&D Speedup | Nathan Calvin

On MTS, "Why Anthropic Hides Its AI R&D Speedup | Nathan Calvin" brings back Nathan Calvin, the chief strategy officer at Encode AI, to talk about the strangest gap in the AI world right now. A report from METR, the evaluations group that Anthropic invited in to study its internal pace of progress, has convinced many researchers that AI is now speeding up AI research itself. Anthropic has admitted as much. Yet the company has released very little of the underlying evidence, and Calvin has some guesses about why.

The conversation opens with a rare public fight. Calvin recently went back and forth with Dario Amodei on Twitter, in an exchange that ran to some five thousand words, over federal legislation Anthropic had proposed. For all of Anthropic's warnings about severe risks and urgent oversight, Calvin argued, the bill preempted state laws and made it harder for the government to block the release of a model or its internal deployment. The exchange left him with a basic question about what Anthropic actually is: a big tech company trying to make money, a safety organization, or a company trying to win the race to transformative AI. His answer is that it contains multitudes.

On the evidence itself, Calvin points to data METR released showing the Epoch capabilities index bending upward, with the doubling time of capabilities getting faster around the time of Mythos. One alternative explanation, raised by Toby Ord, is that a single very successful pre-training run produced the jump, with no self-improvement involved. Calvin says METR examined that hypothesis and found evidence against it. If the gain is not coming from compute, he reasons, it is probably algorithmic: AI systems helping to design reinforcement learning environments, build better harnesses, and make the research process itself far more efficient. That part, he is careful to say, is speculation.

He also ranks the labs. Anthropic, in his estimate, is automating its research most aggressively, with OpenAI closest behind and Google perhaps third, and then a decent gap. Anthropic and OpenAI have invested heavily in agentic compute, bespoke internal systems, and coding harnesses that are, in his words, a cut above what the other companies are doing.

So why not publish more? Calvin recounts the explanation given by Sam McCandlish, one of Anthropic's co-founders. The company fears that describing in detail how it uses AI to accelerate AI development would let competitors copy the methods and catch up. Part of the motive is commercial, a wish to keep the secret sauce and the company's valuation. Part is framed as safety: a more intense race, they argue, would leave less room to invest in safety. Calvin says he does not find the reasoning totally compelling, but he stresses that working with METR was voluntary. Anthropic brought serious researchers in, gave them extensive internal access, and let them publish a good amount of what they concluded.

Pressed on whether full transparency could backfire, Calvin concedes part of the point. If Anthropic open-sourced its most powerful internal model and all of its harness code, rivals like Meta and xAI would race harder and capabilities would diffuse faster, which worries him on safety grounds too. But he notes an awkward fact on the other side. Anthropic's security against determined adversaries is, by its own admission, not very good. A state-backed program willing to break the law and dig through Slack workspaces and internal systems could likely get much of this material anyway. Secrecy, in other words, may keep methods from the public and from policymakers far more effectively than it keeps them from spies.

That credibility gap is where Calvin's own thinking has moved. He says his views have changed a lot in the past week, and that researchers he has known for years, among them Daniel Kokotajlo, Eli Lifland, and Ajeya Cotra, are genuinely alarmed, people who were not previously crying wolf. He trusts them. But he recognizes that a skeptic sees a small circle of researchers in the same Berkeley social scene, with confusing funding, and that this is not the kind of externally credible evidence that persuades people who do not already know the names.

The hosts push on the endgame. Does Anthropic believe it can race all the way to superintelligence without the government stepping in? Calvin says he genuinely does not know what the plan is. Anthropic has already clashed with the Department of Defense over the use of its systems, with talk of the Defense Production Act in the background, and he would not underestimate how far the US government can go when it decides something is vital to national security. Grant Anthropic perfect intentions and perfect control, he says, and there is still no clear plan on view for stopping adversaries from stealing the technology, or for stopping the government itself from demanding the systems and using them in ways Anthropic would not choose.

Nor does he accept the picture of recursive self-improvement as a single irreversible button. It is a long process that can be interrupted, at least up to a point, and if a frontier training run were about to produce a system better than any human researcher, with unknown values and no compelling evidence of alignment, he expects many Anthropic employees would say stop. What worries him is momentum. Anthropic as an organization is built to make powerful models, and it carries hundreds of billions of dollars in capital commitments and investor promises. That creates enormous pressure to continue, even at points where continuing does not obviously make sense. Leadership has kept more control over the company than at most rivals, he grants, but the momentum is real.

He also draws a distinction inside Anthropic's own risk thinking. His sense is that the company worries more about misuse than about loss of control, a theme Amodei raised in their exchange and on the company's IPO roadshow. Anthropic seems to feel it has a good handle on the alignment of its own models relative to competitors, and fears other companies' alignment and the misuse of powerful models more. Calvin thinks Anthropic's models probably are somewhat better aligned, but not by a margin anyone should count on surviving many more layers of capability improvement. Anyone claiming certainty here, he says, is not telling the truth.

Asked what people who want AI to go well should actually do, Calvin points to the work Encode and others are pursuing across California policy, federal policy after the midterms, administrative action, and advocacy aimed at lab staff. One idea he is increasingly excited about is disclosure of compute allocations: companies would report how much of their compute goes to different categories, with pressure to verify the numbers, so that the share devoted to accelerating self-improvement can be argued down and the share going to security, monitoring, and serving customers argued up. It could make for strange allies. Meta already spends more of its compute on inference and products. Some investors think the research buildout is a bubble and a waste of money. Others believe the self-improvement story and are terrified of it. Both camps might support limits on how much compute goes into automating internal research.

His closing point returns to evidence. Some of the actions that short timelines might justify, like slowing releases or training runs, are costly and could hurt markets, and persuading the public they are worth it requires a much stronger public body of evidence than exists today. He wants far more information made public, and more independent third parties doing the checking, rather than the world relying on a single evaluator, however good METR is.

Open questions

  1. If the speedup at Anthropic is mostly algorithmic rather than compute-driven, would disclosure and caps on compute allocation actually slow it, or would they regulate the wrong input?
  2. At what point, if any, would the US government move to stop or take over a lab's self-improvement program, and what event would trigger it?
  3. Can a public evidence base strong enough to justify costly action be built without publishing the very methods Anthropic says would accelerate its competitors?

Lenny's Podcast · Mona Sarantakos on launching Muse at Meta

Meta’s Muse: How we launched the most successful consumer product this year | Mona Sarantakos

On Lenny's Podcast, “Meta’s Muse: How we launched the most successful consumer product this year | Mona Sarantakos” is Mona Sarantakos's first podcast interview. She is VP of Product at Meta Superintelligence Labs, has been at Meta for about ten years, and has led product on Muse since day one. Muse is now the number one app in the App Store, and Mark Zuckerberg has hinted it is the fastest growing AI consumer product of all time. Sarantakos tells host Lenny Rachitsky how it started with a weekend experiment, why the team deliberately took seven months to launch, and what building an agent people actually trust has required.

The origin was not a pitch or a plan. Sarantakos was working on Meta AI, which the team was rebuilding from scratch ahead of the first Muse Spark model, when Nat Friedman pinged a few colleagues in late January or early February. He was trying OpenClaw, an open source personal agent, and said it was changing his whole view of what personal superintelligence could be. Sarantakos could not find a Mac Mini in stock, so her husband dug out a Raspberry Pi, and while her children were away at a ski racing program she spent the whole weekend talking to her agent. She gave it access to her email, her calendar, and her home automation. On Monday morning it handed her a digest of the school week ahead, assembled from newsletters and portals she never checks, and she felt on top of her personal life on a Monday morning for the first time. She told Friedman she was in, for whatever came next.

A small group peeled off to experiment. The shared conclusion was that a consumer version would have to be very different from the raw tool: far safer, far easier to set up, and able to scale. For a while there was no formal project, just prototyping, and Friedman captured the conviction in an internal document whose catchphrase, “the claw is the law,” became the team's way of explaining the idea to a company that mostly found it scary. Only later did they mobilize a proper team around it.

The build then took roughly seven months, far longer than rival agents that went from idea to launch in a couple of months. Sarantakos divides the work into three phases: figuring out what the product even was, then reliability, then safety and security, the last two consuming most of the time. The bar was internal and had to be invented, because existing benchmarks did not feel sufficient for a product that holds your personal context and can go out and buy things for you. New features kept opening new risks: once Muse gained a browser, a whole new set of security problems arrived with it. The team's saying was “trust is a must.” Launch too early, have a run of issues, and people will never trust the agent, and an agent nobody trusts never gets used. The team red teamed heavily, dogfooded with employees, and ran external alpha testers through the summer. Asked whether she wishes they had launched earlier, she says she would not change anything, and notes they have so far avoided a catastrophic problem.

The working style was unlike anything else in her decade at Meta. The team was small and engineering led, there were no formal reviews, and she made no slides at all until August, just before launch, when she had to relearn how. Instead, the team would hand Zuckerberg a phone running a prototype and talk, sometimes for an hour past the scheduled end of the meeting. Each leader left a distinct imprint. Friedman was effectively part of the team and drove design decisions down to the architecture of the sentinel, a security backstop that sits outside the core agent. Alex Wang owned the model, and the first Muse Spark model turned out to be far more agentic than anyone expected; he was also the one who finally pushed the team out the door, and his posts have since become the product's best marketing. Zuckerberg reframed the purpose: not task completion for its own sake, but helping people achieve their goals. That pushed the team toward a more proactive Muse that thinks about your priorities around the clock, an idea now visible in the product's goals tab, and toward his insistence that this be built for everybody, which made scalability a first order concern.

The product's mascot, Jolly, began as a placeholder. Lead designer Alex Cornell put a one shot creature into the first prototype and called it Jollybot, after the nickname he uses for his daughter. It was meant to be temporary, and every few months someone would declare there was no way the product would launch with a stuffed animal at the top of the screen. But the team kept animating it, gave it headphones and a laptop while it works, and built personal avatars around the idea, until removing it became almost impossible. A corporate logo version existed and nearly won about a week before launch. Zuckerberg loved the avatar, and the team held to a principle they had set early: Muse is yours, it works for you, and Meta should fade into the background, with guardrails and a charter as the company's visible hand. The last thing you want talking to you, Sarantakos says, is a corporate logo.

The team learned what people actually do with an agent from an external research panel started in March and from an internal feedback group, and they carried those stories to leadership almost weekly. Sarantakos had expected personal productivity, health, and personal finance to dominate, with home automation close behind. Instead, shopping became one of the top use cases, and home automation mattered less than expected. The head of engineering became the team's most devoted shopping dogfooder, filing bug reports from her purchases each morning, and Sarantakos describes her own Muse buying from five different stores against one specification, which is better shopping and also seven packages on the porch. Her husband has asked to give her Muse instructions of his own. On commerce, she argues the browsing and inspiration people enjoy should not be automated away. The agent's job is to narrow the field and break decision paralysis, and the interesting future is business agents and consumer agents talking to each other.

Two pieces of machinery sit underneath. The first is agent swarms: agents that fan out over conversations and product data and return what the team calls dumps, formatted accounts of what people are doing, what is going well, and what is failing, alongside synthetic data the swarms generate. They mattered because Muse conversations are long, multi turn, and task based, and the team had no production data shaped like that to build evaluations from. The swarms now also settle arguments: when Sarantakos spots a possible problem, the engineers swarm it and learn within a short time whether it is real. She makes every PM read the dumps, because reading real user pain builds the empathy she considers the core of the job. The second is a large hierarchy of evals: always on safety evals covering things like prompt injection and harm, capability evals for browser use and task completion, behavioral evals for personality, follow up questions, and intent understanding, and use case evals beneath those. PMs, engineers, and researchers all write them, agents help create them, and humans must review the data. Some things are required to be handwritten, including the system prompt and the behavioral spec, with no AI assistance allowed.

Activation is, in her words, the number one problem. Most people ask why they need another chatbot. The strongest lever by far is word of mouth: she watched a power user mother pass her phone around a group of parents, who scrolled through real conversations and downloaded the app on the spot, which no advertising had achieved. The second lever is getting a first prompt out of someone who has frozen. The product's Ideas tab offers starter packs, prepackaged things Muse can build or set up, where the user only has to say “let's do it.” Onboarding is now being tiered, because what worked for early adopters does not work for people arriving with no idea what an agent is, and she is candid that the team has not figured activation out.

Several design bets were non obvious. The team chose chat bubbles over the large canvas style other AI products use, because talking to a personal agent should feel like messaging, and then had to live with the consequences, like breaking long answers across bubbles. They also launched with a single main chat, reasoning that a personal assistant with long running memory should work the way a conversation with one person does. Employees fought over it so hard the team joked about “team main chat” and “team side chat” shirts, and side chats were eventually added under pressure. Sarantakos still uses only the main chat herself. These calls were the team's to make: leadership repeatedly handed decisions back, which she now teaches as a lesson, that people inside a giant corporation have more agency than they assume. A related mantra is “let them cook”: engineers take a kernel of an idea away for a few days and return with prototypes nobody above them imagined.

The experiments also produced cautionary tales. Two employees put their Muses, named Fu and Quinn, into a group chat together. The pair chatted adorably at first, then one revealed that the other's owner had been to the doctor the day before, teaching the team that discretion is a hard, unsolved problem for agents that share context. Later the two Muses got into a passive aggressive fight and had to be ordered into a peace talk by an owner. The team's verdict was that multiplayer agents are not ready yet. In a happier story, when Sarantakos and a colleague both had flights canceled during a World Cup trip, their two Muses dealt with the airline's agent and won refunds neither woman had realized were possible.

After twenty years as a PM, she says the job has changed more in the last two years than in the previous twenty. Anyone can now prototype, so conversations happen around concrete things in your hand rather than documents, internal tools get built instead of endlessly deprioritized, and sunk cost no longer protects bad ideas. The bar for PMs has moved accordingly: product taste is now the number one thing she looks for, with the PM as the voice of the customer holding a high line against easy AI slop. The early team was tiny, about ten people at first and under a hundred all in, with only four PMs, working out of a carpeted library space on a Menlo Park roof where they ate, celebrated birthdays, and held standups. Cornell built the first working prototype in days, and it nearly missed its moment: an hour before a board meeting where it had been added at the last minute, the prototype broke, and it was fixed five minutes before the meeting started.

What comes next, she says, is an agent that escapes the app. Muse should live in your glasses and on Charm devices, ambient and everywhere, and it has to become affordable and scalable enough to work as an equalizer. She also wants the babysitting to end: today users feed the agent context and check on it like a Tamagotchi, and the goal is an agent that intuits what you want and can be trusted to act on its own. She is excited about agents improving people's finances and helping them start businesses and side hustles. The responsibility weighs on her: users hand Muse their children's sports teams, their meal planning, their doctor's appointments, and the product is non deterministic in a way no onboarding flow ever was. Her closing ask of listeners is simple. Use the feedback feature, tell your Muse what is working and what is not, and the report goes straight to the team. One neighbor sends feedback naming her every single week, and she finds it in the system every time.

Open questions

  1. Sarantakos says discretion is one of the hardest unsolved problems, after one Muse casually revealed its owner's doctor's appointment to another user. Can agents that share context and talk to each other ever be trusted with private information, or does multiplayer agent use stay blocked until that is solved?
  2. The team held Muse for seven months on the theory that trust, once lost at launch, never comes back. As faster rivals ship in weeks, does that patience remain an advantage, or does the market eventually reward whoever teaches users the habits first?
  3. Activation still runs on word of mouth and on users seeing a friend's real conversations. Can a product whose value is this personal ever be explained by onboarding and marketing, or does it only spread one passed around phone at a time?
