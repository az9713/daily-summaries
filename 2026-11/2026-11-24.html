AI Cyberattacks Have Jumped 5x in the Last Year | Wiz

On MTS, "AI Cyberattacks Have Jumped 5x in the Last Year | Wiz" is a conversation with Gal Nagli and Alon Schindel, security researchers at Wiz, the cloud security company Google acquired in 2026. They describe working with Google DeepMind to train Gemini 4 for autonomous penetration testing, and they argue that frontier models now outperform the best human security experts at offensive security, in speed and in creativity alike. The consequence, on their account, is a surge in AI-driven attacks: Wiz sees four to five times the number of incidents it saw in the same period last year, and most of those attacks are based on AI models and automated attack infrastructure.

The DeepMind collaboration pairs Wiz's security expertise with DeepMind's model training. The two teams picked what the guests describe as the only public evaluation for penetration testing, and the latest Gemini model, running in Wiz's harness, beat every other model there by a wide margin. In practice, they say, you point the model at a website, give it a little context inside the harness, and it finds critical vulnerabilities from the outside, the kind anyone could exploit, entirely on its own. Wiz uses the setup with design partners and strategic customers, large enterprises and SaaS companies, to stress test products and simulate attackers, and the guests say the jump in capability with each model version has been huge.

Asked whether current frontier models are at the level of the best human experts, Nagli says they are better. He has earned more than three million dollars in bug bounties over the years, yet he no longer opens an HTTP proxy or any of his old tools. Instead he steers a swarm of agents running the latest Gemini models, with a human still in the loop. Schindel adds that skill is only part of it: the other part is scale, and scale is what attackers now have.

That is where the incident numbers come from. Attackers use the same models and harnesses to target companies, some of them AI companies, some sprayed widely, and they operate at a speed no human can match. An AI-run attack, the guests say, can fully compromise an organization in twenty minutes, and not only at the entry point: the lateral movement, the data exfiltration, and the operation inside the organization all run at machine speed too. However good models or humans get, humans simply cannot compete with that pace.

It is not only pace. Nagli describes running a swarm of thirty Gemini agents against a very large application for ten hours. Only in the ninth hour did the swarm chain eight different leads and bugs into a single exploit, in a category he says he did not even know existed. Some exploits, like the PHP to shell case they discuss, are close to impossible for a human to find at all: the chaining and the level of detail exceed what a person can hold together. AI does in hours what might have taken months of research, and the sophistication itself is new. That, they argue, is why so many vendors now report the same vulnerabilities: everyone is using AI-based discovery tools, and nobody can work as a vulnerability researcher today without them.

The guests also push back on where the industry focuses. A recent moment, as they put it, concentrated attention on code security, but they believe the real risk on the internet is public websites and public servers, the easiest way into an organization. Wiz runs a "scan for good" initiative that attacks such sites defensively, including government entities, and they argue that protecting the public surface with AI is where the worry and the effort belong.

Their advice to companies follows directly. If you are not testing your public assets with offensive AI today, start: if you do not scan your external attack surface with AI red team tools, the attackers will, and they describe campaigns hitting dozens of organizations at once from hundreds of IP addresses. Nagli offers an anecdote. Two years ago his overnight bug bounty automation woke him with code execution on a bank. Run the same thing today, he says, and by morning it would not stop at initial access: it would have exfiltrated the bank's entire cloud environment. Just as important is the fix side. A codebase has to be patchable faster than before; waiting three days, or a week, is no longer an option, and a company that cannot fix issues at the new speed risks being put out of business before it patches.

The host closes by asking why, if agents are this capable, the hacking Americans experience has not obviously exploded. Schindel answers that some of it is hidden: a number of recent outages are indirectly caused by attacks, because organizations took systems down to patch their environments. More than twenty AI inference providers, he says, were hacked in the past few weeks, and the major incident response firms are at capacity and cannot take new customers. Nagli adds the timeline. AI showed glimpses of autonomous hacking as early as November of last year, and the capability was there months ago, but the last month is when it skyrocketed: the harnesses got better, the methodology got better, and cheap open models got better at hacking. His word for the result is a democratization of hacking. Interesting times, he calls them.

Open questions

  1. Nagli says frontier models are now better than the best human penetration testers, based largely on one public evaluation and Wiz's own harness. What independent test would settle that claim, and who should run it?
  2. If an AI-run attack can compromise an organization in about twenty minutes, what has to change in defenses built around patch cycles of days or weeks, and can smaller companies afford the new pace?
  3. Wiz scans public and government websites "for good," and urges every company to attack its own surface before criminals do. Who authorizes that kind of scanning, and what separates defensive use of these tools from the same tools aimed at someone else's systems?
