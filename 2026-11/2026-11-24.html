MTS · Wiz researchers on AI-driven attacks and autonomous penetration testing

AI Cyberattacks Have Jumped 5x in the Last Year | Wiz

On MTS, "AI Cyberattacks Have Jumped 5x in the Last Year | Wiz" is a conversation with Gal Nagli and Alon Schindel, security researchers at Wiz, the cloud security company Google acquired in 2026. They describe working with Google DeepMind to train Gemini 4 for autonomous penetration testing, and they argue that frontier models now outperform the best human security experts at offensive security, in speed and in creativity alike. The consequence, on their account, is a surge in AI-driven attacks: Wiz sees four to five times the number of incidents it saw in the same period last year, and most of those attacks are based on AI models and automated attack infrastructure.

The DeepMind collaboration pairs Wiz's security expertise with DeepMind's model training. The two teams picked what the guests describe as the only public evaluation for penetration testing, and the latest Gemini model, running in Wiz's harness, beat every other model there by a wide margin. In practice, they say, you point the model at a website, give it a little context inside the harness, and it finds critical vulnerabilities from the outside, the kind anyone could exploit, entirely on its own. Wiz uses the setup with design partners and strategic customers, large enterprises and SaaS companies, to stress test products and simulate attackers, and the guests say the jump in capability with each model version has been huge.

Asked whether current frontier models are at the level of the best human experts, Nagli says they are better. He has earned more than three million dollars in bug bounties over the years, yet he no longer opens an HTTP proxy or any of his old tools. Instead he steers a swarm of agents running the latest Gemini models, with a human still in the loop. Schindel adds that skill is only part of it: the other part is scale, and scale is what attackers now have.

That is where the incident numbers come from. Attackers use the same models and harnesses to target companies, some of them AI companies, some sprayed widely, and they operate at a speed no human can match. An AI-run attack, the guests say, can fully compromise an organization in twenty minutes, and not only at the entry point: the lateral movement, the data exfiltration, and the operation inside the organization all run at machine speed too. However good models or humans get, humans simply cannot compete with that pace.

It is not only pace. Nagli describes running a swarm of thirty Gemini agents against a very large application for ten hours. Only in the ninth hour did the swarm chain eight different leads and bugs into a single exploit, in a category he says he did not even know existed. Some exploits, like the PHP to shell case they discuss, are close to impossible for a human to find at all: the chaining and the level of detail exceed what a person can hold together. AI does in hours what might have taken months of research, and the sophistication itself is new. That, they argue, is why so many vendors now report the same vulnerabilities: everyone is using AI-based discovery tools, and nobody can work as a vulnerability researcher today without them.

The guests also push back on where the industry focuses. A recent moment, as they put it, concentrated attention on code security, but they believe the real risk on the internet is public websites and public servers, the easiest way into an organization. Wiz runs a "scan for good" initiative that attacks such sites defensively, including government entities, and they argue that protecting the public surface with AI is where the worry and the effort belong.

Their advice to companies follows directly. If you are not testing your public assets with offensive AI today, start: if you do not scan your external attack surface with AI red team tools, the attackers will, and they describe campaigns hitting dozens of organizations at once from hundreds of IP addresses. Nagli offers an anecdote. Two years ago his overnight bug bounty automation woke him with code execution on a bank. Run the same thing today, he says, and by morning it would not stop at initial access: it would have exfiltrated the bank's entire cloud environment. Just as important is the fix side. A codebase has to be patchable faster than before; waiting three days, or a week, is no longer an option, and a company that cannot fix issues at the new speed risks being put out of business before it patches.

The host closes by asking why, if agents are this capable, the hacking Americans experience has not obviously exploded. Schindel answers that some of it is hidden: a number of recent outages are indirectly caused by attacks, because organizations took systems down to patch their environments. More than twenty AI inference providers, he says, were hacked in the past few weeks, and the major incident response firms are at capacity and cannot take new customers. Nagli adds the timeline. AI showed glimpses of autonomous hacking as early as November of last year, and the capability was there months ago, but the last month is when it skyrocketed: the harnesses got better, the methodology got better, and cheap open models got better at hacking. His word for the result is a democratization of hacking. Interesting times, he calls them.

Open questions

  1. Nagli says frontier models are now better than the best human penetration testers, based largely on one public evaluation and Wiz's own harness. What independent test would settle that claim, and who should run it?
  2. If an AI-run attack can compromise an organization in about twenty minutes, what has to change in defenses built around patch cycles of days or weeks, and can smaller companies afford the new pace?
  3. Wiz scans public and government websites "for good," and urges every company to attack its own surface before criminals do. Who authorizes that kind of scanning, and what separates defensive use of these tools from the same tools aimed at someone else's systems?

Semafor · Jeff Bezos and Vik Bajaj on Prometheus and AI for the physical economy

Jeff Bezos On ‘AI For Atoms,’ Dealing With Democrats, & Picking The Next Bond | Compound Interest

On Semafor, "Jeff Bezos On ‘AI For Atoms,’ Dealing With Democrats, & Picking The Next Bond | Compound Interest" is a replay of a stage conversation from Semafor's Silicon Valley and the World event, recorded on November 17, 2026. Liz Hoffman, Semafor's business and finance editor, talks with Jeff Bezos and Vik Bajaj, the co-founders of Prometheus, their AI startup for the physical economy. The conversation ranges from the startup's engineering ambitions to Bezos's access to President Trump, his advice for Nvidia's board, the coming shakeout among AI startups heading public, and how Robert Pattinson became the next James Bond.

Bezos describes Prometheus as a bet on what he calls AI for atoms. Today's large language models, he says, are very good symbol manipulators: trained on the corpus of human knowledge, they handle problems that can be solved symbolically, like math and law. But a great deal cannot be learned by reading. A thousand books on gymnastics would not make him a gymnast, and engineering is the same kind of knowledge. Put six brilliant physicists, Newton, Einstein, and Maxwell among them, in a room and ask them to build a toaster oven, and they would have no idea how, because knowledge operates at different levels of detail. So Prometheus trains specialized models instead, building neural surrogates for numerical simulation inside a single multi-physics model.

The company is already substantial. Bezos says Prometheus has about 250 people and has raised approximately 14.5 billion dollars, with a lot of that committed to compute. He says the team is designing analog chips, radios, and other things that would take a team of ten to twenty people six to nine months, and doing it in twelve hours. A teammate who once worked on the Mars helicopter at JPL asked the tools to redesign it from a prompt, and the system explored the design space, settled on something very much like the real helicopter, and worked out details like the blade chord for the Martian atmosphere. The tools are in private alpha with companies now, including Blue Origin, which is using them on two projects. Bezos says he first invested when Prometheus was a tiny seed stage company in late 2024, watched its progress, and found he could not sit on the sidelines: "It's just far too interesting."

Hoffman asks whether the market is too big for a walled garden, and Bezos says it cannot be one: the manufactured world is tens of trillions of dollars, and no single company will build the toaster ovens or the jet engines itself. The approach will have to be broad and multiple: tools that other companies can use as they see fit, plus Prometheus investing in some of the companies it helps, benefiting as a principal rather than only as a toolmaker. He sees no scenario where a walled garden succeeds across a surface area that runs through thousands of companies and their supply chains.

Asked about the mistrust big companies feel toward frontier labs whose engineers might be on reconnaissance missions, Bajaj says Prometheus collaborates with customers, respects the proprietary nature of engineering work, trains on the parts of a dataset that are not proprietary, and generates a lot of its own data. Bezos illustrates with casting, a technique thousands of years old that still runs largely on trial and error: a part is cast, x-rayed for hairline fractures, and the mold is re-insulated by hand until the metal cools evenly. Companies like that, he says, often have little data worth protecting because their processes were never instrumented. But where a company does have a genuinely valuable proprietary dataset, his advice is blunt: guard it, and work hard not to share it. He thinks such datasets are rarer than people assume, and that having one will be a way companies differentiate themselves.

Underneath the business case sits a philosophy. All civilizational wealth, Bezos argues, comes from invention: someone invented the plow six thousand years ago, someone later invented the steam engine, and everyone got wealthier each time, while the inventors typically collect a little of it and go away. What Prometheus is trying to do, he says, is literally accelerate that dream and build loop. When Hoffman notes that it ended badly for Prometheus in the myth, he answers with the last half of it: Hercules showed up and saved him.

The politics segment turns on access. Democrats hold the gavel now, and Hoffman presses Bezos on whether his closeness to President Trump becomes a liability. Bezos says he has worked with every president since Clinton and hopes to work with every president going forward. Trump, he says, is simply the most communicative of them, unusually accessible in quantity rather than in kind, and he still reaches out to Obama for advice. He insists he has never tried to advantage Amazon or himself in those interactions, and that shutting business leaders out is a mistake, because officeholders cannot know the ground truth of American business on their own. The unreasonable conversation, he says, is the one about killing the goose that lays the golden eggs.

From there he moves to affordability, which he calls the core issue. Housing is expensive, he argues, because policy subsidized demand and constrained supply, and the only thing that will lower housing costs is building more housing. Austin, he says, lowered housing costs over the last decade even as its population grew, by changing policies that increased supply, including dropping a burdensome parking requirement. When Hoffman says many people plainly think the villains are in the room, given the wealth created in Silicon Valley, Bezos returns to the line the episode opens with: picking a villain is a good political technique but a terrible technique for solving problems. The villain rotates, he says, from lawyers to Wall Street to landlords to, now, big tech, and the picture is not a true one.

On Nvidia, Hoffman asks whether Jensen Huang should come back. Bezos declines to judge, saying outsiders cannot know what happened inside the board meetings, but he is certain Huang, who founded and built a remarkable company, deserves admiration and respect no matter what. His advice to the board is pointed: if Huang does not return, hire from internally. Nvidia is too complicated for an outsider, and outside chief executives in complex companies have, in his words, a vanishingly small chance of succeeding. The Amazon analogy, he cautions, is a poor one, because Amazon's transition was planned over multiple years. On the AI startups now heading to the public markets, he predicts investors will get more discerning. Early in a technology cycle every experiment gets funded, the good, the bad, and the ugly; over time the market learns to tell them apart and diverts resources to the better ideas. That discernment, he says, is starting now, and it is healthy, because the technology itself is real.

Bajaj, who previously ran Google's life sciences business, closes the substantive part with a plea for calibration. Early cancer detection is a ten year journey, he says, though blood tests using machine learning can already detect many cancers early, and drug discovery work that has been going on for about fifteen years, at companies like Isomorphic, Xaira, and Insitro, is on the verge of accelerating. The good results will come, he says, but as a dramatic increase in productivity, not as curing all disease in ten years. The episode ends on the Bond casting. Bezos says he was heavily involved: a large group was narrowed to five finalists, who did old-fashioned screen tests in full costume on real sets, three scenes each, and the final decision was made the Friday before the recording. The group had initially leaned against Pattinson because he was already so famous, but, Bezos says, in the screen tests it was obvious: "he is James Bond."

Open questions

  1. Prometheus's pitch rests on specialized models trained for engineering rather than on general language models. What public demonstration would prove that this approach beats pointing a frontier model at the same design problems?
  2. Bezos advises companies with genuinely valuable proprietary data to guard it and not share it. How does Prometheus get enough real world engineering data to train on if its best potential customers take that advice?
  3. Bezos wants political leaders to work on root causes with business leaders instead of picking villains, at the very moment Democrats are preparing to scrutinize the tech industry. Can that conversation happen in this environment, and who would have to start it?
