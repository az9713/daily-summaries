Alex Karp on Why AI Will Hurt the Middle Class: You Can’t Have Billionaires Becoming 100x Wealthier

On The Builders with Walter Isaacson, “Alex Karp on Why AI Will Hurt the Middle Class: You Can’t Have Billionaires Becoming 100x Wealthier” is Isaacson’s conversation with Alex Karp, the philosopher turned co-founder and chief executive of Palantir. Karp traces the company to his outsider’s temperament. Dyslexic and never, in his word, colonized by the broader culture, he says he could violate every norm of how software is built. Palantir spent three years building its anti-terror product with no revenue at all, something he thinks only a neurodivergent person at the helm would have fought for. The product, he says, changed the history of Europe by stopping hundreds of terror attacks, and Palantir’s tools later became fundamental to the defense of Ukraine.

The company began when Peter Thiel, Karp’s friend from Stanford Law School, proposed taking the pattern-finding methods PayPal had used against fraud and applying them to intelligence work. Karp had written a dissertation in Germany critiquing Heidegger, and he argues that Heideggerian thinking, communitarian and regressive, now sits inside movements on both the far right and the far left. He calls himself a still unhappy Democrat, warns Democrats that a faction of their party is not actually progressive, and defends the First, Second, and Fourth Amendments as still radical. The Constitution, in his telling, is the most productive thing still keeping the country working.

On surveillance, Karp says Europe got privacy right by constraining both commercial and government overreach, while Americans underestimate how much commercial products shape what they think. He calls Palantir the inverse of its surveillance caricature: its software works under a legal mandate, keeps data only while a warrant stands, and purges it when the warrant expires. He favors legislation that would bar battlefield AI technology from domestic use. A fascist state, he notes, has no crime because the government is the only criminal band left, and people with views like his would be the first against the wall.

The title’s argument takes up the middle of the conversation. Karp agrees that AI will widen wealth disparities sharply, and he calls it a legitimate critique that billionaires cannot keep multiplying their wealth while everyone else gains a few percent. He expects AI to create better jobs for working-class and vocational workers, but not at a pace that keeps up with people in tech. He rejects a wealth tax all the same, arguing that it would not help the poor, would drive liquid tech wealth away, and would require the state to value everything a person owns, a surveillance project in itself. His alternative is a bargain: taxes on people like him go up, steeply in his own sketch, on the condition that government identifies half a unit of waste for every unit it raises. Inequity and helping the poor, he insists, are two different problems, and policy that picks only one divides the society it claims to fix.

That bargain grows out of his larger charge against Silicon Valley. The valley, an insulated island born of a military, university, and venture capital hybrid, broke its covenant with the country by decoupling wealth creation from value creation, building on consumerism and ads while a carpenter in Louisiana sees little material improvement over thirty years. AI pours gasoline on that fire, he says, when the people running the labs tell everyone else that their lives will get worse and their jobs will vanish. Enterprises, meanwhile, are livid at the large model companies for training on their proprietary data while charging them for the product, and Palantir’s answer is sovereignty: an ontology layer that lets a company or a country use models without handing over its data or its way of working.

Isaacson closes by asking what remains distinctly human when machines surpass human cognition. Karp answers with the American founding. Rights in the United States are treated as granted by something higher than the state, which presupposes a human spirit that is artistic, individual, and special, worth more than cognitive ability alone. That, he says, is unique to this country and worth fighting to keep.

Open questions

  1. Karp argues AI-era disparity must be answered but rejects the wealth tax. Could a bargain like his, higher taxes traded for identified waste, ever be designed and enforced at national scale?
  2. He wants surveillance software bound by legal mandates and battlefield AI kept out of domestic life. Can those lines hold once the same capabilities sit in commercial products anyone can buy?
  3. If human worth rests on something beyond cognition, as Karp claims, what happens to that claim as machines take over more of the work and judgment societies used to prize?

Sourcery with Molly O'Shea · Brendan Foody on Mercor at a 20 billion valuation

Mercor Raises $750M at $20B Valuation | CEO Brendan Foody

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, "Mercor Raises $750M at $20B Valuation | CEO Brendan Foody" is a conversation with Brendan Foody, co-founder and chief executive of Mercor, the company that pays domain experts to build the training environments frontier AI labs learn from. The raise in the title frames the talk, but the interview itself is about something larger: Foody's claim that the way humans contribute to the economy is being rebuilt, and that Mercor intends to define the new category.

His starting point is that most knowledge work today is repetitive. People answer the same kinds of support tickets, redline the same kinds of contracts, edit the same kinds of podcasts. In Foody's telling, the future version of that work is done once, as teaching: a person trains an agent to perform the repetitive task, and contributes through evals, skills files, and training data instead. Mercor has grown more than fivefold since he and host Molly O'Shea first walked and talked in September of last year, and the question he works backwards from is what human work looks like when agents do the repeating.

The machine has three parts. The talent platform holds more than five million people looking for work, about seventy percent of them arriving by referral, and it now pays out well over five million dollars a day through Stripe Connect, close to a billion dollars cumulatively. Twenty-four months ago the same platform paid out about a million dollars a year. On top of it sits Studio, where experts use an AI co-pilot to build reinforcement learning environments, and a third layer, an AI project manager: agents that hire experts, set expectations, judge data quality, and answer for customer outcomes, doing the coordination work human project managers used to do.

What Mercor actually sells is the environment, not the match. Foody pushes back on the idea that Mercor is a marketplace like Uber taking a fee for introductions. An RL environment, as he describes it, has three ingredients: the apps, meaning working clones of tools like Google Workspace, Salesforce, and Adobe; the worlds, meaning the messages, documents, slides, and sheets inside those apps; and the tasks, meaning prompts paired with rubrics, the way a professor grades an essay against a written standard so a model can attempt the work and learn from its score. The experts are one ingredient of that product, and covering the full distribution of apps, worlds, and tasks across the whole economy is the barrier the whole industry runs into.

The payments operation carries its own origin story. Early on, Mercor supplied talent to another company in the space that ran into public payout trouble, and Mercor was flooded with support tickets from more than a thousand hired experts, many in poorer countries, who were not being paid and were struggling to feed their families. Foody says that flood was a large catalyst for insisting on a direct relationship with the labs and for overinvesting in a payments team that never misses. Being accountable for the expert experience, in his telling, became a foundation of the business rather than an operational detail.

Agents, meanwhile, have made his experts more valuable rather than less. An hour of expert time now produces several times the model improvement it once did, because the expert works with an AI co-pilot while building data. Foody invokes Jevons paradox: making teaching cheaper expands the demand for teaching, because by his estimate ninety-nine percent of knowledge still lives inside people's heads. The cheaper it gets to move that knowledge into models, the more of the economy tries to do it.

That is also his answer to the charge that the category is not durable, that training data is a bridge business that evaporates once models get good enough. The market for humans teaching models is bounded, he argues, by the set of things humans can do that models cannot, across both the digital and physical worlds: running a persuasive meeting, running a wet lab experiment, repairing a car. Models, like students, cannot easily grade their own work beyond their own frontier; they need a teacher's feedback to know where they are wrong. A few domains have automatic checkers, the way math has calculators, but most of the economy does not, and Foody says top lab researchers expect human feedback to remain the mechanism of improvement for decades.

The expert supply itself runs on an invention from the founders' college days. In April 2023, just after ChatGPT launched, Foody and co-founder Adarsh Hiremath built what he describes as the first AI interviewer, with seven or eight seconds of latency, which asked questions off a resume and scored answers against a rubric. Scaling it taught them that labor matching is mostly a data collection problem: for some talent pools you can meet five people and find a good one, and for others you must interview hundreds or thousands, which is only possible if the interviewing is automated. Mercor has now run more than seven million interviews, and Foody calls the result the fastest growing job category in history.

Asked how a founding team of college dropouts in their early twenties keeps winning against much larger rivals, Foody names three operating values. The first is high standards, a hiring bar he says the company refuses to compromise. The second is a value the company calls time travelers: living in the world of five to ten years from now and working backwards, which is how he says they found the market before the other talent networks and why he distrusts watching competitors, since imitation is, in his phrase, a really good way to be second place in a market. The third is customer obsession, moving mountains for the labs because they build important technology. He admits to varying chips on his shoulder, and to no interest at all in being second.

The harder years get their due. In the beginning the customers were seed stage startups, and most of them died within a year, a constant churn of loss. There was a security incident, and there were early team members who left. Foody reaches for Elon Musk's line about chewing glass, and says founders learn that chaos is the norm. Managing a team through it, he agrees with investor Alfred Lin's framing, means being a shock absorber: cracking down when everyone feels on top of the world, and cheerleading through the rough patches. He also describes pushing back when an executive put up a weekly revenue graph, arguing the company should watch customer happiness and expert utilization instead, the things that actually compound.

His heroes are Musk, whom he calls the greatest entrepreneur by results while wishing he stayed out of politics, and Jeff Bezos, for principled long-term thinking. A freshman research paper of Foody's argued that job creation is the economy's largest positive externality while payroll taxes are its largest revenue source, a direct disincentive to hiring, and that taxes should be eliminated for the bottom half of Americans, who account for about three percent of federal revenue. Bezos later posted the same idea on X and reposted Foody's retweet, which he recounts with undisguised delight. He has still never met Bezos in person.

The most concrete business news in the conversation is an acquisition. Mercor bought Deeptune, a company whose strength was the apps layer, the clones of Salesforce, Adobe, and Google Workspace that experts then populate with content. Apps were Mercor's biggest bottleneck, since worlds and tasks were its native competency. A customer pointed Mercor to Deeptune as its top apps partner, the partnership came first, founder Tim had recently raised a Series A from a16z, and the teams were aligned enough that Foody calls it a one plus one equals three combination that also anchors Mercor's New York office. It was Mercor's second acquisition and much the larger, and he says M&A generally is high leverage and something the company thinks about across a few categories. The reverse direction does not interest him: he and his co-founders have had chances to walk away with life-changing cash and turned them down, because the motivation is the problem, not the payout. He discusses the Scale AI and Meta arrangement respectfully, calls Alex Wang a generational entrepreneur, and says flatly that Mercor would never do something like that.

From there the conversation widens into a map of the industry. Foody sorts companies with real revenue into frontier labs, inference providers, and application layer companies, and argues that large parts of each get commoditized: GPUs commoditize the inference layer's hardware resale, and cheap software commoditizes the app layer's code. The durable moats he sees are brand and data. Harvey is his example of an app company doing both, building a world-class brand in law and a data flywheel that Mercor helps supply, including public legal benchmarks like its APEX work, so it can build the frontier model for its own domain rather than renting one. Labs defend themselves with economies of scale in compute and data plus a measure of recursive self-improvement. Inference companies, he argues, only escape zero switching costs by building custom models for customers, as Fireworks does, and he says investors overrate the revenue quality of API businesses generally, since sophisticated buyers switch APIs daily based on eval results, Mercor included.

Data, in his account, is the mispriced leg of the AI stack. Progress needs compute, algorithms, and data, yet investors have spent three years predicting superintelligence within three years and treating human training work as temporary; many top firms passed on Mercor's seed round on exactly that logic. The Jevons dynamic proved them wrong, and Foody is candidly grateful: contrarian status meant capital without much competition. He thinks the view is now on the verge of becoming consensus, as enterprises start evaluating and post-training their own models.

His ten year picture is that every Fortune 500 company comes to look like a small AI lab. Data sovereignty is the driver: a bank's advantage lives in its people, processes, and documents, and letting a frontier lab train on that material would be like handing it to a competitor, so companies will build their own models and agents that codify what they know. He offers a spending heuristic for the transition. Below about ten million dollars a year in inference spend, companies mostly do not care about evals. Between ten and a hundred million, roughly a tenth of the inference budget should go to building the data sets and evals that decide routing. Past a hundred million, as with Harvey or Cursor, post-training your own models starts to pay, because the fixed cost amortizes across a huge inference bill. Mercor itself, he says, spends almost a million dollars a day on inference.

His advice to those enterprises is to understand how frontier models actually learn now. Most training compute, he says, no longer goes into reading documents or imitation learning; it goes into reinforcement learning, where a model attempts a task many times and is scored against a rubric. That requires subject matter experts building tasks with clear eval scores. So: build tasks rather than assuming a model can read everything and learn, and start with the eval, because the eval is the baseline every serious lab and application company improves against.

On the near term, he says Mercor will not go public in the next twelve months, though an IPO is on the radar, and what he most looks forward to is classic Fortune 50 companies outside technology grasping all of this as frontier models creep into their sectors and the threat turns existential. His own days are less glamorous: up around 7:45, a workout, in the office by nine and there until ten at night, six days a week, with Sundays worked from home. His time splits roughly into thirds across hiring, customers, and research and product, and he still holds himself to inbox zero, while conceding that at some point you have to stop answering everything. The company, he notes with some irony, is hiring as fast as it can, experts and full-time staff alike.

Open questions

  1. Foody's durability case rests on models needing human feedback at the frontier for decades; if automated verification keeps expanding beyond math-like domains, how quickly does the boundary of what needs a human teacher actually shrink?
  2. The demand story runs on Jevons paradox, more productive experts creating more demand for experts; what happens to Mercor's economics if expert productivity rises faster than labs' and enterprises' budgets for data?
  3. If every large enterprise follows his advice and builds sovereign models on proprietary tasks and evals, does the independent data vendor become more essential, or do the biggest customers eventually take the work in house?
