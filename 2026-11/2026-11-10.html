The Builders with Walter Isaacson · Alex Karp on AI, wealth and the middle class

Alex Karp on Why AI Will Hurt the Middle Class: You Can’t Have Billionaires Becoming 100x Wealthier

On The Builders with Walter Isaacson, “Alex Karp on Why AI Will Hurt the Middle Class: You Can’t Have Billionaires Becoming 100x Wealthier” is Isaacson’s conversation with Alex Karp, the philosopher turned co-founder and chief executive of Palantir. Karp traces the company to his outsider’s temperament. Dyslexic and never, in his word, colonized by the broader culture, he says he could violate every norm of how software is built. Palantir spent three years building its anti-terror product with no revenue at all, something he thinks only a neurodivergent person at the helm would have fought for. The product, he says, changed the history of Europe by stopping hundreds of terror attacks, and Palantir’s tools later became fundamental to the defense of Ukraine.

The company began when Peter Thiel, Karp’s friend from Stanford Law School, proposed taking the pattern-finding methods PayPal had used against fraud and applying them to intelligence work. Karp had written a dissertation in Germany critiquing Heidegger, and he argues that Heideggerian thinking, communitarian and regressive, now sits inside movements on both the far right and the far left. He calls himself a still unhappy Democrat, warns Democrats that a faction of their party is not actually progressive, and defends the First, Second, and Fourth Amendments as still radical. The Constitution, in his telling, is the most productive thing still keeping the country working.

On surveillance, Karp says Europe got privacy right by constraining both commercial and government overreach, while Americans underestimate how much commercial products shape what they think. He calls Palantir the inverse of its surveillance caricature: its software works under a legal mandate, keeps data only while a warrant stands, and purges it when the warrant expires. He favors legislation that would bar battlefield AI technology from domestic use. A fascist state, he notes, has no crime because the government is the only criminal band left, and people with views like his would be the first against the wall.

The title’s argument takes up the middle of the conversation. Karp agrees that AI will widen wealth disparities sharply, and he calls it a legitimate critique that billionaires cannot keep multiplying their wealth while everyone else gains a few percent. He expects AI to create better jobs for working-class and vocational workers, but not at a pace that keeps up with people in tech. He rejects a wealth tax all the same, arguing that it would not help the poor, would drive liquid tech wealth away, and would require the state to value everything a person owns, a surveillance project in itself. His alternative is a bargain: taxes on people like him go up, steeply in his own sketch, on the condition that government identifies half a unit of waste for every unit it raises. Inequity and helping the poor, he insists, are two different problems, and policy that picks only one divides the society it claims to fix.

That bargain grows out of his larger charge against Silicon Valley. The valley, an insulated island born of a military, university, and venture capital hybrid, broke its covenant with the country by decoupling wealth creation from value creation, building on consumerism and ads while a carpenter in Louisiana sees little material improvement over thirty years. AI pours gasoline on that fire, he says, when the people running the labs tell everyone else that their lives will get worse and their jobs will vanish. Enterprises, meanwhile, are livid at the large model companies for training on their proprietary data while charging them for the product, and Palantir’s answer is sovereignty: an ontology layer that lets a company or a country use models without handing over its data or its way of working.

Isaacson closes by asking what remains distinctly human when machines surpass human cognition. Karp answers with the American founding. Rights in the United States are treated as granted by something higher than the state, which presupposes a human spirit that is artistic, individual, and special, worth more than cognitive ability alone. That, he says, is unique to this country and worth fighting to keep.

Open questions

  1. Karp argues AI-era disparity must be answered but rejects the wealth tax. Could a bargain like his, higher taxes traded for identified waste, ever be designed and enforced at national scale?
  2. He wants surveillance software bound by legal mandates and battlefield AI kept out of domestic life. Can those lines hold once the same capabilities sit in commercial products anyone can buy?
  3. If human worth rests on something beyond cognition, as Karp claims, what happens to that claim as machines take over more of the work and judgment societies used to prize?

Sourcery with Molly O'Shea · Brendan Foody on Mercor at a 20 billion valuation

Mercor Raises $750M at $20B Valuation | CEO Brendan Foody

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, "Mercor Raises $750M at $20B Valuation | CEO Brendan Foody" is a conversation with Brendan Foody, co-founder and chief executive of Mercor, the company that pays domain experts to build the training environments frontier AI labs learn from. The raise in the title frames the talk, but the interview itself is about something larger: Foody's claim that the way humans contribute to the economy is being rebuilt, and that Mercor intends to define the new category.

His starting point is that most knowledge work today is repetitive. People answer the same kinds of support tickets, redline the same kinds of contracts, edit the same kinds of podcasts. In Foody's telling, the future version of that work is done once, as teaching: a person trains an agent to perform the repetitive task, and contributes through evals, skills files, and training data instead. Mercor has grown more than fivefold since he and host Molly O'Shea first walked and talked in September of last year, and the question he works backwards from is what human work looks like when agents do the repeating.

The machine has three parts. The talent platform holds more than five million people looking for work, about seventy percent of them arriving by referral, and it now pays out well over five million dollars a day through Stripe Connect, close to a billion dollars cumulatively. Twenty-four months ago the same platform paid out about a million dollars a year. On top of it sits Studio, where experts use an AI co-pilot to build reinforcement learning environments, and a third layer, an AI project manager: agents that hire experts, set expectations, judge data quality, and answer for customer outcomes, doing the coordination work human project managers used to do.

What Mercor actually sells is the environment, not the match. Foody pushes back on the idea that Mercor is a marketplace like Uber taking a fee for introductions. An RL environment, as he describes it, has three ingredients: the apps, meaning working clones of tools like Google Workspace, Salesforce, and Adobe; the worlds, meaning the messages, documents, slides, and sheets inside those apps; and the tasks, meaning prompts paired with rubrics, the way a professor grades an essay against a written standard so a model can attempt the work and learn from its score. The experts are one ingredient of that product, and covering the full distribution of apps, worlds, and tasks across the whole economy is the barrier the whole industry runs into.

The payments operation carries its own origin story. Early on, Mercor supplied talent to another company in the space that ran into public payout trouble, and Mercor was flooded with support tickets from more than a thousand hired experts, many in poorer countries, who were not being paid and were struggling to feed their families. Foody says that flood was a large catalyst for insisting on a direct relationship with the labs and for overinvesting in a payments team that never misses. Being accountable for the expert experience, in his telling, became a foundation of the business rather than an operational detail.

Agents, meanwhile, have made his experts more valuable rather than less. An hour of expert time now produces several times the model improvement it once did, because the expert works with an AI co-pilot while building data. Foody invokes Jevons paradox: making teaching cheaper expands the demand for teaching, because by his estimate ninety-nine percent of knowledge still lives inside people's heads. The cheaper it gets to move that knowledge into models, the more of the economy tries to do it.

That is also his answer to the charge that the category is not durable, that training data is a bridge business that evaporates once models get good enough. The market for humans teaching models is bounded, he argues, by the set of things humans can do that models cannot, across both the digital and physical worlds: running a persuasive meeting, running a wet lab experiment, repairing a car. Models, like students, cannot easily grade their own work beyond their own frontier; they need a teacher's feedback to know where they are wrong. A few domains have automatic checkers, the way math has calculators, but most of the economy does not, and Foody says top lab researchers expect human feedback to remain the mechanism of improvement for decades.

The expert supply itself runs on an invention from the founders' college days. In April 2023, just after ChatGPT launched, Foody and co-founder Adarsh Hiremath built what he describes as the first AI interviewer, with seven or eight seconds of latency, which asked questions off a resume and scored answers against a rubric. Scaling it taught them that labor matching is mostly a data collection problem: for some talent pools you can meet five people and find a good one, and for others you must interview hundreds or thousands, which is only possible if the interviewing is automated. Mercor has now run more than seven million interviews, and Foody calls the result the fastest growing job category in history.

Asked how a founding team of college dropouts in their early twenties keeps winning against much larger rivals, Foody names three operating values. The first is high standards, a hiring bar he says the company refuses to compromise. The second is a value the company calls time travelers: living in the world of five to ten years from now and working backwards, which is how he says they found the market before the other talent networks and why he distrusts watching competitors, since imitation is, in his phrase, a really good way to be second place in a market. The third is customer obsession, moving mountains for the labs because they build important technology. He admits to varying chips on his shoulder, and to no interest at all in being second.

The harder years get their due. In the beginning the customers were seed stage startups, and most of them died within a year, a constant churn of loss. There was a security incident, and there were early team members who left. Foody reaches for Elon Musk's line about chewing glass, and says founders learn that chaos is the norm. Managing a team through it, he agrees with investor Alfred Lin's framing, means being a shock absorber: cracking down when everyone feels on top of the world, and cheerleading through the rough patches. He also describes pushing back when an executive put up a weekly revenue graph, arguing the company should watch customer happiness and expert utilization instead, the things that actually compound.

His heroes are Musk, whom he calls the greatest entrepreneur by results while wishing he stayed out of politics, and Jeff Bezos, for principled long-term thinking. A freshman research paper of Foody's argued that job creation is the economy's largest positive externality while payroll taxes are its largest revenue source, a direct disincentive to hiring, and that taxes should be eliminated for the bottom half of Americans, who account for about three percent of federal revenue. Bezos later posted the same idea on X and reposted Foody's retweet, which he recounts with undisguised delight. He has still never met Bezos in person.

The most concrete business news in the conversation is an acquisition. Mercor bought Deeptune, a company whose strength was the apps layer, the clones of Salesforce, Adobe, and Google Workspace that experts then populate with content. Apps were Mercor's biggest bottleneck, since worlds and tasks were its native competency. A customer pointed Mercor to Deeptune as its top apps partner, the partnership came first, founder Tim had recently raised a Series A from a16z, and the teams were aligned enough that Foody calls it a one plus one equals three combination that also anchors Mercor's New York office. It was Mercor's second acquisition and much the larger, and he says M&A generally is high leverage and something the company thinks about across a few categories. The reverse direction does not interest him: he and his co-founders have had chances to walk away with life-changing cash and turned them down, because the motivation is the problem, not the payout. He discusses the Scale AI and Meta arrangement respectfully, calls Alex Wang a generational entrepreneur, and says flatly that Mercor would never do something like that.

From there the conversation widens into a map of the industry. Foody sorts companies with real revenue into frontier labs, inference providers, and application layer companies, and argues that large parts of each get commoditized: GPUs commoditize the inference layer's hardware resale, and cheap software commoditizes the app layer's code. The durable moats he sees are brand and data. Harvey is his example of an app company doing both, building a world-class brand in law and a data flywheel that Mercor helps supply, including public legal benchmarks like its APEX work, so it can build the frontier model for its own domain rather than renting one. Labs defend themselves with economies of scale in compute and data plus a measure of recursive self-improvement. Inference companies, he argues, only escape zero switching costs by building custom models for customers, as Fireworks does, and he says investors overrate the revenue quality of API businesses generally, since sophisticated buyers switch APIs daily based on eval results, Mercor included.

Data, in his account, is the mispriced leg of the AI stack. Progress needs compute, algorithms, and data, yet investors have spent three years predicting superintelligence within three years and treating human training work as temporary; many top firms passed on Mercor's seed round on exactly that logic. The Jevons dynamic proved them wrong, and Foody is candidly grateful: contrarian status meant capital without much competition. He thinks the view is now on the verge of becoming consensus, as enterprises start evaluating and post-training their own models.

His ten year picture is that every Fortune 500 company comes to look like a small AI lab. Data sovereignty is the driver: a bank's advantage lives in its people, processes, and documents, and letting a frontier lab train on that material would be like handing it to a competitor, so companies will build their own models and agents that codify what they know. He offers a spending heuristic for the transition. Below about ten million dollars a year in inference spend, companies mostly do not care about evals. Between ten and a hundred million, roughly a tenth of the inference budget should go to building the data sets and evals that decide routing. Past a hundred million, as with Harvey or Cursor, post-training your own models starts to pay, because the fixed cost amortizes across a huge inference bill. Mercor itself, he says, spends almost a million dollars a day on inference.

His advice to those enterprises is to understand how frontier models actually learn now. Most training compute, he says, no longer goes into reading documents or imitation learning; it goes into reinforcement learning, where a model attempts a task many times and is scored against a rubric. That requires subject matter experts building tasks with clear eval scores. So: build tasks rather than assuming a model can read everything and learn, and start with the eval, because the eval is the baseline every serious lab and application company improves against.

On the near term, he says Mercor will not go public in the next twelve months, though an IPO is on the radar, and what he most looks forward to is classic Fortune 50 companies outside technology grasping all of this as frontier models creep into their sectors and the threat turns existential. His own days are less glamorous: up around 7:45, a workout, in the office by nine and there until ten at night, six days a week, with Sundays worked from home. His time splits roughly into thirds across hiring, customers, and research and product, and he still holds himself to inbox zero, while conceding that at some point you have to stop answering everything. The company, he notes with some irony, is hiring as fast as it can, experts and full-time staff alike.

Open questions

  1. Foody's durability case rests on models needing human feedback at the frontier for decades; if automated verification keeps expanding beyond math-like domains, how quickly does the boundary of what needs a human teacher actually shrink?
  2. The demand story runs on Jevons paradox, more productive experts creating more demand for experts; what happens to Mercor's economics if expert productivity rises faster than labs' and enterprises' budgets for data?
  3. If every large enterprise follows his advice and builds sovereign models on proprietary tasks and evals, does the independent data vendor become more essential, or do the biggest customers eventually take the work in house?

Blackstone · Ari Emanuel on entertainment in the age of AI

Ari Emanuel on the Future of Entertainment in the Age of AI

On Blackstone's Inside Blackstone, “Ari Emanuel on the Future of Entertainment in the Age of AI” is host Christine Anderson's conversation with Ari Emanuel, the longtime Hollywood agent who built William Morris Endeavor, now leads TKO, the company behind UFC and WWE, and is building MARI, a new live events and experiences company. The interview doubles as a tour of his bestselling memoir, Roll the Calls, which he says took ten years and was written on a simple warning from his own team: if he did not write his story, someone else would, and he would hate their version. Where other memoirs sand down the edges, he leans into them, because the honest version of the job is brutal. His book opens on the claim that work is the true story of Hollywood, a 24/7 business with hundreds of millions of dollars constantly on the line.

That work ethic defines his account of the agent's job. Emanuel describes himself as do or die for clients, a partner in the business side of their lives who thinks about them from the moment his head leaves the pillow until it returns at night. On a given day he might make around 300 calls, spanning agency clients like Mark Wahlberg, Jonah Hill, Martin Scorsese, and Aaron Sorkin, the 16 businesses inside MARI, and TKO, circles he happily describes as a collision course. His famous bluntness, short calls with no small talk and no goodbye, grew out of lean years and conviction: you will know if he is your friend, and you will know if he is your enemy. He tells how he met his partner Mark Shapiro on a Friday night flight, watching a stranger type nonstop with two fingers for three and a half hours, moving a sleeping passenger so he could sit down and demand to know what the man was doing. They have been friends and partners ever since.

Asked how he values creative assets, Emanuel answers with the first negotiation of his career. As a new agent at CAA he was fighting a Warner Brothers television executive over a few thousand dollars on a small option, protesting that the studio's position was not fair. The executive summoned him to the Valley and delivered the lesson: fair is where we end up. Emanuel says that is still his whole method. His job is to build the environment in which bids rise, package great material, and find out what the marketplace will bear. Both sides settle at the number each can accept, and that number is fair by definition.

MARI is his biggest current build, and he frames it as the opposite bet of AI. He raised roughly $2 billion of equity and about $900 million of debt to assemble live businesses across three arenas: holiday and seasonal entertainment such as Winter Wonderland, food festivals, and flower shows; collectibles, including Frieze, Barrett-Jackson, and the Pokémon event Collect-A-Con; and sports, from the Miami and Madrid tennis Opens to participatory events like the Escape from Alcatraz triathlon and the Melbourne Marathon. Underneath sits TodayTix, the ticketing platform, and Bucket Listers, a discovery channel that helps people decide what to do in a city. The ambition, he says, is to become for live events what Live Nation is in music.

On AI and filmed entertainment, Emanuel is an enthusiast with a caveat. He expects far more movies and television to be made, at much lower production cost, with AI unlocking creativity rather than replacing it. New forms will keep multiplying: social video, podcasts, and microdramas, which in China have already surpassed new movies, many of them AI-made. Advertising will push deeper into streaming. The pain lands below the line, where production jobs will be challenged and the industry will have to readjust. What survives, he argues, is judgment and taste. Audiences do not want fully AI movies, but at the right production price the economics of AI-assisted content work, and the balance will be found case by case rather than in black and white.

His core conviction is that live cannot be disintermediated, a point he says Elon Musk once put to him directly: live is the one thing that cannot be taken apart by technology. Emanuel extends the argument into a prediction about time itself. By the data he watches, hotel, restaurant, and travel bookings, people already work something like a four-day week, Thursday has become baby Friday, and the workweek is heading toward three days. More free time means more spending on venues, travel, and events, by his math roughly a third more revenue flowing into leisure. Sports sit at the center of this bet because they are scarce, shared, and unscripted.

The agent's business will change too, he says, as brands become content creators in their own right across podcasts, microdramas, and social. Hollywood, in his telling, is not even in the bottom half of the first inning of the AI era. The change will be bigger and faster than anyone expects, investors do not yet understand the speed or magnitude of either the dislocation or the upside, and the path will bob and weave the way his own career did. In the aggregate, though, he expects a net positive for the agency business, the finance business, and AI itself.

For young people trying to enter the industry, his advice is unfashionable: the mailroom and the training programs still exist, and what they should study is the arts. Taste, culture, literature, and creative thinking are, in his words, values that AI does not do, and they take years of deliberate work to refine. His own edge is maintained by routine. He sleeps six and a half to seven hours, works out and uses a sauna every day, does a breathing meditation each morning, fasts every Monday and for three days each quarter, and says that if he could keep only one element, it would be sleep.

The episode bookends the interview with two Blackstone voices. Vice Chairman and CFO Michael Chae opens with a cautiously optimistic market read: an investment boom and a strong consumer are driving the highest S&P earnings growth in five years, the main headwinds are a cost-of-capital shock and doubt about the boom's durability, and inflation outside energy looks contained enough that the Fed may gain room to ease. He also reports that large companies in Japan and Korea need private capital solutions more than ever. In the closing debrief, Chris James, who heads the firm's Tactical Opportunities business, makes the investor's version of Emanuel's argument: live entertainment is an attractive asset class because scarce, emotional, in-person moments stand apart in a world of digital abundance, and the ways to invest run well beyond teams, into arenas, venue infrastructure, entertainment districts, and the premium end of the fan experience.

Open questions

  1. Emanuel's bet rests on scarcity: filmed content becomes abundant and cheap while live stays rare. Do new forms like microdramas strengthen that premium by growing overall appetite for stories, or do they train audiences to stay home?
  2. If taste and judgment are the durable human skills in an AI-saturated industry, how do agencies and studios actually identify, train, and pay for them, and who captures the value they create?
  3. His shorter-workweek dividend assumes leisure spending keeps rising as AI spreads. If the dislocation reaches household incomes before the free time does, does the live-events boom arrive on schedule?

Cheeky Pint · Nik Storonsky on building Revolut without compromise

Nik Storonsky: The most uncompromising banking CEO

On Cheeky Pint, "Nik Storonsky: The most uncompromising banking CEO" is John Collison's conversation with Nik Storonsky, the co-founder and chief executive of Revolut. Storonsky insists he never optimized for scale. He worked on the product and solved problems end to end, and the company went from a handful of customers a day to roughly 100,000 people joining daily. The first seven years were purely organic, word of mouth and press, and Revolut only built serious growth budgets and referral machinery in 2022.

The first product was a multi-currency card with zero foreign exchange fees, and Storonsky argues there was nothing special about the wedge. Most bank products are simply bad, he says, so any one of them, acquiring, credit, or trading, could have been the starting point if it was rebuilt far better and far cheaper. Business accounts were the second proof: they began at about ten customers a day with no marketing and now onboard around 1,500 a day, still mostly organic.

Revolut builds products through small teams of about ten people working from first principles. Its new bets process lets anyone in the company raise an idea, which is validated on unit economics and then judged by people who have built bets before. A funded team takes roughly 9 to 18 months to launch, releases in one test country, and is then fed into an automated cross-sell engine. Each bet is graded against the gross profit curve of the roughly one hundred bets that came before it. Top quartile bets get more teams, bottom quartile teams are redistributed, and bets customers rely on are maintained but no longer funded. The early trend, Storonsky says, almost never lies: products that grow fast in the first year keep growing, and slow burners that later take off basically do not happen.

He is similarly blunt about ideas. Execution matters more than the idea, and too many early failures of his own judgment have made him optimistic about almost anything plausible. The Ultra subscription, a premium account at about 50 euros a month that he compares to an Amex premium card, struck him as merely okay and became a very large business. Virality, on the other hand, disappoints him: peer-to-peer payments drive signups in countries with weak payment systems, but identity checks create so much drop-off that the viral coefficient has stayed below one in everything Revolut has tried over twelve years.

Revolut's revenue model looks nothing like a traditional bank's, and Storonsky traces that to Europe, where low interchange and tightly regulated credit rates forced the company to make transactional and subscription revenue work. Credit is growing now that Revolut holds deposits, but the target is to keep lending under ten percent of the balance sheet and securitize the rest. Credit profit, in his framing, is like selling options: you collect the premium for a decade and can lose it all in year eleven when unemployment jumps. Subscription revenue cannot go negative, and that difference in quality, not investor fashion, is why the two models earn different multiples.

The cross-sell engine is where the model compounds. A machine learning model weighs conversion, gross profit, and the cost of bothering the customer, and a large language model acting as an account manager makes the final call, using what Revolut can see in a customer's transactions. Products that earn no direct revenue are still held to account through indirect gross profit, an attribution of the churn they prevent. RevPoints, the rewards program, shows small accounting profit and very high indirect profit. Most companies never get this strict, he argues, because they run only a handful of bets, their feedback loops are slow, and management turns over before the data accumulates.

Winning a customer's salary deposit is the real prize, because salaried users adopt everything else. Only 10 to 20 percent of monthly active users are salaried accounts today, but more than half of people under 35 in most European markets already hold a Revolut account, and the company courts children early because adults are so hard to move. Churn even runs in Revolut's favor: cohorts show a smile, with monthly actives dipping and then rising above their starting level after two years as lapsed users hear about the product again and return.

Market selection follows a simple rubric: bad incumbent banks, strong unit economics per customer, and a license that is actually obtainable. That is why Revolut prefers sleepy legacy markets and deprioritizes countries with strong digital rivals. The United States failed to produce neobanks, he argues, because startups avoided the hard licensing path and partnered with legacy banks instead, a model that never fit together. Revolut has conditional approval from the OCC, expects its remaining approvals, and plans a credit card led launch around next year, going directly at Amex Platinum and Chase Sapphire while adding wealth and crypto on top. A euro stablecoin is coming for the same reason: the dollar has credible stablecoins and the euro does not.

Artificial intelligence is now in nearly every part of the company, and Revolut is trying to build an AI employee end to end. Storonsky concedes that one developer can now cover frontend, backend, and data work, and that a product team could shrink to two people, but Revolut still runs its specialized structure and will only switch when the new way has a proven track record. His own days are hyper-scheduled in 15 and 30 minute slots, spent almost entirely inside the company on product and process reviews. Part of his job, he says, is detecting bullshit: numbers can look good because of luck, and when he pushed on a wealth product growing 60 to 80 percent a year, the fix was a new team, after which it grew 300 percent.

Two ventures bookend the conversation. The Pragmatic Engineer · Sean Allen on software correctness and programming languages

Software correctness and programming languages with Sean Allen

On The Pragmatic Engineer, "Software correctness and programming languages with Sean Allen" is Gergely Orosz's conversation with Sean Allen, a Distinguished Engineer at Antithesis, recorded around a bug bash in Copenhagen. The episode is framed as a discussion about the challenges of developing with AI that tries not to mention AI at all, on the theory that the fundamentals matter as much as ever. Allen works on Antithesis's red team, using the product exactly as customers do, with only the tools customers have, and teaching what he has learned about being sure software works.

His path into the field was accidental. Music was his first love, and he got into tech by fixing computers at Scholastic and teaching himself Perl. As a teenager he had been put off by a machine language book for the Commodore 64 his grandmother bought him, and as a young programmer he was mentored on a BeOS mailing list by the head of Metrowerks' compiler group and, though he did not know it at the time, by the science fiction author Neal Stephenson. Both men, fittingly, had their first professional software jobs at companies called SenseNet, his in New York and Orosz's in Budapest.

The language tour starts with OCaml and the ML family. Allen explains that ML languages are built around strong type systems: sum types, pattern matching, and exhaustive matching, so that when you add a sixth case to a type that had five, the compiler points at every place in the codebase that fails to handle it. Finance firms like Jane Street love OCaml for this, he says, though almost nobody uses the object-oriented features that give the language the O in its name. Rust, he notes, inherited much of this from ML, which is why its compiler catches so much.

He contrasts that with his own early choice between C and Ada. Ada, built for defense and other mission-critical work, was so strict he could barely get programs to compile, while C happily compiled code that then segfaulted. Only later did he understand that Ada had been refusing exactly the memory-unsafe things he was trying to do. He spent years building personal rules for writing concurrent C safely, and eventually concluded he would rather have a compiler enforce those rules, because humans invariably slip. He is similarly warm about F#, Don Syme's OCaml descendant on Microsoft's .NET platform, praising how seamlessly it fits its runtime and how many of its ideas have since flowed into C#, with sum types and exhaustive matching the notable holdouts.

Pony, the open-source language Allen helps maintain, is where these threads meet. His college friend Sylvan Clebsch built its actor-based runtime at Morgan Stanley, then earned a PhD at Imperial College London to design a language on top of it. Allen's startup Wallaroo adopted Pony for high-speed data processing for banks, where the JVM's stop-the-world garbage collection made latency guarantees impossible. Pony aims to make illegal states unrepresentable: its type system enforces the concurrency rules Allen once kept in his head, and each actor manages its own heap, so memory is freed in small interleaved steps instead of world-stopping pauses. Garbage collection, he argues, is not inherently slow; the confusion is between the mechanism of freeing memory and one particular stop-the-world design, and sophisticated manual allocators are already halfway to being collectors.

Correctness itself, for Allen, means a program does what you expect it to do, framed as invariants it upholds, and it does not wake anyone up at 2 a.m. This is why he insists Antithesis is a correctness and verification company rather than a testing company, and why he wrote that no one cares about testing: tests are a means to an end, a late-stage check for things you had no better way to guarantee. The better move is to push guarantees into construction, encoding states and their allowed transitions in a type system so whole classes of bugs cannot be expressed. He loves test-driven development as a way to design APIs, but tells how, in pairing exercises, he would defeat example-based tests by hardcoding the expected answers, until the only way to beat him was to randomize the inputs, which is to say, to arrive at property-based testing.

The missing piece in most testing strategies, he argues, is the workload: not just the operations a test performs, but the shape of the inputs driving the system. Uniformly random operations on a key-value store amount to a drunken walk that never strays far from where it started, so it never reaches the states where resharding or disk pressure cause trouble. His favorite counterexample is the bank test for database isolation: ten accounts holding ten thousand dollars in total, money moved between them in transactions, and a checker that must only ever see ten thousand. It is a trivially simple workload, yet under fault injection of the kind Kyle Kingsbury's Jepsen work made famous, and that Antithesis performs, serious bugs fall out of real databases.

That leads to his claim that quality is not a gate. The gate picture, quality as something you protect by catching bad code at the door, is comforting but false: every tool leaks, production always contains data your generators never produced, and everyone, admitted or not, tests in production. In an ideal world a specification in a language like TLA+ would be model-checked into a correct implementation, and below that ideal sit static checking, type systems, and actor models that limit what code can do. What remains is testing and verification for whatever those cannot enforce. Teams that move fast and stay reliable, he says, think about quality from the first day, asking how they will know the system works before they build it, rather than bolting on a staging environment at the end. Even for a system like Google Search, where the single right answer is subjective, correctness decomposes into invariants: a query returns results, results belong to the query that asked, and one user's data never surfaces in another's account.

On supply chain security, Allen is bleak. Nearly every language lets a downloaded dependency do anything the host program can do, a trust model inherited from Perl's CPAN era. Cooldown periods, vendoring, and forking are all naive defenses, and sandboxing leaks. The principled answer he points to is object capabilities, the research line from Mark Miller's language E, where code can only act on capabilities it has been handed, built into Pony but absent from every popular language, and undermined by foreign function interfaces even where it exists. Worse, speculative execution means side-channel attacks void nearly every other guarantee: the confidential computing systems he worked on at Microsoft Research all carried the caveat "modulo side-channel attacks," and the industry, having seen early kernel patches cost enormous performance, chose speed and whack-a-mole over giving up speculation. He admits he exploits branch prediction in his own open-source code.

He closes with two reframings. Technical debt, he still maintains a decade on, only hurts if you have to pay it back: debt can be sidestepped by new technology, as when a distributed database dissolved his team's MySQL sharding ceiling, or never paid at all when a product dies, so the real goal is malleable software that can adapt or be thrown away. And going slow to go fast is literal for him: writing the planning document, agreeing on what words mean, since the distributed systems community once spent weeks arguing about exactly-once delivery when the two sides meant processing and arrival, deciding how a thing will be tested before building it, and resting, because a tired brain produces the bugs that eat the time you saved.

  2. If object capabilities are the principled fix for supply chain security but no popular language has them, is there any credible migration path for existing package ecosystems, or does the industry simply accept the risk?
  3. He says everyone tests in production because no tool can guarantee correctness. What should teams building regulated or safety-critical systems, where production is not an acceptable test bed, do with that claim?

Silicon Valley Girl · Max Jaderberg on finding the next big opportunity in AI

DeepMind Veteran: How to Find the Next Big Opportunity in AI

On Silicon Valley Girl, "DeepMind Veteran: How to Find the Next Big Opportunity in AI" is a conversation with Max Jaderberg, an Oxford-trained AI researcher who is now president of Isomorphic Labs, the company Demis Hassabis founded to design new medicines with AI. Jaderberg helped develop AlphaFold 3 at Google DeepMind, and the interview is built around one claim: the same kind of breakthrough that cracked protein structure can be repeated across the rest of drug discovery, and the pattern behind it points to where AI's next big opportunities sit.

His path ran through core deep learning. He started in computer vision, then spent years at DeepMind on general algorithms, new architectures, early generative and language models, and deep reinforcement learning, working on challenge projects such as AlphaGo and AlphaStar. Watching those systems solve problems that had looked impossible, and then seeing the AlphaFold results emerge internally, convinced him that AI models could genuinely generalize in the space of biology and chemistry. Nobody had shown that before, and he calls it the proof point that justified founding Isomorphic Labs to go after what the company states plainly as its mission: solving all disease.

AlphaFold, he is careful to say, is not itself a medicine. Making a drug takes about ten years from conception to patients, and roughly 90 percent of medicines fail along the way, because a working drug requires a dozen hard problems to come together at once. You have to pick the right protein target and pathway, design a molecule that modulates it as intended, get that molecule to the right part of the body, and be confident it will not be toxic. His answer is that drug discovery needs not one breakthrough but half a dozen or more AlphaFold-level breakthroughs, assembled into a single drug design engine.

The team frames those breakthroughs as superpowers. If you could hand a chemist or biologist one capability that would radically improve their work, what would it be? The answers cover understanding how cells and disease biology work, designing different aspects of molecules, and predicting human toxicity. AI then works on two fronts: foundation models of biology that explain how proteins, DNA, and RNA fit together into pathways and how disease changes them, and design models that create the intervention itself, whether a small molecule pill, an antibody, or a peptide.

The business was built to avoid a decade of quiet research. From day one, Isomorphic Labs developed its platform while also doing real drug design, through internal programs it funds and owns outright and through partnerships where major pharmaceutical companies brought what Jaderberg calls the industry's hardest problems. The point of the partnerships was discipline: in his phrase, you cannot mark your own homework, and being held to the industry's top standard forced the models to speak to true commercial problems.

He also argues that the bar for "ready" is lower in drug discovery than in fields like self-driving cars, which need near-perfect accuracy before deployment. Drug discovery's status quo is an empirical, trial-and-error lab process with weak predictive power, so a model does not need to be perfect to create real value. It needs a significant accuracy delta over what the rest of the industry can access. The working method is to track progress on internal benchmarks and evals, then aggressively translate those gains onto live drug programs. When benchmark progress stops translating into real results, the evals themselves are wrong, and the team goes back and redesigns them.

An audacious mission, he says, only works if it is decomposed. Isomorphic Labs uses what he calls a mission tree, breaking "solve all disease" down level by level until every individual has a personal mission that ladders up to the top. Teams get quantitative leaderboards: one modeling task carried a target of 80 percent accuracy on a benchmark where the world sat at 10 percent, and the day-to-day experience was watching the number climb a percent or two at a time until the out-of-reach target compounded into reach.

Asked where the next AlphaFold moment could happen outside biology, Jaderberg draws a line between reasoning and simulation. Today's reasoning models can analyze data and generate hypotheses, but they cannot yet predict what a physical experiment will return, and in biology, materials science, and much of engineering the bottleneck is still poking the real world and waiting for its response. The opportunity is models that simulate part of that physical world well enough that you can poke the model instead. And the binding constraint, he argues, is rarely the algorithm or the compute. It is data: if the available data is small, noisy, or low in signal, the modeling task cannot move, which makes new ways of generating data a business opportunity in themselves.

Hiring follows from the same logic. There is no world expert in designing drugs with AI, because the field has not existed long enough, so Isomorphic Labs hires the best people from each separate discipline, including AI engineers and scientists with no life sciences background at all, and asks them to learn each other's languages. The naivety is a feature: outsiders bring first-principles thinking to a field they have no habits in. His interview probe is to ask candidates to talk honestly about learning a completely new field and about where their technical work failed, and to listen for how they approached being in over their heads.

The scarcest skill in this setup is what he calls research taste. AI can generate thousands or millions of candidate molecule designs, and the value sits in choosing which few are worth pursuing. Isomorphic Labs pairs its generative design models with simulation models, a virtual lab that predicts how a design would respond in the real world, and uses them to whittle the candidates down to the 20 or 50 actually worth making and testing. That replaces the old workflow of experimentally screening hundreds of thousands of designs. The researcher's job shifts accordingly: instead of proposing individual ideas, researchers define the design space and the goals, and agents work autonomously inside that space, in some cases running for days at a time. Success for an agent is not a single accuracy number but a goal space of many properties that often conflict, and he insists on staying eval-driven, because in biology the results cannot be strongly verified on a computer the way code can.

On regulation, he defends the slow part. Animal models are only about 30 percent predictive of human toxicity, and even with all the preclinical evidence regulators require, around 90 percent of drugs still fail in the clinic, so the phased caution is earned. The opportunity is that better predictive models should eventually let regulators move faster with the same safety. He would not put a date on the first fully AI-designed drug reaching a pharmacy, but says the company's own pipeline is being pushed toward the clinic as fast as possible, with its most exciting programs in oncology and in immunology and inflammation. Over the next decade he expects a big shift in the kinds of medicines available, moving toward treatments designed for narrower groups of patients and eventually for individuals.

His advice to someone who wants to work in AI for science over the next five years is unfashionable: do not neglect learning the science itself. Compute is finite, so the enduring human contribution is deciding which questions deserve it. Research taste, the ability to filter quickly to the questions worth asking and the plausible ways to answer them, is built from hands-on experience and intuition about a domain, and he values generalists because crossover ideas from other fields often turn out to be the ones that work.

  2. His readiness test is a significant accuracy delta over a weak, trial-and-error status quo. Who verifies that delta in drug discovery before patients are exposed, and what happens to the advantage when the rest of the industry adopts the same models?

Lex Fridman · Preston Stewart and Michael Kofman on the Russia-Ukraine war and the future of warfare

Russia-Ukraine War: Putin, Zelenskyy, Drones and the Future of Warfare | Lex Fridman Podcast #504

On Lex Fridman, “Russia-Ukraine War: Putin, Zelenskyy, Drones and the Future of Warfare” is a late-night, nearly three-hour conversation with two of the most closely watched analysts of the war. Michael Kofman is a senior fellow at the Carnegie Endowment who specializes in the Russian and Ukrainian militaries and hosts the Russia Contingency podcast. Preston Stewart is a West Point graduate and Afghanistan veteran who covers the war on his Preston Stewart channel and the War and Conflict podcast. Fridman walks them from the current state of the front through drone tactics, the failures of 2022, Bakhmut and the Prigozhin mutiny, and finally to leadership, endurance, and the paths to peace.

Kofman opens with a dichotomy. On the ground, 2026 has been Ukraine's best year of combat performance since the summer of 2023, and Russia's worst since the winter of that year. The Russian way of fighting hit diminishing returns around the summer of 2025, and Ukrainian forces are now running localized offensives of their own, in places gaining more terrain than the much larger summer 2023 offensive managed. At the same time, Russia has shifted weight onto an expansive strike campaign against Ukrainian infrastructure, which has broadened from power and heating plants to warehouses, data centers, schools, medical facilities, shopping malls, and trains. The open question hanging over the winter is how much cumulative damage that campaign does to Ukraine's economy and population centers, even as its army performs well.

The mechanism of that campaign has changed. What began in the fall of 2022 as combinations of cruise and ballistic missiles is now mostly saturation strikes built around large numbers of one-way attack drones, which have evolved from cheap propeller-driven models into jet-powered systems that are, in effect, light ground-launched cruise missiles. Ukraine faces both periodic mass strikes and daily pressure meant to exhaust its air defenses and disrupt economic life. Kofman adds a historical caution: strategic bombardment has a poor record of achieving maximalist political aims when the ground campaign is floundering, though political leaders reliably believe it might work for them.

Geographically, the fighting still sits in the east and southeast. There are Russian infiltrations in Sumy and fighting in Kharkiv region and around Kupyansk, while the heaviest fighting remains the Donbas, where the Ukrainian Fortress Belt around Sloviansk, Kramatorsk, Druzhkivka, and Kostiantynivka is costing Russia heavily every day. Zaporizhzhia is unusually fluid, with both sides attacking at once, and Ukrainian operations, including the Third Corps offensive near Lyman that Kofman calls Vihod, have been wiping out Russian gains from 2025. Between the lines lies a wide gray zone, roughly fifteen kilometers deep in places, where neither side holds cohesive positions and small infantry pickets mark ground that drones watch constantly.

The drone war itself is widely misunderstood, and both guests work to correct the picture. Drones inflict around eighty percent of losses, but they work in combination with minefields and artillery, which force attackers to disperse and slow down, and artillery matters most exactly when weather grounds the drones. One side may fly eight to ten thousand FPV drones a day, hundreds of thousands a month, yet almost all of them are remotely piloted by teams of two or three people. Kofman stresses that the war is manpower-intensive rather than autonomous: semi-autonomous terminal guidance exists, but it is a small share of the fight, and the autonomy story has been overhyped. The televised kill videos mislead in another way, because only successes are ever shown. In practice it takes roughly five drones to kill one soldier, ten to thirty to destroy a tank, and in one extreme case Kofman heard of, seventy-nine to kill a heavily protected one.

Territory changes hands by two very different methods. Russia has defaulted to infiltration: individual infantrymen, and earlier waves on motorcycles and ATVs, are sent five to twenty kilometers forward to slip through the gaps between Ukraine's sparse three-man posts, find a path, and accumulate behind the lines, where even a handful of infiltrators threatens the drone units operating there. An American volunteer described the tactic to Stewart as a great one if you do not care about losing soldiers, with ninety to ninety-five percent of the men lost along the way. Ukraine, which cannot afford that arithmetic, attacks differently. Its successful operations start by winning superiority in what soldiers call the kill zone: suppressing Russian drone units with strikes and artillery, interdicting their logistics, and only then advancing, whether with drone-supported mechanized units like Code 9.2 and the First Separate Assault Regiment or with three-man fire teams clearing tree line by tree line in the south.

The technology keeps mutating on a three to four month cycle. Fiber-optic drones, which trail a thin cable back to the operator and so cannot be jammed, were rolled out heavily by Russia after the Kursk offensive and now reach out to around fifty kilometers, though they remain a small share of tactical strike drones. They also give no warning, because the usual defense is a handheld detector, nicknamed the Chuika, that picks up a radio-controlled drone's own video feed, so a soldier can watch the enemy drone's view of his own vehicle on a small screen. Kofman describes the innovation dynamic as asymmetric: Ukrainian units experiment bottom-up like defense startups and tend to innovate first, while Russia, once it identifies something effective, is better at funding, standardizing, and scaling it across the force.

On what this war means for warfare generally, both guests resist easy generalization. It is a transitional war, comparable in some ways to aviation in the First World War, that began as a recognizably twentieth-century artillery war between two successor armies of the Soviet military and then rapidly became something new. Neither side has air superiority; both live under air denial, which is nothing like the permissive skies American forces enjoyed in Iraq and Afghanistan. Kofman warns that the next war's opening phase will not look like this war's attritional middle, and that which lessons transfer depends on geography, political objectives, and how a given military wants to fight. The United States, they agree, is still figuring out who owns small drones, is running a drone dominance program to find agile manufacturers, and is tactically superb, but it is an open question whether its traditional advantages deliver the same benefit on a battlefield like this one.

Asked who is winning, Stewart refuses the scoreboard framing: the two sides are fighting for different things, and wars that look one-sided for years, as with Germany and Japan in the Second World War, can flip quickly. Neither analyst sees either side near collapse, though Kofman notes that Ukraine currently holds the initiative on parts of the front, and that advantage may be a window rather than a trend. He also lays out his levels of analysis, from the tactical detail that feeds leaders' sense of bargaining leverage, through operations, to strategy, which he defines as a theory of success that reveals itself most clearly in what a side chooses not to do.

The autopsy of February 2022 is unsparing. Kofman was among those who predicted a full-scale invasion, but he says analysts over-rationalized how Russia would conduct it, missing that the plan was built on political assumptions: that Russian intelligence had prepared the ground, that troops would be greeted as liberators, and that a rapid advance on Kyiv could decapitate the government before a real war began. Putin had laid out his view of Ukraine in a July 2021 article, and he had not updated it since 2014. The West also overestimated Russia and underestimated Ukraine, partly from a professional habit of worst-casing adversaries and partly because far more was known about Russian attack plans than Ukrainian defense plans. Stewart adds the cultural layer: Western forces push decision authority down to nineteen and twenty-year-olds acting on a commander's intent, and NATO training had instilled some of that in Ukraine, while Russian units that got stuck simply stopped, which is why so many senior Russian officers were killed near the front in the opening weeks, moving traffic jams.

Bakhmut gets a long treatment as the war's great fixing point. Wagner's convict assault groups ground through the city from the summer of 2022 to May 2023 while Russian airborne troops held the flanks and artillery leveled the place, and by the end there was very little city left to take. Kofman argues the loss was never as critical as it felt: it took Russia a full year to get from Bakhmut to Chasiv Yar, the next town over. His term is Bakhmut syndrome, the pathology of defending a place past the point where it makes sense because it has acquired political significance, a risk Ukraine ran because its support in the West seemed tied to a perception of winning, and one he sees forming again around Kostiantynivka. Both guests note that the decision of when a favorable urban attrition fight becomes a trap is a judgment call that political leaders ultimately own.

The Prigozhin story follows from Bakhmut. The mutiny of June 2023 grew out of Wagner's rivalry with Defense Minister Sergei Shoigu and Chief of the General Staff Valery Gerasimov, and it was triggered when Shoigu ordered all Wagner personnel to sign contracts with the military, which would have ended Prigozhin's autonomy. The march on Rostov and then toward Moscow, Kofman argues, was less a coup attempt than a petition backed by leverage: once Putin sided against him and no elite support materialized, Prigozhin traded the advance for a stand-down. His death two months later, when his jet exploded in midair, surprised neither guest. The lasting effect is what Kofman calls the Wagnerization of the Russian military, which absorbed Wagner's convict assault model into formations like Shtorm-Z and Shtorm-V, and in doing so overspecialized in small assault actions until that, too, reached diminishing returns. Prigozhin himself said Wagner lost twenty thousand men killed at Bakhmut alone.

Some of the conversation's sharpest material concerns truth itself. Russia, Stewart says, claims territory it does not hold: a single soldier waves a flag for a drone camera, a video circulates, and maps are redrawn, including at least one fake video that Russians themselves called out. Kofman estimates Russian maps routinely sit ten to fifteen kilometers ahead of reality, and the falsification is not harmless. Units rotate in with wrong maps and get killed, fire support is planned around lines that do not exist, offensive efforts surge each October and November as commanders are pressed to actually take ground they have been claiming for months, and the distorted picture reaches Putin, shaping his view of the war. Real control, Kofman argues, means being able to use terrain for your own purposes while the enemy lacks fire control over it, a standard much of the marbled front, where Russian and Ukrainian troops sit commingled in the basements of the same city, cannot meet. Ukraine has its own reporting failures, and commanders were relieved under the previous commander-in-chief, Syrskyi, but both guests judge the distortion far worse on the Russian side, where a coercive, centralized culture punishes bearers of bad news. Stewart connects the same optimism bias to America's twenty years in Afghanistan, and Kofman notes that political leaders often resist adverse information or ask only for enough force to avoid losing on their watch.

On the two commanders in chief, the contrast is structural. Zelenskyy, in Stewart's assessment, has offloaded military decisions to the military, while Putin, though not a tactical micromanager, owns the war's central defect: a fundamental mismatch between his maximalist political goals and the military means Russia actually has. Kofman periodizes the war into its first thirty days, when the attempt to erase Ukrainian independence failed decisively and irreversibly, and everything since, an attritional war over borders, economic viability, and Ukraine's strategic orientation. Russian demands, including the rest of the Donbas and limits on Ukraine's armed forces and alliances, are tied to the performance Russia wishes it had rather than the one it is producing, while Ukraine's demands, a ceasefire along the line of control and the return of deported people and stolen children, sit close to its battlefield performance.

Endurance, then, is the real contest. Russia's economy has absorbed sanctions better than Stewart expected, and Kofman judges that Russia has the resources to keep fighting into 2027, even while spending roughly forty-four percent of its government budget, some nine to ten percent of GDP, on the war, and even as a shadow mobilization coerces more men into uniform. North Korea at one point supplied forty percent of Russia's artillery ammunition. Ukraine's dependence runs the other way: it cannot sustain the war without Western money, weapons, and intelligence, though the arrangement has shifted, with Europeans now paying for much of the American equipment. On Trump, both guests are critical. Stewart says the United States ceded its leading role, lost leverage over both parties, and left mutually beneficial cooperation with Ukraine, including on interceptors and joint production, on the table. Kofman calls the engagement fitful and deal-driven, though he credits the administration with one thing: forcing every party to state its requirements for ending the war, something neither the previous administration, whose slogan was not a plan, nor the Europeans had done.

The paths to peace look narrow. Kofman sees no Russian willingness to negotiate seriously now: Putin is likely waiting on the winter strike campaign and on spring elections in Europe, including in France, hoping Ukraine's bargaining position weakens. Partial, compartmentalized ceasefires, on energy strikes or on Black Sea shipping, are the most he finds plausible. He also warns against ceasefire romanticism: a pause without genuine security guarantees could leave Ukraine acutely vulnerable six to eight months later, after its soldiers demobilize while Russia's contract force does not, and the war that began in 2014 has already produced two failed Minsk ceasefires. Wars end, in his formulation, when both sides judge a settlement better than what they can gain by fighting, and Russia is not there.

The conversation closes on the future and on human nature. Kofman frames the war as one of Soviet succession, the product of Russia's failed transition from empire to post-imperial nation state, while holding that nothing about that failure is permanent. Stewart calls war a sickness humanity cannot kick, and both men reach for hope in the same unit, Code 9.2: a SkyFall drone that flew a wounded soldier thirty kilometers to safety in a pod slung beneath it, the man talking with the crew the whole way and pictured the next day in a hospital with his child on his lap, and an earlier rescue in which a company commander raced into the open without body armor to pull two wounded men out of a disabled ground robot. Their shared observation is that a dark sense of humor, and people who have not lost their humanity, are what carry soldiers through.

Open questions

  1. If Ukraine's current battlefield advantage is a window rather than a trend, what would have to change, in Western supply, Ukrainian manpower, or Russian adaptation, for it to hold through another winter strike campaign?
  2. Can any ceasefire be structured so that it does not simply become a rearmament pause that leaves Ukraine more vulnerable, given that Russia's contract force may not demobilize while Ukraine's soldiers go home?
  3. As drones push more of the fighting onto screens and small uncrewed systems, how much of this war's character comes from the specific conditions of air denial and attrition, and how much will actually transfer to the opening phase of the next major war?

In the o3 launch video, the model analyzed an image by zooming in, reasoning over details, inspecting what it found, and planning its next step. Reasoning plus tool use, repeated, is what they call the agentic loop.

Codex, OpenAI's general agent, pushed the idea further by giving the model a computer. Raj explains that Codex was built around a model and a harness designed together: the harness manages the instructions, the tools, the execution environment, and the context the model carries from step to step, and the model is trained to work inside that harness, so each side shapes the other. The harness itself, which is open source, is deliberately minimal and opinionated, built on general tools like the shell and the file system, on the theory that if a person can do a task on a computer, the model should be able to figure it out too. At the center sits the Responses API, which runs the loop, passes tool definitions to the model, executes its tool calls in a sandbox, and decides whether another turn happens.

Long tasks broke the single context window, so OpenAI trained compaction into both the harness and the model. As the window fills, the harness asks the Responses API to condense the conversation history into a state the work can continue from, and because the model itself understands what it means to be compacted, it knows what to keep and how to resume. That unlocked long horizon work across many context windows. The next problem was persistence: earlier models made good progress and then simply ended their turns early. Developers responded with outer loops, from Peter Steinberger cueing the model with repeated continue instructions to Geoffrey Huntley's Ralph loop, a bash loop that restarts the agent against a prompt file with a fresh context each run.

OpenAI brought that pattern into Codex as /goal, shipped with GPT-5.5. The objective is saved outside the conversation, where compaction cannot touch it, the harness tracks its status, and the agent keeps working across turns until the job is done or it reports a genuine blocker. Then, with GPT-5.6, persistence was trained into the model itself, so it checks its own work and keeps going without the nudges. Raj cites Shopify chief executive Tobi Lütke calling it the first model that no longer needed /goal, and Wharton professor Ethan Mollick, who gave Codex access to his computer and had it play Slay the Spire 2 for about five hours straight.

Eric then takes over on working with more than one agent, and his first advice is restraint: start with a single agent given a clear task and a way to check its work, and add agents only when the work gives a reason. With GPT-6, the models were trained to coordinate from the start, and under Ultra, Codex can copy itself and divide large projects among the copies, but every added agent can multiply token use without making the work faster or better. Some work divides obviously, like assigning one subagent to each of five pull requests, and some just needs a nudge, like asking for a couple of subagents to split an investigation into a tricky race condition.

His more structured patterns come next. Effort scaling routes work by difficulty: his Coordinate skill has a mid-tier model handle most tasks, escalate hard problems to a stronger model, and hand simple busywork to cheaper ones, which reduces the anxiety of picking the right model for each job even if it does not always cost less. For a weekend project porting an old game of his into Blender, he used what he calls the pair: two agents, one getting capture technology working in the old engine and one unpacking the scene in Blender, with the communication boundaries spelled out explicitly because each side's findings had to shape the other's work. Above the subagent level, Codex can also manage whole chat threads in the ChatGPT app, starting and steering separate conversations, which suits long projects whose work streams, like art, design, programming, and playtesting on a game, can each be led by a specialized thread reporting to a producer. His warning is what he calls the middle management tax: every extra rule about how agents must collaborate can stall them as they seek approval through hierarchies nobody intended.

Eric closes with a small habit that made a large difference: asking the agent to keep a compact work log holding the overall goal, the work in progress, and how the latest steering relates to both, so that after several compactions it does not drift from the original brief. He also cautions that best practices have a shelf life, and that nobody should get attached to their current workflow.

Raj returns for scale. Working on the Navier-Stokes problem, a Millennium Prize problem, OpenAI's internal model and Codex ran 10,000 agents together, exchanging 2.7 million messages and producing about 130 billion output tokens, and found a solution. The pattern, which he calls agent teams, initializes several teams with different versions of the problem and different encouraged approaches, lets them communicate within and across teams so findings are shared and duplicated effort avoided, and spawns or redirects teams as the work deepens, while the goal itself never changes. When an unforced Euler result emerged, every team was diverted onto it. The same internal setup, he says, has since resolved more than 100 longstanding problems across mathematics. His closing challenge to the audience is to take a problem they have been putting off, or one earlier models could not finish, and just build.

Open questions

  1. As persistence and coordination move into the model itself, which parts of today's harness design and loop engineering remain necessary engineering, and which quietly become dead weight?
  2. When 10,000 agents produce a claimed solution to a problem like Navier-Stokes, what does verification look like, and who checks the work at that scale?
  3. For ordinary teams, where is the practical line between multi-agent structures that genuinely help and the middle management tax, given that every added agent raises the token bill?
