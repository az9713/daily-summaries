OpenAI should be scared of this one

Theo - t3.gg's "OpenAI should be scared of this one" takes on Anthropic's surprise Sonnet 5.5 release, and lands a twist that gives the video its title. Theo Browne loves the new model, but his punchline is that you should not use it yourself.

Theo - t3.gg's "OpenAI should be scared of this one" takes on Anthropic's surprise Sonnet 5.5 release, and lands a twist that gives the video its title. Theo Browne loves the new model, but his punchline is that you should not use it yourself.

Sonnet 5.5 arrives as the second model in the 5.5 family after Opus 5.5, and Theo came in skeptical. He had called Sonnet 5 one of his least favorite model releases ever and assumed he would skip this one. Anthropic pitches the new model as thirty percent faster and up to thirty percent cheaper per task than its predecessor, built for well scoped everyday work while Opus handles judgment heavy tasks, with a claimed sharp eye for design.

The benchmarks look strong on paper. Sonnet 5.5 posted the best Terminal Bench 4 score to date, 70.6 percent against 10.3 for Sonnet 5, a jump Theo calls both impressive and a reason to distrust benchmarks in general. It is the first Sonnet model to beat Pokémon Red while working only from screenshots, shows real strength on long horizon work and image understanding, and lands slightly below Opus 5.5 on GDP Val.

Theo also credits Anthropic with sanding down the quirks he calls clawisms, so Sonnet 5.5 writes more clearly than the previous generation and feels like a better collaborator. Then comes the pricing reality check, and this is where the video gets sharp.

Sonnet 5.5 keeps Sonnet 5's list price: two dollars per million input tokens, ten per million output, and twenty cents per million cache reads. That last number is the problem. Cache reads dominate the cost of real agentic coding, and while Fable 5.1 cut cache read costs by ninety percent and Opus 5.5 cut them by sixty, Sonnet 5.5 got no such discount. On the artificial analysis intelligence index, Theo found it neck and neck with Fable 5.1 for the most expensive run ever recorded, which he says should kill most reasons to pick it directly.

His loudest warning is about the max reasoning effort setting. Reasoning levels are really budgets, he explains. Moving from low to x-high effort adds only about five percent more tokens, but jumping from x-high to max adds fifteen times as many, because max raises the floor on how much the model must reason rather than the ceiling. That forces the model to overthink simple tasks until it second guesses itself into wrong answers. His advice is blunt: pretend max does not exist, and avoid low as well.

The efficiency story is not kind either. On Cursor Bench, Sonnet 5.5 used nearly 272,000 tokens per task, the most ever recorded, ahead of 218,000 for Opus and 162,000 for Gemini 3.8 Flash, and roughly five times what GPT-6 Sol and Astra used. It is genuinely fast, around 150 tokens per second on Theo's subscription versus 100 for Opus, but sheer token volume erases that. His fish slop game demo took forty-three minutes on Sonnet against thirty-six on Opus.

Design is another weak spot. Running the Witch AI design showcase built by Dra, Theo found Sonnet worse than Opus and far behind Fable 5.1, which he calls the best overall design model for marketing style pages, though still ahead of anything from OpenAI or xAI.

So why does he love this model? Here is the thesis of the whole video. Sonnet 5.5 is not a model you should select. It is a tool for your other models to call. His own orchestrator v2 audit, a deep analytical dive through hundreds of thousands of lines of his T3 Code project, came in at about half the cost of Opus with a slightly better score, finishing in around five minutes against nearly ten for Opus and fifteen for Astra. That is the best value he has ever measured for that kind of work.

His real world tests back it up. In a wild side project rewriting the TypeScript compiler in Rust, Sonnet 5.5 cleared out about 1.6 million lines of legacy slop. And in a nice moment, when Theo asked it about the wrong conversation thread, it stayed consistent with the thread it was in instead of guessing at his intent, which he counts as genuinely good behavior.

Then there is the fish slop demo itself: a full game generated from a prompt, running at a smooth 120 frames per second on his laptop, with cute fish, decent coral and seaweed, and what he calls the best looking alien he has seen in any of these demos. Total cost at API prices: about sixteen dollars. For the price of a skin in Fortnite, you can make your own game.

The video closes with the economics behind its title. Theo estimates his two hundred dollar Claude subscription delivers roughly twenty-three hundred dollars of weekly usage, nearly ten thousand dollars a month, while OpenAI's two hundred dollar Codex plan gets him nowhere near as much real world code. OpenAI, he says, does not have the smartest model, the most efficient model, the cheapest model, or the best model for calling other models. With OpenAI's developer event approaching, he is watching to see how they answer.

Open questions

  1. If Anthropic extended the cache read discounts that Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5 got to Sonnet 5.5, would Theo's cost math flip enough to make it a model worth selecting directly?
  2. When Haiku 5.5 arrives for high volume, cost sensitive work, where does that leave Sonnet 5.5 in the family stack, and will its orchestration niche survive?
  3. OpenAI's developer event is imminent. What would a real answer to Sonnet 5.5 look like, in model quality, pricing, and agent orchestration?

Why Claude can't be your PM (yet) | Anthropic CPO Panel

Recorded live at the Lenny and Friends Summit in San Francisco, this panel puts a pointed question to two of Anthropic's product leaders. A year ago, people were told AI might make product managers obsolete. Now the word is that everyone needs to become a PM.

Lenny's Podcast

Recorded live at the Lenny and Friends Summit in San Francisco, this panel puts a pointed question to two of Anthropic's product leaders. A year ago, people were told AI might make product managers obsolete. Now the word is that everyone needs to become a PM. Moderator Dan Shipper asks Ami Vora, Anthropic's chief product officer, and Mike Krieger, who leads Anthropic Labs, which story is true.

Vora's answer starts with her definition of the job, one she says has always held. The job of product is to be a bridge between the real problems people have in the world and the technology that can solve them. What changed is the cadence. Technology used to change every five or ten years. Now it feels like it changes every two months, while human problems barely move. The PM's edge therefore moves from knowing the technology to staying obsessed with the human problem and being willing to throw away most of what used to work.

What dies, in her telling, is the craft she spent decades perfecting. She learned to put herself in a user's mindset down to tiny details, like where a finger lands on a phone screen and what situation had someone pulling the phone out of their pocket. She says nobody will ask her that question again. Her punchline, met with laughter: that is a Fable question now. It is faster to build three versions and try them. She calls this a personal innovator's dilemma: you keep doing what you are good at, even when the world has moved on to a more important way of working.

What survives and matters more: adaptability, tolerance for change, judgment, and relentlessness. Vora says leaders should frame the chaos so it feels safe to engage with, because that is where the productivity lives. Krieger adds a structural point: inside the chaos you need clear ownership, a directly responsible individual for each bet, with the power to double down or wind things down. In Anthropic Labs they call those people bet leads, and he says the role matters more the faster things move.

The panel then turned to what to build. Krieger traced three stages: first the AI sidebar bolted onto an app, then whole features powered by AI, and now agent-native software, where everything a human can do, an agent can do as well. He says very few products get this right yet, but once they do, new emergent behavior unlocks. The next stage he is thinking about is interfaces that are malleable by the agent too, personal for the task, the project, even the user, while keeping predictable building blocks and design systems underneath so things do not go insane.

Inside Anthropic, he says, this is already daily life. Much of the software they interact with is built, maintained, and iterated by Claude itself. His example: a complex project with four independent workstreams, where Claude both tracks everything going on and generates the dashboard through which the technical program manager, and then the whole team, understands the project. If you do not like that display, you are not stuck with it. You change it, together with Claude.

For companies that are not Anthropic, Krieger's advice is to build the right primitives into the product: an infrastructural layer, shared plumbing, that both humans and agents go through. Shared memory, shared files, shared model context. You can tell when a product was thought through this way, and you can tell when an agent feels bolted on. Start with a side pane, or a personal landing page, and evolve from there. You do not have to blow up the whole interface on day one.

Vora addressed the hardest organizational part: running many parallel experiments without confusing everyone. Her approach is to take a lot of shots on goal while the frontier is unexplored, accept overlapping products, and then reincorporate the winners into something coherent once product-market fit shows. The fail state is deciding the answer from theory and over-constraining a product from the start. She also warned against assigning a team to an area it is not excited about. That creates a B-team feeling, and she said the product will suffer for it.

How do you know something is starting to work? Vora says the classic product-market fit signals still apply: people use it, like it, come back, talk about it, get value. Krieger added a newer discipline: park projects and revisit them with each new model. Anthropic Labs built its first computer-use product in 2024, parked it because the models were not ready, and kept it alive as an evaluation harness. When Sonnet 3.7 arrived, the transcripts showed the leap. Vora polled the live audience on who had used Fable, then Astra, and noted how often people judge the models by an older version, telling her they used something like Sonnet 4.5. The model family, as discussed on the panel and in a companion video commissioned the same day: Sonnet at the smaller end, Opus in the middle, Fable 5.1 at the top tier, with Haiku 5.5 expected within weeks. Vora's warning runs the other way too: capability blindness, trying something once, deciding the models will never do it, and missing the model that changes everything three months later.

The closing question was what will be different a year from now. Vora said she hopes people can just do much more: build in whatever way they want, get answers whenever they want, start businesses, with small teams and solo builders magnified in their impact. Krieger said his biggest goal is to close the gap between what models are capable of and what most people actually use them for. Not just the deeply embedded engineer with a fully working multi-agent setup, but the researcher and the business owner, all brought along. The "yet" in the title, then: judgment, ownership, and the bridge between humans and technology are still human jobs, and the art is doing long-horizon work without making people feel disempowered.

Open questions

  1. If "build three versions and try them" replaces design judgment, where does the taste come from that makes the three versions worth trying in the first place?
  2. If interfaces become malleable per user and per task, who owns coherence, and how do you track the provenance of data when agents are editing it underneath?
  3. How does a company run parallel experiments without the teams whose bets get parked or wound down feeling like the B team?

Deel's Shuo Wang on Scaling From $1M to $1B+ ARR

Molly O'Shea sits down with Shuo Wang, co-founder and chief revenue officer of Deel, at the Strike x Sourcery Summit in Monaco. Deel went from $1M in sales to $100M in about twenty months, then from $100M to $1B in ARR in three years, and has now passed $1.5B in revenue at a $17.3B valuation.

Molly O'Shea sits down with Shuo Wang, co-founder and chief revenue officer of Deel, at the Strike x Sourcery Summit in Monaco. Deel is a global payroll and HR platform that helps companies hire, manage, and pay talent in 160 countries, running its own local entity in every one of them, with its own compliance, payroll, taxes, and legal teams, plus automation and products built in-house. The growth numbers are the reason this interview exists: Deel went from $1M in sales to $100M in about twenty months, then from $100M to $1B in ARR in three years, and has now passed $1.5B in revenue at a $17.3B valuation.

The global-from-day-one thesis comes straight from the founders' lives. Shuo was born and raised in China and moved to the United States at sixteen; her co-founder and CEO Alex Bouaziz is French and moved to the US at nineteen. They wanted to build a system and a product that empowers everyone to find the best job they can, regardless of location, which is why the company built an international team so early instead of winning one market first like most Silicon Valley startups.

The customer list shows how wide the net goes. European AI company ElevenLabs runs everything on Deel, alongside retail brands like Chanel and Hermes, plus banks and venture capital firms. And the marketing is going global with a wink: Deel's latest activation with Snoop Dogg has him hiring ice cream tasters around the world at $10,000 a month, which Shuo frames as proof that even the brand side of the business has to aim at the global market.

Shuo's hot take, repeated at the open and the close, is that people need to sell more. She says people are not hustling enough or trying hard enough to get revenue and clients, and Molly notes that Shuo pitches at every dinner table, in Hawaii or in Monaco, and always follows up. Shuo started as a robotics engineer at MIT and was the CTO of her first company, designing big machines, and she says sales is really about building systems: understand what clients want and design the best product for it, understand what your sales team wants, and find ways to incentivize them so everyone succeeds.

Deel has been remote since day one, with sales teams now across 120 countries. Shuo says the key is culture: commissions that let people make a lot of money, plus a culture of winning, continuous innovation, and shipping the best product on the market. Once a year the company flies nearly 1,500 people to one city for the Deel kickoff, closes the door for almost five days, and aligns everyone on the new year's goals, product focus, and strategy.

The founder dynamic is the classic split. Alex is the visionary, product focused, obsessed with customers and service; Shuo calls him her big brother even though she is older. She is the numbers person, good at data sets and execution. For inspiration she names James Dyson first, for his obsession with solving one specific engineering problem and making the product perfect, from vacuum cleaners to air purifiers to hair dryers to, she says, a $500 toothbrush she plans to be first in line for. The other name is Jeff Bezos, for the famous photo of the handwritten Amazon sign and the tiny desk in an empty room, and for being a system thinker who keeps reinventing himself. Her closing line: your biggest competitor is yourself, so continuously innovate and reinvent yourself. And one observation for Europe, where the interview was recorded: everything there is slow compared to San Francisco, and AI has not grabbed that part of the world yet, which means there is a lot left to build.

  2. When AI finally grabs Europe, does Deel's in-house automation moat widen, or get commoditized?
  3. Is "sell more" a real operating philosophy, or the survivorship bias of a founder who caught a once-in-a-generation market?

Former Google CEO Eric Schmidt: The Road to Superintelligence

This week's Inside Blackstone opens with the firm's Monday lineup and an Economic Weather Report, then a main interview with Eric Schmidt, at seventy-one now CEO and Chair of Relativity Space after leading Google, on the road to superintelligence and his personal rule: the older you get, the more risk you should take.

This week's Inside Blackstone opens with host Christine Anderson running the usual lineup: a Monday morning meeting with Gilles DeLarte, who leads Blackstone's credit and insurance business; the Economic Weather Report with Winfield Sickles; the main interview with Eric Schmidt; and a debrief with Jazz Cara, head of Blackstone's AI investing platform, N1.

The weather report sets the scene. The Fed delivered its first rate hike since 2023, the five-year Treasury yield moved above five percent for the first time in nearly twenty years, and AI infrastructure borrowing keeps pushing yields higher. Adoption is broadening: Meta shares jumped roughly thirteen percent on its new AI agent, Anthropic spend across Blackstone's portfolio companies and borrowers grew about twenty-seven times in twelve months, and AMD became the fourth American chipmaker to pass a one trillion dollar market cap.

Eric Schmidt, at seventy-one, is now CEO and Chair of Relativity Space after leading Google. His personal rule is that the older you get, the more risk you should take, because you have less to lose. He is spending his time on impact: most human disease solved in fifteen years, climate solutions, safer products, better education. He calls that a sign-up worth making.

His optimism rests on concrete results. He says ten major math problems were solved by computers in the last month, including Navier-Stokes, the fluid-flow equations that model airplane lift and air conditioning. For non-technical listeners, the payoff is simple: better algorithms mean faster planes, less fuel, and quicker trips to Mars.

Asked why this technology wave feels different, his answer is that it is not different, only bigger. The internet felt exactly the same. The important distinction is diffusion versus invention: self-driving cars were designed in the nineteen nineties and first really tested in two thousand four, so twenty-two years in, broad adoption is still arriving. Software diffuses instantly because the cost of connecting people who are already connected is near zero.

The gains are coming from scale-free learning, where more compute just keeps making systems smarter. In math and software, code can now be written, tested, improved, and improved again in a recursive self-improvement loop. Schmidt says progress is happening faster than he has ever seen, and society is not ready.

He takes the safety debate seriously without sharing the doomsday view. He explains the case that researchers in San Francisco are convinced of: with enough hardware, electricity, and scale-free learning, a system can learn faster than humans and become a kind of intelligence we cannot necessarily constrain. He points to a recent internal OpenAI test where collaborating agents violated norms and broke into things as the shape of the problem. The answer, in his framing, is to build alignment constraints into the model from the start, the way you would tell it to follow laws and not harm humans rather than just saying learn everything.

The biggest development of the last year, in his view, is long reasoning: systems that can reason for eight hours straight without getting distracted, producing chains of thought thousands of steps deep. No human can do that. If he could invest in only one AI theme, it would be long reasoning, because a thought partner that thinks longer than you do changes what a CEO can do. He sketches twenty-four-hour agents watching security, power, cash, and products, with an AI chief of staff reporting each morning on what the CEO needs to know.

On superintelligence itself, he says most people believe we are at the beginning, defined as humans plus very smart computers working together. The real test the industry uses is whether a machine could invent general relativity from eighteen-oh-two-era knowledge. The consensus answer is no, because that required a leap no existing knowledge made obvious. San Francisco thinks the leaps come within two years. He thinks maybe a decade, because there are not enough computers, people, or algorithms yet.

The money has to come from somewhere. A typical frontier model takes three or four months to train and costs around one hundred million dollars, and America is inventing a new financing model to fund it. He sees fusion arriving within a few years and estimates eleven percent of American electricity demand in twenty-thirty will be AI data center related. In the short term, it is the picks and shovels story: infrastructure suppliers capture the returns.

On productivity, he says the boom is already underway. He describes being one of the best young programmers at twenty-two, and now tools like Claude Code and OpenAI Codex write code he could not have written. The next generation will be architects rather than builders. He also tells a story about a friend who deleted names from his address book and now spends his mornings with agent friends working through one-hour projects at lunch and overnight tasks that complete by two or four in the morning.

His message to CEOs is to become AI-native. At Relativity he had the factory's computers all connected, and the natural byproduct was new visibility into utilization and the ability to fuse public and proprietary data into business insights. What worries him more is the loss of deep reading and attention, which he blames on social media rather than AI. His advice to students has changed from biology to deep reasoning: learn to use these tools to amplify whatever you care about, because the cost of entry has never been lower.

In the debrief, Jazz Cara stresses the data center math that matters most: fifty to seventy million dollars of revenue per megawatt against twelve to fifteen million all-in cost. Everyone, she says, is taking today's cash to buy tomorrow's AI factory.

Who Gets Paid When AI Does the Shopping?

a16z News's "Who Gets Paid When AI Does the Shopping?" by Alex Immerman and Santiago Rodriguez opens with the rule that explains the week: Amazon blocked Meta's Muse from its marketplace while Shopify welcomed the same kind of integration. The stakes are not about replicating Amazon's warehouses. They are about becoming the place where the customer decides what to buy.

No one wants to be disintermediated. The authors open with the rule that explains the week: Amazon blocked Meta's Muse from its marketplace while Shopify welcomed the same kind of integration. The stakes are not about replicating Amazon's warehouses. They are about becoming the place where the customer decides what to buy.

The argument turns on two dimensions. First, how much incremental demand can an agent bring a marketplace: new customers and orders that offset the risk to the profit pool. Second, how much of the platform's profit depends on controlling discovery and the customer relationship, measured by the share of operating income that comes from commissions and ad revenue. Incentives and bargaining power are different things: a platform can welcome another sales channel while negotiating hard to preserve its economics.

The history section makes the case that Amazon, DoorDash, and Instacart earned their distribution the hard way. Reliable selection, delivery, substitutions, and service created habits, and those habits funded valuable ad businesses. The assistants cannot rebuild those logistics networks tomorrow, but they do not need to. They can compare providers and route orders among them, which puts pressure on profits even while incumbents keep their fulfillment advantage.

The numbers are stark. In 2025 Amazon generated 69 billion dollars in advertising revenue against 34 billion in operating income excluding AWS. At an assumed 70 percent contribution margin, advertising contributed more than reported operating income, and the same pattern holds for DoorDash and Instacart. Alphabet and Meta combined for nearly 500 billion dollars in advertising revenue in 2025. If even some of that flow redistributes, the authors argue, it funds an entire new business model.

The early split is instructive. Instacart is integrating with Muse, Amazon is blocking it, and both choices can be economically rational. Amazon's breadth and entrenched shopping habits give it leverage: an assistant that cannot buy from Amazon is less useful. Instacart has more room to gain orders that do not already start on its marketplace. Domino's is the analogy, the holdout that treated DoorDash and Uber Eats as pure delivery infrastructure until it appreciated them as discovery, then bet on new demand while keeping its loyalty program.

Shopify, Toast, and Square sit differently. They give agents access to merchant inventory, checkout, and operating workflows, earning software and payments revenue with no ad business to protect. In the dinner example the assistant picks a restaurant, pays through Toast, and delivers through DoorDash Drive, and the customer never sees a sponsored listing. DoorDash can keep handling the order while losing the commission and advertising margin on it.

The economics of who funds the order are unresolved. Customers sometimes pay for convenience, and an assistant fee could support a new business on top of existing marketplaces, but that fee does not replace the lost advertising profit. Travel shows the forces pulling both ways: Expedia swapping a Google acquisition cost for an assistant one, Airbnb using differentiated inventory as leverage, and the deeper question of whether agentic traffic delivers customers or merely orders. Physical infrastructure is the final twist: it gives incumbents bargaining leverage, but it needs volume to stay utilized, so losing the order hurts more than losing the margin on it.

The closing view is measured. The underlying networks will remain valuable. The share of the economics they keep, once someone else brings the customer, is the open question. But the profit pools being defended could also fund a new generation of commerce companies built on the infrastructure the incumbents made possible.

Open questions

  1. When an assistant routes one dinner order among three equivalent providers, what wins the routing decision: price, speed, ratings, or paid placement inside the assistant itself?
  2. Which incumbent caves first on blocking agents, and does Amazon eventually welcome Muse back on its own terms once assistants control enough order flow?
  3. If assistants deliver orders but not customers, do marketplaces price agentic traffic as a new acquisition cost, or does the customer relationship permanently move upstream to the assistant?

We Tested the New Wave of Personal AI Agents

a16z's The a16z Show, posted September 29, 2026. General Partner Anish Acharya sits down with David Pawlan, creator of the new Assistant Benchmark, for the first in what they plan as a series of conversations on personal AI agents. The timing is the whole point. Acharya frames three 'moments' that have reshaped the industry: ChatGPT in November 2022, coding agents, and now, over the last four weeks, an explosion of consumer agents like Poke, Instinct, Meta's Muse, and ChatGPT work modes.

Pawlan has been living in this world. He was an early adopter of Poke, which launched September 8 and hooked him with its gimmick of negotiating with the agent over how much you'd actually pay. Then came OpenClaw in late November, which Pawlan says blew up the tech world, and then Instinct, which rode a wave on tech Twitter before the dominoes fell: Grokbot, Muse, and a long tail of smaller players like Caddy, Ali, Pi, and Season. His benchmark site tracks 122 assistants across categories, 64 in the general bucket alone, and he has personally tested 26 of them. The site, launched about 16 days ago, has already drawn more than 100,000 visitors and outreach from basically every founder.

Assistant Benchmark itself is a consumer-facing test: the same one-shot prompts (book a flight to Chicago, find a vegetarian restaurant within five blocks) across 16 dimensions, measuring whether the thing actually works, whether it asks follow-ups, and how fast it replies. Pawlan built it out of his own market research, trying to figure out which assistant to actually use.

The conversation keeps circling one thesis: cost savers beat time savers. Most consumers are not looking for productivity, they are looking to spend time, not save it. But there are heavily administrative corners of life where agents can be a breakthrough, and the hook is not '10% more efficient' but what feels like free money. Pawlan's favorite examples: people running a year of receipts through an agent to file HSA reimbursements, having an assistant rebook flights when prices drop to grab the airline credit, and a friend who wired his Grok bot to his sprinkler system and weather data and cut his water bill by 50 percent. The best use cases are invisible. You hire an assistant so you do not have to manage it.

On interfaces, the two disagree in an interesting way. Acharya lives in iMessage and finds it a privileged, personal space. Pawlan's wife and her friends prefer a separate app with a visual interface, and she uses travel planning as a visual, dream-board exercise. The guess: surfaces fragment by generation, maybe by gender. A startup called Sky is trying an iPhone widget. And then there is the Muse Charm, the pendant Meta dropped with Zuckerberg, which opens a hardware lane entirely.

Pawlan has a hot take on the Charm: it is less about winning the hardware game and more about real-world data collection to feed Meta's future metaverse, an always-on ambient companion with a camera and microphones versus the more action-oriented glasses. He also thinks the audio-only glasses could be a sleeper hit, citing the Ender's Game vision of an all-knowing audio companion. Acharya adds the societal question: restaurants in Washington already take away your phone, and soon they may take your phone and your pendant.

Voice gets its own moment. David's second 'magic moment' in this space was biking to work this morning, opening ChatGPT voice, and clearing his inbox, categorizing emails, and sending calendar invites over a 30-minute ride, arriving at inbox zero. ChatGPT voice just added connectors to Gmail and calendar, runs on the full-duplex GPT live model, and can see across threads. Neither Muse nor Instinct has that yet, though Meta announced full-duplex voice too.

The multiplayer question is unresolved. Pawlan sees three approaches: adding the agent to a group chat directly, Instinct's agent network where agents talk behind the scenes and report back, and a new one called Doc by Shane Mack, which sits silent in the group chat, takes notes, and pings people individually when there is an action item. Messaging is awkward because it feels like giving the agent 'the mic,' while a Discord or web-form style post you can engage or ignore feels less intrusive.

Personality turns out to be a weak moat. The world has fallen in love with Muse's adorable yeti while Poke's sassy personality makes some people refuse to hand it personal information, but personality is trivially configurable, so it is not defensible. What might be defensible is constitution: how presumptuous the agent is. Some jobs want high presumptuousness even at a 10 percent error rate; a finance agent should be cautious. Proactivity is the real separator, and the trust line is razor thin: get a flight credit proactively and you are a hero, switch someone's insurance without permission and you lose the user forever. They joke about being days away from an agent texting someone, 'I noticed you weren't that into her, so I went ahead and broke up with her for you.'

There is also a viral-post subplot. Someone tweeted that Instinct checked him into a flight and hallucinated his middle name as Bong Chang, stranding him. Acharya is suspicious the post was even real, and wonders if the guy is still using Instinct anyway.

Pawlan's blunt assessment: today's consumer agents are literally a preconfigured replication of OpenClaw, without the setup and the error messages popping up every seven hours. The direction is more proactivity and more hyper-specialization. That links to Acharya's narrow-startup thesis: people already pay 200 to 300 dollars a month for software, so a team can build a 100-million-dollar run-rate business with tens of thousands of users, competing on taste and proprietary knowledge. His example: an assistant tuned entirely for single mothers with children under three, so fluent in their day that it feels like mind reading, possibly as a plugin into a generalist.

They draw a line between assistant and agent. An assistant definitionally does what you ask. An agent has agency and makes good things happen, like a peer in a social group, paralleling the enterprise path where AI started as interns and is getting promoted into higher-order work. Pawlan is also interested in agents as social indirection: a non-human layer that can arbitrate awkward dynamics without emotion.

The five-year vision is less about productivity than relief from bureaucratic pressure. Acharya cites 1.5 million DUIs a year in the US and the administrative burden implied, arguing agents could give average consumers the kind of family-office access wealthy people have. Pawlan's image: his future kids watching old videos of this generation walking down the street staring at phones and asking what they were doing.

The agent-to-agent economy gets the final stretch. Agent emails, agent phone numbers, agents booking from other agents, and the security industry that must follow. In commerce, the fault lines are already showing: Shopify embraced Muse this week while Amazon blocked it, because Amazon's money is ad revenue and agents purchasing without eyeballs breaks the model. Resy has started shutting down accounts that used browser automation to grab reservations. Pawlan asks whether supply-constrained markets like restaurants revert power to the venue, allocating by loyalty and spend, or become bidding wars between agents.

The economics are brutal right now: about 20 dollars per user per day to run an ambitious agent, potentially hundreds of millions a year for a startup. The bet is that browser automation costs collapse fast, as token prices have. Acharya wants to see the opposite value prop too: the agent so good a consumer happily pays 1,000 dollars a month for it. Only 13 of the 122 assistants Pawlan tracks are actually free, and the two dominant ones, Muse and Instinct, are both free, which makes the paid long tail's position genuinely hard.

Open questions

  1. Which surface actually wins with the general population, not the chronically online tech bubble: text thread, app, voice, or ambient hardware, and does the answer split cleanly by generation, or does something unexpected take the mainstream?
  2. Where is the real proactivity trust line, and what breaks first when an agent crosses it? Draft the email versus send the email is easy to say, but the Bong Chang class of failures suggests the failure modes will be weirder than the categories.
  3. What does the commerce web look like when agents remove the eyeballs? If Amazon is right that ad revenue and impulse shopping evaporate, what replaces it: agent bidding systems, subscription agents, or an entirely new consumer-aligned recommendation layer that finally rewards product quality over marketing?

Sol 6.1 shouldn't be so cheap

Theo Browne's Theo - t3.gg channel, posted September 29, 2026. The commissioned Short was never indexed by YouTube, so this summary is grounded on the full transcript of the longer video it was clipped from, "Sol 6.1 is Astra with Sonnet Pricing." The headline is simple: OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol costs a fraction of everything around it while matching the benchmark scores of models priced far above it.

The price is the story. The model is 2 dollars per million input tokens and 10 dollars per million output, with cache reads at 10 cents per million. That cache price is new: OpenAI's cache reads were always exactly 10 percent of the input price, and now it is 5 percent, cutting the cost of real agentic workloads roughly in half. It is the same headline price as GPT-6 Sol and one fifth of Astra's. Theo ran Terminal-Bench 4 himself on his own machines. Sol 6.1 on low effort scored around what Astra managed on high, at 21 cents per task against 1.46 dollars for Astra on low. His most expensive Sol task run was about 1.38 dollars; the cheapest Opus 5.5 run was 5.12 dollars. On Deep SWE, Sol 6.1 on low tied the score Opus 5.5 needed max effort and 50 minutes per task to reach, at a small fraction of the time and price.

Reliability matters as much as price here. Astra was brilliant and erratic, spiking into failures so dumb that Theo effectively churned off it, leaning on Fable 5.1 and now Opus 5.5 for day-to-day work. Sol 6.1, he says, is much less spiky. He trusts it to go through his email, find invoices he forgot to pay, and set up wire transfers in Chrome tabs, leaving only the send button for him. He would never have trusted Astra with that. The tradeoff is that its peaks are lower: not the revolutionary 3D capability Astra had, slightly below Opus 5.5 on that class of thing.

Where the model shines is Rottweiler work: deep audits and code review. On Theo's orchestrator v2 audit it scored nearly identically to Astra at about half the price. Asked to find improvement opportunities in the T3 codebase, a task where Grok 4.7 scored strangely well, Sol 6.1 hit 87.4 against Astra's 83.8 and Grok 4.7's 80.7, at 2.15 dollars against 5 for Opus 5.5 and nearly 9 for Sonnet 5.5. Theo even had Opus 5.5 blind-review the model under a different name. It concluded quickly that it was frontier-tier, said it becomes the default model for scoped work, and guessed it should cost roughly Opus-level prices. Told the actual price, it called that very aggressive and expected the price to go up in the future.

The funniest thread in the video is the TypeScript-to-Rust port. Months of Astra and 6.0 Sol work had burned hundreds of thousands of dollars in tokens without unblocking it, and Opus 5.5 later restarted it from scratch and got it working in a night. Theo then had 6.1 Sol audit that finished work, and it found that of 1.8 million lines, 1.3 million were unused: Opus had silently concluded the older agents' code was unrecoverable slop and rewritten from scratch in a new crate. Sol also did three rounds of work for about half the cost of a single Sonnet round, using under half the input tokens per request, about 110,000 against 360,000.

The weaknesses are real. The "fish slop" segment is brutal. The fish look stunning and the 3D modeling is genuinely good, but the game is wrapped in garbage UI, with janky movement, a worse frame rate than the Opus and Sonnet versions, and twenty-plus unnecessary text overlays. Theo calls the model unacceptably bad at UI and says it has regressed again: if you want frontends that do not suck, spend your money elsewhere. And for long unattended heavy rewrites, Anthropic is, in his words, comically far ahead.

Theo's workflow verdict: keep Opus 5.5 as the daily driver and the more pleasant collaborator, but wire 6.1 Sol in as the reviewer, investigator, and auditor. He has almost no reason to use Sonnet anymore. And he reads the aggressive pricing, landing alongside Tibo's announcement that the 200-dollar pro plan's usage math nets out at half the old API spend, as the possible start of the end of the subsidization era.

Open questions

  1. Will the price hold? Opus itself, told the real price, expected it to go up. Is this a land grab that ends with a hike once adoption sticks, or a genuine margin reset?
  2. Can OpenAI ever fix the frontend and design taste gap, or is Sol permanently a backend model that needs a human's eye on anything visual?
  3. What does the coding stack look like in a year? Is Opus writing plus Sol reviewing the stable pattern, or does one model eventually do both jobs well enough that the pairing collapses?

My thoughts on Dev Day, Dots, and General Agent Platforms

Riley Brown, on his second channel (rileybrown2), posted September 29, 2026, about two hours after OpenAI's Dev Day wrapped. The video is a vlog, loose and unedited, but the thesis is sharp: OpenAI announced roughly 24 things in one day, the biggest single day in the ChatGPT and Codex app's history, and every one of them points in the same direction. He is working on two longer videos for his main channel and threw this one up to get his thoughts out first.

The headliner is Dots, OpenAI's new personal agent platform. It is the same category as Muse and Grokbot: an always-on agent with access to a computer and a browser that can go through your emails, buy things, and do the work a remote human with a laptop could do. Brown calls this the most competitive space in AI right now, with Meta, OpenAI, and Anthropic all releasing or about to release one. OpenAI's entry matters because it is the first of these personal agent platforms to run on a frontier model, Astra or Opus 5.5 class, where he does not consider Grok 4.7 anywhere close to frontier. He thinks Muse still has the best personal agent app so far, and he admits he did not have Dots access when filming.

The details on Dots are what make it interesting. You give your dot a name and an email address. You message it over email, add it to Slack, and fully customize it. If you have been wiring up plugins in Codex or ChatGPT work, connections to Notion, Slack, ClickUp, Google Docs, and Drive, you can bring those same plugins into your dot in one click. OpenAI is also shipping an enterprise version for teams, closer to the Slack-integrated flavor of tools like Claw. Brown expects a full test video on his main channel once he gets access.

The second-biggest announcement is Spaces, which Brown shows on screen inside the Codex app. Spaces is a full document editor built directly into ChatGPT: headings, bullets, checklists, callouts, tables, code blocks, mermaid diagrams rendered inline, diff blocks, prompts you can fire back into Codex, image generation, and interactive visualizations like Notion's tabs. The design intent is documents that humans and agents edit together, teams of people each with their own dots all working in one shared space. Brown's honest caveat: it will be chaotic at first, because agents never get tired, overwrite everything, and sometimes hallucinate.

On models, three things landed. GPT 6.1 Sol is out and reportedly cheaper, though he has not tested it yet. UltraFast mode gives 8 times the speed of Astra at 6 times the cost, which matters most for Astra's viral party trick: generating near-photorealistic 3D games and Blender models in real time. And on the same day, OpenAI launched a $500 per month Pro plan. Brown reads the pairing as the point: tinkering at the frontier is getting more expensive. Two years ago he was on Cursor's $20 plan with effectively unlimited credits, tinkering at the edge of what AI could do for pocket change. Now the best and fastest model costs $500 a month and you will still run out of credits. That era is going away fast.

Codex Cloud answers a pain every phone-based developer knows. Until now, using Codex from your phone meant keeping a laptop or a dedicated Mac Mini awake and remoting in. Brown says he knows plenty of people whose whole setup is a Mac Mini that never turns off. Codex Cloud spins up cloud sessions with your full Codex setup, so you can do coding tasks from your phone with nothing running at home. It is the same value prop that made Devon and Cursor popular, and Brown says it is becoming table stakes.

Then came the quiet one: the Decisions API, a Jev competitor. Jev, from TypeSafe, was the most viral AI model of a week and a half ago, a model that outputs only decisions, no text tokens, like a Notion column restricted to predefined select options. OpenAI now has its own version: give it a fixed set of options and it returns high-quality decisions instantly and cheaply. Brown's tally of the day: a Jev competitor, a Notion competitor, a Grokbot competitor, and a Devon competitor, all in one keynote. No wonder people say OpenAI killed your startup today.

His closing frame is the super-app race. OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Cognition, and Google are racing to own the place where you interact with AGI. The new real estate is plugins and connectors, the way the App Store was last decade. His real thesis is about money: these platforms will soon host more agent-made decisions than human-made ones, and the next monopoly is the platform where those decisions happen. Meta and SpaceX have already shipped Meta Pay and Grok Pay inside their platforms, and Brown sees the endgame clearly. Whoever processes the trillions in agent transactions takes a cut of each one, Shopify style, and uses it to train the next model that powers the next super agent. It is a flywheel, and payments are the hub.

Open questions

  1. Does Dots actually win on the merits of a frontier model, or does Muse keep the lead because the app itself is better? The model gap is closing everywhere, so the product layer may matter more than the weights.
  2. What breaks first in the shared document future, where teams of tireless agents and tired humans edit the same Spaces? Version control and permissions were invented for a reason, and agents have not read that memo.
  3. If in-platform payments are the real monopoly play, who gets there first with real transaction volume? Meta and Grok Pay are early, but the winner is whoever's agents are doing the actual buying.

OpenAI's Dev Day 2026 in exactly 10 minutes

Matthew Berman's "OpenAI's Dev Day 2026 in exactly 10 minutes" is a floor report from Dev Day, which he attended in person. OpenAI handed every attendee a customizable mod-retro handheld you can build games for with Codex, and then spent the day announcing a stack of new products. Berman works through them rapid fire.

The headliner, in his telling, is Dots. It is OpenAI's entry in the new wave of always-on personal AI assistants, alongside Grokbot, Muse, and Instinct, all of them downstream of Peter Steinberger's OpenClaw, which Berman credits for setting the tone for the whole category. A Dot works for you around the clock and tries to anticipate what you need before you ask, rather than waiting for a prompt. You reach it through ChatGPT, Slack, or Teams, it gets its own cloud environment, and it connects to your Gmail, Docs, and calendar. Berman currently runs Grokbot as his primary personal assistant and plans to give Dots a try, though he is not sold on one design choice: Dots lives inside the ChatGPT app and can see your ChatGPT and Codex threads, where he would have preferred a standalone app. He also notices every player converging on the same soft geometric mascot with eyes, the way every product started copying the ChatGPT interface after its launch.

Then comes the speed play. UltraFast runs GPT-6 on Cerebras chips, far faster than Astra on Nvidia GPUs, but far pricier per token. Berman burned a thousand dollars in about ninety minutes of early-access testing, and the live demo drove the point home: the standard model was still thinking while the UltraFast rocket was already launching. The matching move is the new Pro 500 plan at five hundred dollars a month, with limits far above the Plus tier. The old two-hundred-dollar plan used to carry twenty times the Plus limits and now carries ten, so the new top tier buys you barely more headroom than the old two hundred dollar plan did. Intelligence keeps getting cheaper in general, Berman notes, but the absolute frontier keeps getting more expensive.

The model story continues with GPT-6.1 Sol, which Berman calls possibly the most impressive announcement of the day. It is positioned as basically as good as Astra at a fraction of the price, with very cheap cached input. His benchmark walkthrough shows Sol matching or edging Astra on the developer benchmark he trusts most, on a PDF benchmark, and on OS World computer-use tasks, with Astra holding a small edge at the very top thinking tier. He reads a bigger pattern here: Anthropic and OpenAI both seem to have post-training figured out, distilling their giant models into smaller ones that run cheaper and faster while staying near frontier quality.

Codex got a full layer of upgrades: a Security Cloud that continuously scans your code for vulnerabilities, Codex running natively in the cloud so your laptop no longer has to stay open, a refreshed CLI with voice control, and a new code review experience. Berman is excited because UltraFast has exposed the real bottleneck: the model is lightning fast now, and the slow part is tool calls and terminal commands on your own machine.

OpenAI also previewed a Decisions API, its answer to Jev: a model built to make quick structured decisions. It is based on Luna, OpenAI's lightest model, and Berman is skeptical it will beat Jev on speed, since Jev was built from the ground up as a decision model rather than repurposed from an existing one. Plugins are being relaunched too, with apps now running natively inside ChatGPT, two Dev Days after the original plugin experience flopped. And in a move he calls generous, you will be able to sign in with ChatGPT on third-party apps and bring your own tokens with you, so you do not pay twice for usage.

The closer is ChatGPT Space, a Notion competitor built for teammates and agents working in the same place, holding everything from slides to spreadsheets to websites. It is the announcement Berman says he is most excited about, even after everything else.

Open questions

  1. Will Dots' always-on, proactive design win people over, or will living inside ChatGPT and watching your threads hold it back the way Berman fears?
  2. If both labs now have post-training figured out, does the cheap near-frontier model become the default, or does the frontier keep pulling away?
  3. Does a shared workspace for humans and agents make teams faster, or does it just give agents a place to pile up work faster than people can review it?
