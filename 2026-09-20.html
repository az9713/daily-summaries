Open Jev Models Are Here!!

Sam Witteveen benchmarks six open Jev reimplementations — SemIf, Nimble, Decider, Ortega’s OpenJev, DiffusionGemma, NanoJev, plus Layla — and finds them closer than expected on the easy stuff, with a real gap on the hard stuff.

Sam Witteveen’s “Open Jev Models Are Here!!” opens with a small spectacle: a flood of open-source projects that sprang up within days of the Jev announcement, all trying to replicate what Jev does with weights you can run yourself. Where a day earlier there were two or three attempts, by recording time there were twenty or thirty, and several are already scoring close to Jev itself. That explosion is the frame for everything that follows: what makes Jev special, how close the open versions get, and which one you should actually try.

Jev, in Witteveen’s telling, is a universal classifier with perhaps a little regression thrown in. Classification itself is old — but every classic classifier needs training or fine-tuning on your specific use case; change the labels and you train again. There have been zero-training attempts before: back in 2019, researchers reframed classification as entailment, something BERT-era models handled well. The idea never took off because those models lacked enough world knowledge in their weights to generalize. Whatever model Jev runs on evidently has it — and that, Witteveen argues, is the real story: Jev has people thinking hard about how much work can be offloaded from a reasoning LLM to fast, cheap classification.

To measure closeness, someone has already built JevBench, scoring systems on intelligence, calibration, speed, and cost. Jev itself sits at 75.3 overall, with SemIf right behind, then a diffusion model called DJev at 74.3, then Layla. GPT-5.6 Luna scores far higher on pure intelligence, but at roughly six times Jev’s cost and far slower — System 2 still wins the hard tasks, but you pay for it. Witteveen flags with open disbelief that Jev’s terms of service reportedly forbid benchmarking it against other models.

The bulk of the video is a live demo bench of six open implementations, all running on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell in a Dell T2 Pro Max workstation — sponsored compute, courtesy of Dell. First is SemIf, originally called OpenJev: a frozen Qwen 3.5 4B with no training at all. The trick is the prefill readout — put the question and options in the prompt, don’t let the model generate, and instead softmax over the logits for the option tokens. Witteveen’s guess in his previous video was that Jev does essentially this, and many builders seem to have converged on the same idea.

Second is Bespoke Nimble, the same readout trick but with a LoRA on a Qwen 3.5 9B trained on fewer than 3,000 examples — explicitly not distilled from Jev. The interesting part is the data recipe, contrastive data curation: write two nearly identical examples that differ in one fact so the right answer flips. The example is a refund rule — only Mira can authorize refunds — and flipping the signatory from Mira to Noah flips the decision. On Bespoke’s own holdout set the adapter hits about 90 percent against the base model’s 66 and Jev’s 93, but on JevBench’s hard tier it manages only around 44 — strong on what it was trained on, unproven beyond it.

Third is Mapika’s Decider, built on the much smaller Qwen 3.5 2B. It reads the hidden state at each answer slot rather than output logits, serves the same wire format as Jev’s TypeSafe API — choice, score, nul — and averages 33 milliseconds per answer. The catch is calibration: Decider hedges in the middle where Jev reads as confident. Fourth is Alex Ortega’s OpenJev, built on the entailment idea and, unusually, taking images as input — shown answering true/false statements about UI screenshots and playing Doom and Minecraft through choice-classification. Fifth is DiffusionGemma, a 26B diffusion model that runs a denoising step and reads the log probabilities of the answer slots. Throughput is startling — sixteen support tickets in what feels like an instant — though Witteveen feels it is undertrained relative to similar-size models. The bonus entry is NanoJev, a tiny 600-million-parameter model playing Snake as pure choice classification — and playing genuinely well.

One more gets added late: Layla, built on modern BERT Large at about 420 million parameters — huge in 2018, tiny now — multilingual across roughly 100 languages, with a Google Colab anyone can try. Layla lags on accuracy, which Witteveen reads as a generalization gap, and he notes the bittersweet write-up from its creator, frustrated that nobody paid attention to this research for over a year until a well-funded lab got the credit.

The recommendations are crisp. Zero training on a model you already run? Use the SemIf-style readout. A small server speaking the Jev wire format? Try Decider. A narrow policy decision? Take Nimble’s contrastive recipe and run it on your own data. Images? Ortega’s build. The honest limit: on genuinely hard stuff — multi-hop reasoning, date arithmetic — none of these work well. Witteveen’s proposed trick is a cascade: let the fast System-1 model take everything first, and pass only low-confidence cases up to a reasoning model — System-1 speed and price for most of the workload, System-2 judgment where it counts.

If Jev’s terms forbid benchmarking it against competitors, what does that do to trust in a product sold as a measurement tool? If the open models already cover the easy majority, how long before the cascade trick closes the hard-task gap too? And who owes credit — and compensation — to the researchers whose older ideas the well-funded labs are now productizing?

Steerable Reranking: How JEV Solves RAG

Prompt Engineering on steerable reranking: Jev as an instruction-following, 27×-cheaper reranker that lifts top-1 accuracy from 21% to 54% — because the criterion, not cosine similarity, should decide what ranks.

Prompt Engineering’s “Steerable Reranking: How JEV Solves RAG” takes the week’s most-hyped model and puts it to work on one of the most boring — and most real — problems in AI engineering: retrieval-augmented generation. Jev is the only thing people are talking about, the creator admits, but there is little content showing where it actually fits in production. His answer: all over a RAG pipeline, at every point where the system has to make a decision — retrieval filtering, reranking, citation — and Jev turns out to be a near-perfect candidate for those decision points.

The video starts with Jev’s three primitives. First, binary decision-making: a true/false classifier with calibrated probabilities. Second, a choice ranker: hand it multiple options and a criterion, and it picks. Third, nul: a score ladder that assigns different scores to different options. For this video the focus is the reranker, because it shows off Jev’s most distinctive capability — and because it exposes a genuine pain point: what happens when an organization’s policy changes but the underlying documents stay the same.

That is where conventional rerankers break down. Vector search and cosine similarity measure semantic proximity, not correctness under your business logic — they can retrieve a chunk that is about the right topic without answering the question. Cross-encoders, the traditional reranking workhorse, take a question-document pair and score it, but they are frozen in time: they cannot follow instructions, so when policy changes, they cannot be told the new rules. LLM rerankers can follow instructions, but they are expensive and slow. Jev’s pitch is a third way: steerability at extreme low cost — a classifier that follows instructions.

The key idea is treating the criterion as an executable policy. Rather than a loose prompt, the criterion sets strict decision boundaries. The creator demonstrates with the nul primitive: define what counts as true — the passage directly answers the question — versus false — it shares words or topics but does not answer. Running four sample passages through it, Jev assigns the highest probability to the direct answer and low confidence to the rest. This doubles as a classifier: set a threshold, accept above it, discard below. The beauty, and the title’s claim, is that the ranking Jev produces is genuinely steerable: change the criterion and the same documents come out in a different order, because the policy — not the semantic similarity — is doing the ranking.

The centerpiece is a live notebook demo, linked in the video description. The scenario: a documentation-based system answering “how do I fix a 401 unauthorized error from the APIs,” where the corpus contains chunks from multiple competing versions of the API policy, and the system must resolve around them. Vector retrieval pulls a mix of chunks; a cross-encoder would rank them by semantic fit and cannot be told which policy version is authoritative. Jev, given two different criteria — an “authoritative version 2” policy and a looser “fast workaround” policy — produces visibly different rankings from the same chunks. The criterion is doing real work: the strict policy surfaces the version-2 answer, the lenient one surfaces the quick fix. Sign-up for the API is cheap, the creator notes — five dollars of free credit — and the demo runs through the official Python SDK.

The numbers back up the pitch. On concurrency, Jev handles 17 decisions per second serially; using its 64-connection pool, a batch that would take about 40 seconds sequentially completes in 7.6 seconds. On quality, the creator’s benchmark starts from a BM25 full-text-search baseline with 21 percent top-1 accuracy; adding Jev as a reranker pushes it to 54 percent — and top-1 is the hardest metric, since in practice you compute top-K over ten or twenty chunks. On cost, the comparison is against Gemini Flash-Lite and Gemini Flash: at small chunk sizes Jev’s speed and cost are close to Flash-Lite, but as chunks grow, Jev’s cost stays roughly linear while the LLM rerankers get expensive fast. That’s the “27x cheaper” framing on the thumbnail — not a single apples-to-apples figure, but the direction is clear: instruction-following ranking without paying generation prices.

The video closes by framing Jev as a drop-in replacement for decision points across existing workflows, not just reranking. One named example: graph RAG, where Jev can validate entities extracted from a corpus — promised for a future video. The creator’s larger point is methodological: stop thinking of Jev as a demo toy and start thinking of it as infrastructure for the unglamorous decisions inside pipelines — the filters, the rerankers, the validators — where a calibrated classifier that follows instructions is exactly what you want, and a reasoning model is overkill you pay for on every token.

If policies can steer rankings this directly, who owns the policy — and what stops a stale or malicious criterion from quietly corrupting retrieval? When the cost gap is this wide, how much of the “LLM as judge” stack collapses into cheap classifiers, and what breaks when it does? And if vector similarity can’t measure correctness under business logic, how much of today’s RAG infrastructure is ranking the wrong thing?

I spent the day with AI experts at Google

Nate Herk spends a full day on Google’s San Jose campus — a private, all-day mastermind with AI creators and Google Cloud and AI experts — and comes away with themes rather than launches: trust your data, intent-driven orchestration, and a human in the loop.

Nate Herk spent a full day on Google's San Jose campus — just southeast of the company's San Francisco home — for a private, all-day mastermind with a small group of fellow AI creators and a lineup of Google Cloud and AI experts. The format of the day shapes everything he shows: instead of sitting in one room while speakers came to him, the group moved building to building, room to room, for product demos, panels, hands-on workshops, and one-on-one conversations. It gave the day a roaming, behind-the-scenes energy that most event vlogs don't have.

He arrives to breakfast in a big dining hall, and his first impressions are almost entirely about the place itself. The greenery. Plants everywhere, and when he asked whether any of them were fake, an organizer told him every single one is real and maintained by the campus. For a creator who admits most of his own plants are fake, that became a small symbol of the culture: care and color inside otherwise very modern, very techy buildings — big, lots of windows, cool patterns outside, but plenty of character inside, from the plants to the Google bikes scattered everywhere for getting between buildings. The bikes were a burst of color, red and green and orange, and he pushed back on the recent narrative that modern architecture has lost all character, saying Google's interiors had plenty of it.

One of the first hands-on exercises was a genuinely fun one: the group got a coffee-shop location puzzle — "Where should Charlie's open next?" — with twelve possible San Francisco sites and data on monthly rent, space lease, foot traffic, income, and market growth. Nate and the other humans studied the numbers and picked their guesses; he went with site six and wrote up his reasoning. Then the twist: Google handed them a repo of much richer data — live inventory, customer reviews, forecasting data on top of the static numbers — and had them run the same decision through agentic coding tools. Nate ran it through Codex and through Cloud Code, and both agents independently landed on site six too. His joke afterward was that he'd clearly found his calling and should open a coffee shop. But the real point sat underneath the game: the agents chewed through a whole corpus of information in seconds, cross-checked it against live sources, and converged on the same answer the humans reached far more slowly. It was a tidy, concrete demo of the day's first big theme, which came up in nearly every conversation: trust your data, and let agents do the boring work of checking it. The Google folks kept saying agents can scan a corpus of information in seconds and flag conflicts, bloat, and inconsistencies — then the human steps in to make the judgment call about the actual source of truth.

That dovetailed with the second theme Nate kept hearing: intent-driven orchestration. The framing, as several speakers put it, is that roughly seventy percent of the time on a project used to go to plumbing — setting up the database, the repo, the codebase, the scaffolding. Now the human brings the idea and a very clear definition of done, including what "good" looks like and how to check whether the goal was satisfied, and the agents handle the orchestration. The human's job shifts from setup to vision and strategy. Nate found the consistency striking: execs, engineers, and creators all said the same thing independently.

The product demos were scattered across the roaming rooms. The most memorable involved a soccer ball: in one building, Nate kicked a ball in front of walls lined with Google Pixels, which analyzed his kick and generated a custom player card — his came out at ninety-nine power, ninety-five accuracy, ninety-five style. Beyond the fun, it was a neat physical demonstration of on-device computer vision doing real-time analysis. Other rooms held demos of Google AI applied to retail and inventory use cases, each room different enough that he described the whole thing like walking through a funhouse. He is light on specific product names — this was a themes-and-relationships day, not a launch event — so the demos read more as capability showcases than announcements.

He pulled a couple of Google engineers aside for interviews, and their answers tracked the day's themes. Asked how much faster AI lets him move, one put it at a hundred times, in two dimensions: linear speed on any single task, and the number of contexts he can advance in parallel, because asking questions and getting answers no longer stalls the pipeline. But the speed came with the day's counterweight: unpredictability. Agents are powerful but not deterministic, so humans stay in control with clear boundaries, clear goals, and clear definitions of done. The engineers were candid about what worries them as things accelerate — one mentioned the Hugging Face incident and stressed guardrails, security, data protection, and governance. Reliability came up in two senses: operational (does the thing stay up) and quality (are the answers good, and can this survive past the demo into production). And there was a thoughtful thread on adoption: how you communicate new AI tooling to stakeholders and manage the change when it's suddenly easy for everyone to build things, without duplicated work and rotting data.

The day closed with the group cooking their own dinner together, and Nate wrapped with a line he's clearly proud of: "The best context is the smallest amount of context that gives you the highest quality answer at the cheapest cost." It's his summary of context engineering, and honestly of the whole day — the craft isn't more AI, it's better-directed AI, with humans supplying the judgment.

Open questions: what were the actual product names behind the retail and inventory demos, and are any of them available to try? Was the Pixel kick-analysis wall a consumer-facing feature or a one-off internal demo? Did the coffee-shop exercise's agent consensus on site six reflect genuinely better reasoning than the humans', or just the same data wearing a fancier hat? And what specific guardrails did Google's engineers recommend in light of the Hugging Face incident — the vlog mentions the concern but not the playbook.

Where’s the Beef? Blackstone’s Jon Gray on the Payoff From the Enormous AI CapEx Spend

Blackstone president Jon Gray takes the AI capex question head-on before more than a hundred top investors — the world is spending trillions on AI, and he argues the beef is real: documented returns across the portfolio, history on his side, and demand still running well ahead of supply.

Jon Gray, Blackstone's President and COO, stood before more than a hundred of the firm's top investors with the question that gives the video its title: the world is spending trillions on artificial intelligence, and investors want to know where the return is. Is there real beef in this boom, or just circular financing that ends with a lot of people losing a lot of money? Gray structures his answer around four questions — what happened over the past year, what history teaches, where things stand today, and where they're going — all through the lens of AI, which he argues is the force that will ultimately drive value in portfolios regardless of geopolitical and fiscal noise.

The year-over-year numbers are staggering. AI token usage on Google went from nearly nothing in 2024 to 480 trillion tokens a month, then 3.2 quadrillion by May 2026. Anthropic and OpenAI, which had almost no revenue a couple of years ago, grew roughly a hundred-fold by July 2025 and now run at an estimated combined $105 billion run rate, with their combined valuation moving from $683 billion last September to an estimated $2–3 trillion as they head toward going public. Blackstone's own window into demand runs through roughly 1,400 portfolio companies and borrowers: their run-rate spend with Anthropic alone jumped 21-fold in a year, from $25 million to $525 million, because those companies are earning extraordinary returns on the spend. The capital formation behind it is equally vast: hyperscaler capex doubled in a year to $820 billion — 2.5% of US GDP — while Blackstone's data center leasing platform went from one gigawatt in 2024 to two in 2025 and is on track for at least six gigawatts this year, roughly $100 billion of capex, with tenants putting in another couple hundred billion of chips. Power is the quiet companion: utility capex of $800 billion over five years is expected to nearly double, and stocks of turbine, cooling, and electrical-equipment makers rose 44–81% in a year. Gray concedes one miss — the memory chip makers, with Micron and SK Hynix up roughly 600% and 500% — while noting SK Hynix still trades at a P/E of four, which he reads as lingering skepticism rather than bubble pricing.

To argue this can persist, Gray reaches back to 1870 — post–Civil War America, wood buildings, horses, candlelight — and contrasts it with 1900: steel, rail cars, electric light. In those thirty years, labor productivity doubled, GDP quadrupled, manufacturing output rose six-fold, and the stock market rose seven-fold. His claim: we sit today much closer to 1870 than to 1900. But he's candid about the bust riding alongside every boom — roughly 200 railroads went bankrupt in that era. The crucial difference he draws is that the railroads, like the late-1990s telecom buildout, built supply ahead of demand on heavy leverage, whereas today's buildout has demand running well ahead of supply, contracted mostly with lightly leveraged counterparties.

The heart of the presentation is the "beef" itself: documented use cases inside Blackstone's portfolio. A mobile-tower company processes leases five times faster after a $4 million AI investment that earns $4.5 million a year. The energy analytics firm Enverus gets an 18-fold return on its model spend on automated code fixes. A rental-housing company processes applications 90% faster. Chamberlain, the garage-door company, turned AI visualization into a brand-new $40–50 million run-rate business its CEO expects to reach $500 million within five years. Inside Blackstone, legal and compliance reviews are 50% more efficient and an internal portfolio-intelligence agent cut some timelines by 99%. These roll up into macro data: US productivity growth stepped up from a decade average of 1.5% to 2.6% over the last two and a half years, hyperscaler revenue per employee is up 65% in three and a half years, EBITDA margins are up 500 basis points at the S&P and 700 at Blackstone's portfolio companies, and earnings growth hit 32% in the last twelve months, roughly double its prior average. Beyond the desk, Gray points to Waymo — mileage up 250-fold in two and a half years with serious accident rates 94% lower — a tripling of construction workers at Blackstone's QTS data centers in under two years, and AI-discovered drugs showing meaningfully higher Phase 1 trial success rates with trial durations cut by up to 40%.

Looking ahead, Gray lays out three beliefs: AI use cases will proliferate and drive enormous productivity gains; that diffusion will drive exponential growth in demand for intelligence; and the binding constraint is the physical world — chips, power, data centers. The frictions are real: community resistance and moratoriums on data center construction, turbine delivery slots stretching to 2030–2031, chip shortages because hyperscaler capex rose nine-fold in five years while chipmakers haven't even doubled theirs, and $55 billion of capital per gigawatt-scale AI factory. What keeps him up at night is underestimating disruption: PE software deal volume is down 66% as multiples compress on companies whose futures look uncertain, and he draws the Amazon analogy — Kmart, Sears, and Toys "R" Us died while Walmart, Costco, and TJ Maxx thrived. Away from tech he favors scarce, AI-proof assets: an Indian cricket team, a coffee chain, beachfront property, essential infrastructure like the Rome airport. The risks he flags are cyber safety triggering regulatory backlash, 90% of advanced semiconductors made in Taiwan, evolving technology like edge and space-based compute, and frothy valuations on barely-revenue companies. His conclusion returns to the title: the bun is the trillions going into chips, data centers, and power; the beef is the return on investment — and that return is what's justifying the spend.

Which parts of the current capex buildout are most exposed if demand growth decelerates, given that much contracted supply sits with lightly leveraged hyperscalers who could simply pause? How much of the productivity and margin gains are durable structural improvements versus cyclical strength that unwinds in a downturn? And as AI agents move from the desk into the physical world, does the physical-world constraint become an investment moat — or the place where the boom most visibly breaks?

Navier-Stokes, AI and the Future of Mathematics with Martin Hairer (2014 Fields Medal)

Dr Tom Crawford sits down with Fields Medal 2014 laureate Martin Hairer at the 13th Heidelberg Laureate Forum, days after OpenAI’s announced Navier-Stokes Millennium-prize proof — and Hairer is candid about what AI-written mathematics really is: adversarial to read, brilliant at recombining known ideas, and not yet capable of a genuinely new concept.

TomRocksMaths' "Navier-Stokes, AI and the Future of Mathematics with Martin Hairer" finds Dr Tom Crawford sitting down with Fields Medal 2014 laureate Martin Hairer at the 13th Heidelberg Laureate Forum, days after the story every mathematician has been talking about: OpenAI's announced proof resolving the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize problem — a blow-up, or singularity, result under conditions C and D of the Clay Institute's statement. Hairer works on stochastic PDEs, but he has read partway into the roughly 160-page paper. Crawford — PhD in fluid mechanics, owner of a Navier-Stokes tattoo — is torn between elation and unease about how it was solved.

The ambivalence is concrete. At an Oxford workshop marking twenty-five years of the Millennium problems, it was clear Navier-Stokes was in the "end sprint". Against that backdrop: a trillion-dollar company surveyed the field, picked the highest-profile problem closest to resolution, and threw an estimated ten to twenty million dollars of compute at it — news coverage described an 88-hour agent run — to brute-force the final stretch. OpenAI has not claimed the million-dollar prize: the second Millennium problem, after Perelman's Poincaré conjecture, solved with the money unclaimed. The reward was PR. Dennis Sullivan stressed the solved case is the problem as stated, but that the unforced case, with the forcing term set to zero, is arguably the more interesting one — and it remains wide open.

On the paper itself Hairer is candid. He has read sections one and two: the first a standard non-technical introduction, the second a genuinely well-written explanation of the main proof ideas, readable for a mathematician in an adjacent area. The rest reads like AI-generated text, which he finds very painful to read — so he will wait for someone with more skin in the game to write the expository account explaining how the proof really works.

That leads to the conversation's sharpest observation: reading AI-written mathematics is adversarial, unlike reading a colleague's paper. A human colleague who makes a mistake makes an honest one, so you can trust the parts outside your expertise. An AI text polishes the parts it is confident about and quietly brushes the hard bits under the rug, writing around them so convincingly that you believe all the t's are crossed. You cannot afford to trust any of it, because the most polished passage may be exactly the one being used to trick you.

Hairer then describes the "First Proof" experiment, which benchmarks what publicly available models — the ones ordinary mathematicians can subscribe to, plus open-source harnesses — can do at real research. Ten mathematicians each contributed an unpublished lemma from a paper they were writing, with a proof of about five pages, and fed the bare statement to the models. A hurried first round made a splash; a second, controlled round had the AI outputs human peer-reviewed to journal standards, with time, tokens, and dollar cost tracked. Roughly half the problems were solved — that was the level of the public models as of June 2026. AI proved strikingly good at constructing complicated counterexamples (the Navier-Stokes blow-up is itself one), and at finding ideas in the literature and recombining them — sometimes across subfields — into positive proofs, or discovering tricks people had overlooked. In stochastic PDEs, two young mathematicians got ChatGPT to produce a two-to-three-page proof of something people had chewed on for years — "so easy in a way" that the reaction was, why didn't I think of that? People convince themselves a problem is hard and stop trying the easy stuff; the model just tries it.

What he has not seen is a system coming up with genuinely new concepts. There is a metaphor going around that AI is filling in the "convex hull" of known mathematics — combining existing ideas into everything obtainable from them — though Hairer notes that hull may be vastly larger than anyone thought. But the big results tend to come from entirely new ways of thinking about an object, and he has seen no AI do that. Whether they will in six months or a year, he does not know.

Asked for the ideal future, he is pragmatic: the cat is out of the bag. The ideal is a superpowered assistant — something that probes new ideas fast, shoulders technical calculations that would take months to write up carefully, and lets mathematicians reach further into genuinely interesting problems. But he raises the control problem: the community must not rely entirely on the goodwill of OpenAI and Anthropic. Right now a two-hundred-dollar subscription buys something like twenty thousand dollars of compute a month, which cannot last, and these companies care about math for PR, not fundamentally. They may soon declare mathematics solved and move on, leaving mathematicians with tools that are prohibitively expensive and no longer developed with math in mind. His proposal borrows from physics: academic institutions should build math-specialized AI on controlled hardware — fine-tuned open-source models nearly catching up to the frontier — and mathematicians would apply for compute time the way astronomers apply for telescope time.

He closes with a personal warning: use it or lose it. Outsource technical mathematics to the machine and your ability atrophies — three weeks of holiday already slows you down; six months costs you the intuition itself. His analogy is exercise and food: we have machines that lift for us and food in abundance, yet we still walk and lift and moderate what we eat, because otherwise we degenerate. AI as a tool, in moderation.

Open questions: Will the eventual expository account of the blow-up construction show a new idea, or just a recombined one at scale? If mathematicians apply for "telescope time" on academic AI hardware, who decides which problems are worth the compute? And if models only fill in the convex hull of known mathematics, who keeps training the human intuitions that hull is supposed to cover — the university curriculum, or the machine?
