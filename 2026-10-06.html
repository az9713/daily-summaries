Google's AI Infrastructure Chief, Amin Vahdat, on the Physics & Economics of Frontier AI

Sequoia Capital sat down with Amin Vahdat, who has led Google's AI infrastructure since late last year, for "Google's AI Infrastructure Chief, Amin Vahdat, on the Physics & Economics of Frontier AI." The conversation runs the full stack, from how an AI data center differs from a conventional one to what a frontier supercomputer might look like in 2036.

Vahdat starts with the building itself. A traditional data center is a 25 to 30 year asset designed to stay flexible across many generations of hardware. An AI data center is increasingly purpose built and co-designed with the machines that go inside, because the power profile is so extreme. A storage rack might draw 10 to 40 kilowatts, while a single accelerator rack already draws hundreds of kilowatts and could pass a megawatt. A building shaped for one simply cannot serve the other well.

The metric he trusts is goodput per watt: not the theoretical peak a chip can hit, but the useful work actually delivered once failures, recoveries, and software flaws are counted. At 100,000 accelerators working in lockstep, something fails multiple times a day, possibly multiple times an hour. The causes form a long tail across hardware, networking, and software, so the team's accountability is measured in answered questions, not peak benchmarks.

That discipline drives a startling capacity target. Google aims to roughly double its effective serving capacity, meaning its ability to generate tokens, about every six months. Most of that gain, he says, comes from model and software improvements rather than new silicon, though hardware still delivers a twofold or better yearly multiplier underneath everything else.

The TPU story is a study in betting against the bitter lesson. Google began building custom silicon in 2013, when conventional wisdom said specialization never wins. The first chip handled inference, the second added training, and then the transformer arrived and reshaped the program entirely. This year Google split the line in two: an 8I chip specialized for serving and an 8T chip for training, justified because inference is now projected to be 30 to 60 percent of the market over the hardware's life. Crucially, each chip can still do the other's job, which preserves flexibility if the mix shifts.

He declines to just burn the transformer into silicon. The argument is that the underlying primitives, very large matrix multiply units plus a handful of supporting operations, have barely changed since the first TPU and have carried many generations of model architectures. Specialize to a workload that lasts months and the window to intercept it has already closed.

Co-design, he argues, is where the large wins hide. A fully portable stack that runs on anyone's hardware, network, and software leaves perhaps 10 to 20 percent, sometimes twofold, on the table at each layer, and those losses multiply. Google co-designs shoulder to shoulder with DeepMind, occasionally delaying a tape-out by a week or two when a model idea is worth intercepting, and now uses Gemini to help design the hardware future Geminis will run on.

Agentic workloads are reshaping the demand picture. Long-horizon agents remove the human pause between requests, so interaction times fall from seconds to milliseconds, and the orchestration work lands on ordinary CPUs, which now need to sit near the accelerators along with memory and storage. That complicates building design, since accelerator racks, CPU racks, and disk racks each want different density, networking, and power.

On networking, he describes optical circuit switching, which Google has used for about 15 years: tiny mirrors steer light between ports without converting it to electrical signals, letting the network reshape itself and letting a failed accelerator rack be swapped for a spare in milliseconds, no new fiber required.

Power is the constraint he calls most fundamental. Google prefers staying on the grid, co-planning gigawatt-scale sites with utilities years ahead and covering the infrastructure costs itself, because sharing a large grid beats building two gigawatts of private redundancy. Where utility power arrives late, local generation can bridge the gap and even feed energy back during the hottest weeks of the year.

Old hardware does not retire quietly. Vahdat says TPUs from seven and eight years ago still run at full utilization, with replacement happening at the pod level on roughly a six-year depreciation schedule, while dedicated serving clusters get built worldwide rather than relying on yesterday's training sites. He also presses the case for open standards: JAX is Google's preferred framework, but PyTorch models run on TPUs too, in the same spirit that let IP win the protocol wars.

Two closing notes. Orbital data centers, which Google is genuinely pursuing as a moonshot, trade abundant sunlight for hard problems in cooling and repair. And by 2036, he guesses, the frontier machine is a tightly integrated, possibly multi-megawatt rack with a small bundle of fiber, wheeled in and plugged into water, power, and network, or perhaps launched into orbit.

Open questions

  1. if most capacity gains now come from models and software, how long can that compounding continue before the returns shift back to silicon?
  2. when inference demand is spread across the whole planet in smaller, mixed clusters, how much of the co-design advantage survives outside the giant training sites?
  3. will power procurement, not compute, end up deciding which companies get to train frontier models at all?

Why Every AI Lab Will Become a Biotech Company | Andrew Huberman

Invest Like the Best, hosted by Patrick O'Shaughnessy, sits down with Andrew Huberman, the Stanford neuroscientist behind the Huberman Lab podcast and the book Protocols. The conversation starts with energy as the hidden bottleneck for high performers and ends with Huberman's prediction that the biggest AI companies are, at their core, becoming brain companies.

The opening stretch is about self-knowledge. Huberman argues that people should honestly assess how much sleep they personally need instead of copying outliers who thrive on four or five hours, and that what looks like boundless energy in others often comes with an inability to switch off at night. Early in a career or a company, he says, the goal is simply to get a great deal done without breaking yourself; recovery and optimization come later.

That leads to the skill he calls downshifting: falling asleep on demand. He breaks it into three trainable pieces: quieting thought by shifting attention to pure sensation, slowing the heart through long exhales, and losing track of where your limbs are in space. Rocking works on babies and adults for the same reason, he notes, and an experimental eye mask from an MIT-linked team coaxes the slow eye movements that help the brain let go of body position. Devices like that, seven to twelve months out by his estimate, point to a bigger shift: technology that steers brain states directly, first in sleep, then in waking life.

He is blunt about the state of public health advice, calling the discourse a theatrics-driven mess with a near-zero bar for entry. His forecast is a downturn in optimization culture, followed by a wave of seriously trained scientists and physicians doing public education well. His book, he says, was his way of writing down what he actually knows for certain and moving on.

The centerpiece claim explains the episode's title. Reading from the brain, through electrodes, imaging, or wearables, has been neuroscience's project for decades. The new project is writing to it non-invasively, with ultrasound or similar tools, at high spatial and temporal precision, and with graded control rather than a simple on or off switch. Anyone who thinks Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Elon Musk's Neuralink are only language-model companies, he says, will be surprised: "Every single one of those is going to be a biotech company." AI enters the picture as the titration layer, learning which stimulation pattern produces which state in a given person and adjusting in real time.

He contrasts that precision with today's blunt tools. Drugs mostly raise or lower the brain's firing threshold everywhere at once, which is why they work and why they carry side effects. Vagal stimulation for depression shows what targeted input can do, and he expects small under-the-skin implants, about as invasive as a piercing, to tempt a large share of entrepreneurs within about a year. Peptides, in his telling, matter less than implantables.

The honest limit, he says, is the model of the brain itself. The sensory periphery and deep circuits for hunger, anger, and appetite are well understood, which is why GLP-1 drugs work. Memory and thought are not. He describes animal studies where reactivating the exact neurons tagged during learning, in any order or all at once, produces the same behavior, a result that quietly undermines the textbook idea that firing sequence is everything. The same neurons also serve many behaviors, so writing to the brain carries real cross-talk risk even as it gets easier.

From there the talk runs to gene editing, embryo screening, and the CRISPR babies case, which he treats as proof that these technologies spread whether or not anyone approves them in public. His own excitement, though, is narrower: he wants neuroscience to define waking brain states properly, the way sleep stages are defined, so that future tools can deliberately produce the right state for focus, learning, or calm. He also predicts the health space goes quiet around 2027 and 2028 as the field resets.

The closing answer, to the show's traditional question about the kindest thing anyone has done for him, goes back to graduate school. His advisor, Barbara Chapman, essentially told him to leave the fashionable lab and follow the work he was sneaking off to do at night. His phrasing of her lesson: "Success is determined by how precisely you match your genuine curiosity to the work that you do."

Open questions

  1. If the temporal order of neuron firing matters far less than textbooks claim, what should replace sequence as the working model of memory, and how much of the read-and-write neurotechnology roadmap depends on the answer?
  2. When stimulation devices can dial motivation or calm up and down on demand, who sets the safeguards, and what stops self-experimentation from outrunning them the way it has outrun other blunt tools?
  3. Huberman says the technologists now leading brain exploration came from outside neuroscience. What do academic labs still uniquely contribute, and does the field's center of gravity move to the AI labs for good?

Building Cyber Defense for the Agentic Era

Kevin Mandia spent three decades in cybersecurity and built Mandiant, the incident response firm that Google ultimately absorbed. On this episode of The a16z Show, a16z General Partner David George asks him why he came back to start Armadin, and Mandia's answer is simple: he did not want to sit out the AI shift after thirty years watching the field change. He met Armadin's founders, David Slater, Travis Lanham, and Evan Peña, and concluded the talent was generational and the timing unavoidable. In his words, everything he did before is finished, and everything ahead is new.

The core of the conversation is how AI changes offense. A human intruder, Mandia says, is selective by necessity: limited people and time force one path through a network, moved along step by step. AI attackers are different in kind. They operate at a scale and speed humans cannot match, probing thousands of paths at once, and they betray themselves today mainly by being sloppy, noisy, and occasionally illogical, revisiting doors they already opened. Nation states have historically attacked like a sniper round, deep and narrow against a small set of targets. AI turns that into something more like a drone swarm: louder, less surgical, but far more comprehensive, and harder to attribute. Mandia argues the result democratizes attack skill, letting far less technical actors look far more capable, and he warns that today's open models are already good enough for this. Criminal use, he expects, will surge once attackers can rent compute anonymously.

Armadin's answer is to build the best offense in the world and aim it at its own customers. Mandia frames it as a sports problem: a great defense only exists if a great practice offense pushes it, the way the 2000 Baltimore Ravens defense was sharpened by the offense across from it. The company's first act is offensive. Swarms of agents map a customer's entire network, every service, route, and asset, building a metadata twin of the attacker's view. From there the system polls cheaply for change, a new app, a changed route, an updated service, and attacks the change, so testing is continuous rather than an annual event. When a new vulnerability drops over a weekend, the company can check whether it is actually exploitable inside a customer before real attackers get there.

That approach, he argues, makes traditional penetration testing obsolete. Legacy scanning tools mostly list known weaknesses and hygiene problems, producing long reports full of findings that may not matter. Armadin instead carries the exploit through, to remote code execution or data access, so a finding is proven rather than suspected, and the agents find logic flaws in custom applications that scanners miss entirely. The proof point he returns to: since January 2026, Armadin has found more than ninety previously unknown vulnerabilities at customer sites, all in production systems at Fortune 500 companies, discovered from outside the network with no source code access. The last few, he notes, were found by the AI agents themselves rather than by the human operators steering them.

The second act is defense. Finding risk is not enough, so the company is building a defensive layer that takes what the offense learns and pushes compensating controls into the platforms customers already run, naming CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, and Fortinet as partners in that direction. Mandia compares it to field dressing in war: stop the bleeding first, hospital later. He is blunt that humans cannot stay in the detect-and-respond loop. Once an autonomous intruder is inside, it spreads at a speed he likens to a popped balloon, so prevention, detection, and response all have to collapse toward machine speed, with autonomous security as every security chief's true north.

He is equally blunt that the industry is only at the edge of this. Both attackers and defenders feel a near-term desperation, he says, and large enterprises are running war rooms to close exposures. He points to Anthropic's Mythos moment as the marketing shock that woke boards up, and to the Hugging Face incident as a case of a capable team underestimating what its own models could do. Securing offensive agents, in his telling, is itself a craft: Armadin wraps its attacking systems in hypervisors, proxies, classifiers that watch every prompt in and out, deterministic rules that forbid certain actions, and complete logs of everything an agent does, because domain expertise, not just model skill, is what catches the failure modes.

His internal testing offers a sobering benchmark. The team built twenty full attack chains that experienced human operators had carried out at real victim sites, and no model, open or closed, completed more than eight. Open and closed models ended in roughly the same place; the closed ones were faster, and cost was the main difference. Capability in this domain, he concludes, is compressing.

The last section is about building a company at this speed. Mandiant was self-funded, profitable, and nearly competition-free because nobody believed its founding premise that breaches were inevitable. Armadin cannot work that way: it needs venture funding, a go-to-market machine built ahead of the technology, and sales teams retrained weekly because the product changes every two weeks. In a market crowded with founders, Mandia says differentiation no longer comes from marketing noise. It comes from one loop: get a customer, make that customer happy, repeat. Everyone at the company sits in one room because distributed teams move too slowly for him.

Open questions

  1. If open and closed models alike stall at the same eight of twenty kill chains today, what breaks first when that ceiling lifts, and will defenders or attackers notice sooner?
  2. How much autonomy can defense safely take before a machine-speed compensating control does damage of its own, and who answers for it?
  3. As attribution blurs and anonymous compute spreads, what replaces the old assumption that professionals can tell a nation state from a criminal gang from a machine?
