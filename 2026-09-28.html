Is The New 16gb M6 Mac Mini Good for Local AI? A Beginner's Guide

Bart Slodyczka puts the new 16GB M6 Mac Mini through its paces for local AI work, comparing it against his M4 on the two stages of running a language model, prefill and decode, and weighing it against a 24GB M6 and a cited third-party M5 Pro test, all to answer whether Apple's cheapest desktop can serve as a dedicated always-on agent machine.

Bart Slodyczka's YouTube channel published "Is The New 16gb M6 Mac Mini Good for Local AI? A Beginner's Guide" on September 28, 2026. The twelve minute video compares the new sixteen gigabyte M6 Mac Mini against the M4 version he bought earlier this year, all through the lens of running local AI agents, and it also brings a twenty four gigabyte M6 into the test.

His motivation is practical. He wants a dedicated machine he can leave on his desk running a Hermes or OpenClaw agent on a local model while he works on his main computer. Everything in the video flows from that single use case, and it makes the benchmarks easy to interpret.

Before the numbers, he explains how a language model answers a message. There are two stages. First the model reads and absorbs whatever you send it, a stage called prefill, which leans on the GPU's compute power. Then it generates the reply one token at a time, a stage called decode, which leans more on memory bandwidth. Two stages, two different hardware appetites.

The core test runs a nine billion parameter Qwen model, four bit, inside LM Studio, at inputs of two thousand, eight thousand, and thirty thousand tokens. At eight thousand tokens, the M4 processes input at 222 tokens per second while the M6 manages 998, a dramatic jump. Generation rises from 19.3 tokens per second to 27.2, about forty percent faster. The M6 leads at the shorter and longer inputs too.

The hardware story explains why. Moving from M4 to M6, the CPU and GPU each go from ten cores to twelve, and the M6's GPU carries neural accelerators for AI math. Memory bandwidth rises from 120 to 153 gigabytes per second. That is the first jump.

The second comparison, sixteen versus twenty four gigabytes of M6, is far less dramatic. Both have the same twelve GPU cores and the same AI acceleration, so prefill barely moves. The twenty four gigabyte unit edges ahead by about ten percent at thirty two thousand tokens, and decode improves by roughly five percent despite an eleven percent bandwidth advantage on paper. The thirty two gigabyte M6 shares the twenty four's core count and bandwidth, so do not expect a speed leap there either.

For a real step up in speed, he points at the M5 Pro, which he does not own. It offers sixteen or twenty GPU cores and 307 gigabytes per second of bandwidth, roughly double the sixteen gigabyte M6. A public test of the same model on an M5 Pro reported around 1,280 tokens per second for prefill and 50 for decode, but it ran in a different app, so he cautions against treating it as a direct comparison with his LM Studio numbers.

Then comes the memory math, the most useful part for a buyer. On a sixteen gigabyte machine, the operating system and everyday apps take roughly four gigabytes, leaving twelve for AI. Model weights alone eat about eight, leaving four gigabytes of working space for the conversation. An agent's conversation includes instructions, files it has read, and tool call results, so four gigabytes runs out fast. On a twenty four gigabyte machine, after setting aside six for the system, eighteen remain for AI, leaving ten gigabytes of working space after the same eight gigabyte model loads. That is the real argument for the upgrade, not speed.

Prices frame the decision. The M4 launched at $599 and the M6 starts at $899, but the base M4 had already been repriced to $799 by June, so the new machine costs $100 more than the machine it replaces. Apple blamed rising memory and storage costs for the June increases.

His verdict: the M6 is a decent upgrade for local AI, particularly if an agent does regular work for you. A simulated agent workload comes out close to twice as fast as the M4. If your M4 already does what you need, keep it. If you spend the day waiting on the model, the M6 buys you real time back, except when the agent is stalled on a website or a tool call, where no chip removes the wait. For a first purchase, sixteen gigabytes gets you started, but for long agent sessions with big context, he leans toward twenty four, and if raw speed matters most, look at the M5 Pro.

I'm using Jev more than Opus 5.5 or GPT-6. Here's why.

Claire Vo opens 'Jev week' with a blunt claim: after a week in which Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Sol, GPT-6 Luna, and Muse all landed, the model she used most was Jev, TypeSafe's tiny decision model. She explains its three output primitives, choice, score, and Noul, its four-cents-per-million-input-tokens pricing, and walks through classifying and clustering thousands of GitHub pull requests for cents.

Claire Vo opened "Jev week" on the How I AI podcast with a blunt claim. After a week in which Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Sol, GPT-6 Luna and Muse all landed, the model she has actually used the most is Jev, a small decision model from TypeSafe AI. It takes text in, like any language model, but instead of generated text it returns type-safe values: a Choice from a list you define, a Score on a scale you define, or a Noul, a probability that the answer to a yes-or-no question is yes. It is, in her framing, a model for decisions rather than generation.

The economics are the other half of the story. Jev charges four cents per million input tokens and nothing for output, because it barely outputs anything. Vo says her week of heavy use cost her under ten dollars. She used it first on GitHub pull requests: on a marketing site with 112 pull requests, classifying and pairwise-clustering them cost about one cent, and on her ChatPRD app with roughly two thousand pull requests this year, analyzing 1,700 pull requests across 17,000 pairs cost nine cents and took about two minutes. The payoff for product leaders: an honest map of where engineering time goes. At ChatPRD, nearly thirty percent of pull requests turned out to be platform, security and infrastructure work.

The same pattern scales beyond code. She ran the classification over her own local Claude Code and Codex sessions and watched her work shift in the transcript: almost entirely engineering tasks in January, under forty percent by September, with agents, publishing and a new business filling the gap. She ran it over her personal Gmail to score which emails could be deleted, then handed the tagged clusters to a bigger model for precise action. Her summary of the formula: Jev alone is fine, but Jev plus an LLM buddy is super powerful. Big models like Astra do the analysis, strategy and insight extraction; generation work goes to models like Sol or Luna; Jev does the cheap classification, clustering and mapping that makes the expensive models worth running.

The most ambitious example is ChatPRD's product insights graph, the feature she says she had spent thousands, possibly tens of thousands of dollars, trying to brute-force with frontier models before cracking it with this split. It pulls in pull requests, support tickets, conversation transcripts and Linear tickets, ran over two hundred thousand classifications and pairwise groupings, and cost roughly four dollars on the Jev side. The result closes the gap she cares about most: the distance between what customers say they want and what the team is actually building.

She also built two small apps in an afternoon to show what real-time decision loops enable. One is an audience signal dashboard for the How I AI YouTube channel: forty-five hundred comments pulled through the YouTube API, categorized as positive, negative or neutral, with episode ideas extracted and a request board, plus live search across all comments. The other is a playful voice-to-color app that takes speech in through OpenAI's realtime voice API, has Jev score the emotion against a list of hex color values, and returns a matching quote. Both demos make the same point: when a decision costs almost nothing and returns in near real time, you can put a model inside loops that used to be impractical.

Her advice for newcomers is to start with the TypeSafe docs: the primitives page explaining Choice, Score and Noul, and the cookbook patterns. The honest caveat she repeats is that cross-app comparisons, like the public M5 Pro numbers she cites in passing, need matched software and settings before you attribute a difference to the chip.

  3. what governance should surround a pipeline that cheap when it touches an entire Gmail history or a company repository?

Inside the Personal AI Assistant Growing 10% a Day | Instinct Founder

Invest Like the Best episode 493: Patrick O'Shaughnessy talks with Noah Shinn, CEO of Instinct, a one-year-old invite-only startup building a personal AI agent you text, call, and email like a person. Shinn describes an agent with its own phone, computer, and email address, shares user stories from outfit planning to subscription cancellations, and argues any product dependent on consumer laziness is toast.

Instinct is a one year old startup, still invite only, building a personal AI agent that you text, call, and email like a person. Patrick O'Shaughnessy has used it for months, and opens with proof it works: Instinct has called him three times, including a call at two fifty five in the afternoon reminding him to sign a document due at three.

The pitch is not a new app but a new experience. Instinct gets its own phone, its own computer, and its own email address. Shinn's framing is simple: pretend you have a superhuman person with those tools, and just ask for things.

The stories from users are wild. Instinct recovered footage of Patrick's daughter on the US Open jumbotron. It scanned Patrick's wardrobe and now sends weekly outfit plans picturing him wearing each outfit, and it has shopped for thousands of outfits on a user's behalf. It cancels subscriptions, saving one user about two thousand dollars, which is why Shinn says any product dependent on consumer laziness or inertia is toast.

Then there is the network effect. Users give each other's Instinct instances varying levels of access, spouses share everything, and agents negotiate with agents. The canonical use case so far is meeting scheduling, and friend groups already plan whole weeks together, with the agents optimizing the shared Uber routes.

Shinn argues this reorders whole industries. Restaurant reservations get checked every five seconds across every restaurant, matched to special occasions, with partnerships opening up held inventory. Travel is already fifty percent of transaction volume, and the platform is approaching a billion dollars a year in transactions on a small base, growing around ten percent a day. A voice note saying you need to be in New York tonight turns into flights, a hotel, Ubers, calendar updates, and evening events, all handled.

Trust is the gating factor, and it takes weeks. Forty percent of users share a personal credit card within three weeks, and eighty percent stay after sharing one piece of sensitive information. Safety rests on firewalls that intercept malicious content and a decoupled watchdog monitor that can pause or intercept any action or thought before it executes.

The business model is free for users with a merchant-side take rate, on the Apple Pay analogy. Shinn cites reference points, Shopify around two and a half to three percent, Amazon up to ten, Apple at thirty, hotels offering up to thirty. The point is alignment: the agent pursues higher-level objectives for the user instead of selling their attention, a direct rejection of the "if you are not paying, you are the product" reality.

On building the product, Shinn's obsession is understandability over capability. One early principle: never chase what the AI can do, only what the user can predict it will do. Every text is crafted for low cognitive load, front loaded so the user gets the point in the first third, shaped by how eyes actually scan a page. New behavior goes through staged rollouts: Shinn tries it, the team tries it, early access tries it, then the public.

Forty percent of his time goes to worrying about compute. Growing ten percent a day means compute demand doubles roughly every week, while the resource has a lead time of several months, and being wrong on a purchase costs three to four times over. His answer is workload-shaped inference: most of the agent's work does not need an answer in hundreds of milliseconds, it can finish in minutes or hours, and deployments tuned to that deliver frontier-level performance at very low cost. His personal goal is a product that stays free for everyone for a lifetime.

The growth story is invite only by design, never meant as exclusivity. It started at two hundred friends and family, each user getting five invites, then accelerated from a trickle to ten or eleven percent a day with zero dollars spent on marketing. Invites sell on eBay for around three hundred dollars. The looming question is the race between incumbents who can distribute to two billion people overnight and Instinct's compounding curve.

On competition, Shinn barely looks sideways. He tried Meta's Muse and calls it a great product, but notes that almost nobody he passes in a cafe uses AI the way they say they want to, so the space is still wide open. Early versions of Instinct lacked the firewalls and monitors; instead of patching, the team built a system to solve the whole class of problems. The philosophy is always proactive, never reactive, and the user stays in control of their data and can retract access at any time.

The long arc is radical: all of software collapses into one very easy interface while capability keeps expanding. Some users already interact almost entirely by voice, and Shinn imagines just an AirPod on a hike, the agent reading headlines and contracts into your ear. The deeper shift is from individual tasks to higher-level objectives: hit a fitness goal over four months, or run a small business whose entire back office, down to autonomous inventory bounds, lives on Instinct.

Noah Shinn wants none of the Her-style relationship. Instinct should feel like a seamless, quiet, socially aware operator that learns your communication style over time, never a cheeky persona. The name is deliberately not a human name, so every user arrives with a fresh slate. His latest round was about a billion dollars at roughly a ten billion valuation, led by Sequoia, Benchmark, and KOTU. Asked the traditional closing question, the kindest thing anyone ever did for him, he gracefully answers on a higher level, about the relationships he is grateful for.

No, Seriously. Claude is Starting To Get Dangerous

Nate Herk argues AI agents have crossed the line from helpful to genuinely dangerous, building his case around a dramatic story about Anthropic, a White House directive, and a model shutdown over national security concerns. The summary presents the video's narrative as the video's own claims, with a note that the captions use fictionalized names throughout.

Nate Herk's latest video argues that AI agents have crossed a line from helpful to genuinely dangerous, and he builds the case around a dramatic story he tells about Anthropic and the US government. The video opens with Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei raising red flags about AI autonomy, then claims the White House forced Anthropic to disable its newest models over unspecified national security concerns.

According to the video's telling, the sequence went like this. Three days after Anthropic released its most powerful model ever, a single US directive shut the entire model off. The government had already blacklisted the company months earlier. About three weeks later, restrictions were lifted and the model, which the video calls Fable 5, came back online. Herk reads this as proof that AI development is a race with no referee, where the only incentive is to win.

The video gives specifics of the alleged order. It says no foreign nationals could access the models, including foreign-national Anthropic employees working inside the United States. Because the order took effect immediately, the video says, the only way to guarantee compliance was to shut both models off for everyone. The stated trigger was a jailbreak that got the model to scan software for weaknesses and demonstrate how a flaw could be exploited. Herk says Anthropic pushed back, noting the test found only a few minor already-known vulnerabilities, and that every model they tested, including older ones, could reproduce the exploit demonstration.

Herk also describes two variants of the same underlying model. In the video's telling, Fable is the public version while Mythos has fewer safeguards and is limited to a handful of trusted defenders. Before the shutdown, he says, Mythos Preview found thousands of previously unknown vulnerabilities on its own, including a twenty-seven-year-old bug in OpenBSD and a sixteen-year-old bug in FFmpeg that automated tests had hit five million times without catching. During the eighteen-day shutdown, he says, Anthropic trained a new safety classifier that blocks the reported technique in more than ninety-nine percent of cases, the government tested the updated safeguards, and controls were lifted. Anthropic also agreed to give designated government partners expanded pre-release access to future models.

From there Herk turns to three predictions. First, governments will start treating AI literacy as workforce infrastructure, pointing to a White House AI education task force and the NSF's AI Ready America program, with publicly funded AI credentials offered through community colleges and workforce programs over the next five to ten years. Second, cybersecurity becomes one of the biggest career winners, with the Bureau of Labor Statistics projecting information security analyst jobs growing twenty-nine percent from 2024 to 2034, while the human role shifts toward judgment, access control, and accountability. He expects cyber insurance to become a forcing function, with renewal forms asking which agents can send money, touch customer data, or deploy code, and who can shut them off. Third, governments will attempt a messy version of AI arms control, a stack of chip tracking, cloud logs, model evaluations, and inspections, but never a clean global pause, because the incentives to cheat are enormous.

He also reads aloud a viral resignation thread from a researcher he names Jacob Coxin, quotes Bernie Sanders introducing legislation to ban superintelligence and pause AI development, and quotes Evan Hubinger putting the chance of AI-caused human extinction within a decade at greater than ten percent. His takeaway is that one government could shut off one model for eighteen days but could not shut off the capability, the competition, or the pressure to keep moving.

The video closes with three practical skills for anyone using AI agents at work. First, find the real pain: he tells a story about a med spa client who wanted more leads, but analysis showed the real leak was booked appointments turning into no-shows, so the better project was follow-up, not lead generation. Second, control the system: start with read-only access, draft before acting, keep human approval on money, deletions, and production, log everything, and keep a kill switch. Third, own the outcome: pick the business number the system should change, write down the baseline, the target, and the timeline, and decide in advance what the system could get wrong and who checks it. He puts the full breakdown in a free resource guide inside his community.
