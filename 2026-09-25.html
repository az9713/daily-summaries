Big AI News: Opus 5.5 vs GPT-6 Sol, NotebookLM Updates, Muse Charm & More!

Lipsky pits Opus 5.5 against GPT-6 Sol in writing and design bake-offs, then rounds up the week in NotebookLM, Grok, Muse, and ChatGPT updates.

Paul J Lipsky's weekly AI news recap for September 25, 2026 leads with a rare same-day showdown: Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 and OpenAI's GPT-6 Sol launched within hours of each other, and Lipsky spent the week testing both side by side on his real production work.

The writing test asked both models to script a YouTube video with browser research and no access to his notes. GPT-6 Sol finished first, in about a minute and a half to Opus 5.5's two-plus minutes, and it costs half as much per API token. But speed and price lost to prose. Opus 5.5's script told a clearer, better-paced story, while GPT-6 Sol's felt jumpy. After a week of writing with it, Lipsky calls Opus 5.5 the best writing model he has ever used — the most humanlike, the best at matching his voice — even if it still sounds like AI.

The design test was a blind motion-graphics bake-off against Fable 5.1, his longtime graphics workhorse. GPT-6 Sol came in last by a wide margin. Fable 5.1 took first, but Opus 5.5 was a close second — close enough and cheap enough that Lipsky expects it to take over most of his design work. The video-editing test was the most lopsided: both models plugged into his AI editor to cut silences and bad takes and add zooms and highlights. Opus 5.5 nailed the layout, zoom positions, and highlighting; GPT-6 Sol chose a bizarre full-screen layout, zoomed on the wrong spots, and took fifteen and a half minutes to Opus 5.5's seven and a quarter — the reverse of their usual speed relationship.

Lipsky's verdict is blunt. Opus 5.5 is his new go-to model and, price and speed factored in, the best model available right now — even ahead of Fable 5.1. GPT-6 Sol, meanwhile, he calls a disappointment: somehow worse than GPT 5.6 Sol on some tasks. Opus 5.5 also redeems the Opus line itself; he found Opus 5 so weak he never used it after testing it.

Beyond the showdown, Google had a packed week. Gemini Notebook gained live voice chat with notebooks (Ultra subscribers first, other paid plans to follow), notebooks as citable sources inside Google Docs via the @ menu, and a new "interactive report" format that embeds generated assets — infographics, slide decks, mind maps, quizzes — directly in the report. Google also opened pre-orders for a new Google Book laptop built around Gemini Intelligence Everywhere, made Gemini work with thirteen new apps, and released Gemini 3.8 text-to-speech, an expressive speech model aimed at developers that already captures details like microphone plosives.

On the xAI side, Grok 4.7 launched as a notable improvement over 4.6 at the same price and speed, though early reactions are split and Lipsky himself hasn't formed an opinion. The bigger deal for him is Grok Bot: traffic can now route through your local computer instead of a cloud VM so bots stop getting blocked, and bots gained voice memos plus voice calls — a phone-call-style conversation where each bot gets its own selectable voice and speed.

Meta Connect was dominated by Meta Muse, the number-one app in the App Store. New capabilities include a personal email address for each Muse (just CC it on tasks), live video calls with an animated avatar, and — with permission — control of any app on your Mac so it can work through a task list unattended. Meta says Muse will stay essentially free, with paid plans for heavy users; the business model is a cut of transactions your Muse makes. That means a wave of shopping connectors: Walmart, Best Buy, Sephora, Wayfair, Shop Pay, PayPal, Instacart, and Expedia — but not Amazon, which blocked Muse from its site, a move Lipsky calls a colossal mistake. On hardware, Muse is coming to Meta's AI glasses over the next few months, there is a camera-free Ray-Ban Meta Audio pair for the camera-shy, and a Tamagotchi-style pocket device called Muse Charm with a fingerprint sensor and a camera for asking questions about what you are looking at.

OpenAI rounded out the week with ChatGPT updates: the desktop app now supports any Chrome extension, plugins like Gmail can connect multiple accounts, ChatGPT Voice can use plugins such as email, calendar, and Slack, and ChatGPT can now track your credit score if you let it.

AI News: $500 ChatGPT Plan? OpenAI Agent Leak & Opus 5.5

Brock Mesarich on Opus 5.5, the rumored $500 ChatGPT Pro Max tier, a leaked OpenAI "Aeon" agent, new ChatGPT voice mode, and Meta’s Muse Charm keychain.

Brock Mesarich's Friday AI roundup for September 25 covers a genuinely loaded week: Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5, OpenAI's two cheaper GPT-6 models, a Meta keychain AI device, and rumors of a brand-new OpenAI agent platform landing next week.

The headline is Claude Opus 5.5, the first model in Anthropic's new Claude 5.5 family. It performs at roughly the level of the flagship Fable 5.1 on most work, but costs about 40% less to run than Opus 5. API pricing drops to $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens, with cached input cheaper too, and it needs less compute to serve.

On the benchmarks Anthropic showed, Opus 5.5 beats Fable 5.1 across every listed task — including 66.4 versus 55.8 on agentic coding — and compares well against OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, losing only on business workflows and agentic scientific research. Brock is honest that computer use, the benchmark he watches most, improved only slightly. A code-migration test finished in 9.5 hours versus 12 for Fable 5.1 at 51% lower cost, and a spreadsheet-and-presentation test ran in 63 minutes versus 93 at half the cost.

Brock's bigger point is that the story here is cost per task, not new capability. A near-flagship model at half the price is a real improvement for everyday work. The change he's personally most excited about is communication: Opus 5.5 puts the most important information up front, uses less jargon, and follows the user's writing rules. He tested it against Fable 5.1's output on his own YouTube analytics and found the new model far easier to read at a glance — bolded key points, a table of video views, and a clear "three things to do today" list. Usage limits also got roomier: higher five-hour limits plus a savable reset for Pro, Max, Team, and seat-based enterprise users.

OpenAI answered with GPT-6 Sol and Luna, two cheaper, faster additions to the GPT-6 family alongside Astra. Astra stays the flagship at $10 input / $50 output per million tokens. Sol targets complex coding and professional work at $2 / $10. Luna handles everyday work at scale for $0.10 / $0.50. GPT-6 API prices are roughly halved versus GPT 5.6. All three are available in ChatGPT on paid plans, Codeex on paid plans, and via the API.

The title's "$500 ChatGPT plan" is a leak, and Brock is careful to say so. A code snippet surfaced by a "testing catalog" account points to a ChatGPT Pro Max tier at $500 per month — pure speculation, but Brock thinks ultra-expensive tiers are inevitable since providers have never gone above $200. As someone who hits the limits of the $200 plan himself, he'd consider it. He puts the question to viewers: ridiculous pricing, or justified by demand?

OpenAI also shipped a new ChatGPT voice mode with plugins, powered by the GPT-6 family. It can reach email, calendar, and Slack across ChatGPT work, web, and mobile, and it's rolling out globally. Brock's demo pulled his latest Drip email broadcast and its stats — 28.6% open rate, 0.59% click-through — through a Zapier MCP server bridging apps ChatGPT can't connect to directly, like Skool, Beehive, and Drip. His honest note: app pulls take 15–20 seconds, noticeably slower than plain conversation.

Meta entered the hardware fray with Muse Charm, a physical keychain device running its new MetaMuse personal assistant, announced at a Meta event the day before. Shipping target is December, there's no price yet, and Zuckerberg admitted only a couple of prototypes exist. Brock reads it as part of the week's theme: personal AI assistants moving onto phones, glasses, and now standalone devices.

The assistant theme continues with Grok. A leaked video shows Grok bot running inside a Tesla Cybertruck, managing inbox, calendar, and files. Grok also says it rebuilt customer support around Grok bot: a 175% increase in support tickets handled with no new hires, and roughly 200 hires it claims to have avoided.

Two smaller but practical items: the Claude mobile app now supports multiple accounts you can toggle between, and Brock walked through wiring a Neon database as shared memory — a "second brain" — across Claude Code, Codeex, and Grok bot via connectors, so context follows you between agents. He also posted a full tutorial for the viral Migos clip remakes (Sam Altman and Elon Musk rapping) using Higgsfield's Genjutsu video model: upload a reference video and character photos, add a simple character-swap prompt, render at 1080p.

The biggest rumor is OpenAI's "Aeon" agent (spelled A-E-O-N), possibly to be named ChatGPT Orbit. Per a tweet Brock cites, it would be more than a chatbot — its own profile, DMs, and groups designed to compete directly with Grok bot and Meta's Muse, launching as early as Thursday or at OpenAI Dev Day, with an entire social layer around it. OpenAI hired the creator of OpenClaw, which Brock assumes is connected. He also hears Anthropic will ship its own Grok bot competitor in the coming weeks. All of this is unconfirmed; his bet is that something real drops next week.

BREAKING: YouTube Just Announced Huge Changes

Think Media recaps the 12 biggest announcements from Made on YouTube 2026: video A/B testing, dynamic thumbnails, Ask Studio, custom AI feeds, and more.

Think Media's Sean Cann sat through the Made on YouTube 2026 keynote in New York City — where CEO Neal Mohan opened by saying more than three thousand updates have shipped to the platform since the start of the year, with about thirty-five of them covered on stage — and compressed roughly ten hours of research into the twelve announcements that actually matter to creators. Most of the thirty-five will barely touch your channel. A few could change it completely.

The headline feature is video A/B testing. Creators have long been able to split-test titles and thumbnails — over forty million of those tests have run since the feature launched in 2024 — but beginning in 2027, select creators will be able to test up to three different cuts of the video itself. YouTube will serve the cuts to different viewers, judge them on watch time, let the creator pick the winner, or automatically make the winning cut permanent after seven days. The versions can't be dramatically different — think two different hooks on the same edit, not a ten-minute video against a two-hour one — and TechCrunch confirmed it's coming to Shorts, with long-form details still pending in YouTube's help pages. MKBHD has already pushed back, asking how viewers will know they're watching a test, whether comments will attach to the right version, and what happens to timestamps. YouTube hasn't answered any of that yet.

Thumbnails got their own cluster of announcements. Dynamic thumbnails go beyond A/B testing: instead of finding one winner, YouTube will show different thumbnails to different viewers, Netflix-style, matching the image to the person's taste. There's no single winner; all three versions stay active. Separately, an automatic thumbnail swap arriving later this year lets YouTube Studio flag underperforming thumbnails and test replacements for you, and Ask Studio can now generate long-form thumbnails from a video's themes and your channel style, refined through conversation. Pre-publish feedback rounds out the packaging tools: upload a draft and Ask Studio will critique the script, hook, pacing, title, and thumbnail before anything goes public — on desktop now, mobile soon.

The back catalog is becoming a first-class citizen. Ask Studio is turning proactive — a kind of AI agent that reviews your old videos, suggests fresh thumbnails, and even drafts brand pitches using your channel's audience data. That ties directly into the most disruptive announcement, which DigiDay called the most disruptive development in the creator economy this year: swappable sponsor segments. Creators will be able to insert a sponsor read into up to twenty long-form videos at once, target segments by region with different call-to-action buttons and product shelves, and see sponsor performance data in YouTube Studio. It's been in testing since its announcement at Made on YouTube 2025, with no public rollout date yet — but the implication is that a channel's back library can be sold like a TV network sells ad time. YouTube is also investing hundreds of millions of dollars in agency incentives to bring more brand deals to creators, and creator partnerships plus a one-click brand access code are already live to make sponsorship deals easier.

Discovery and search are being rebuilt around AI. Analytics is being renamed Insights, and the Research tab is going outlier-based — keyword phrases are being retired, with a three-month window to export saved keywords as CSV. The new Research tab will surface videos from other channels that broke out, measured by outlier score: a video performing far above its channel's norm. Ask YouTube, the ask button on videos, has grown from twenty million users in December to one hundred forty million in June; conversational search rolled out to mobile in the US on August 12th, and now returns AI answers stitched from moments across many creators' videos. That creates discovery opportunity for well-structured videos — and a red flag about who keeps the affiliate commission when a purchase happens inside the AI answer rather than on a creator's video.

For viewers, custom feeds arrive this fall in the US: type a prompt like "home remodeling tips" and get a saved, self-updating homepage for that interest. Shorts series roll out globally with seasons, episodes, and custom thumbnails, driven by micro dramas — watch time for the format rose by half year over year, and TV viewership of micro dramas nearly doubled. YouTube Shopping now includes Amazon affiliate products, live in the US since summer and reaching India and Brazil soon, with tags switching to local stores for international viewers. AI comment moderation is in testing as an opt-in filter that learns from what you delete, hide, or allow, and conversational video editing — describing an edit in plain words and letting the app build a first draft — arrives in early 2027 for YouTube Create and Shorts, built on Gemini Omni. Honorable mentions included going live together, likeness protection for face and voice, and live autodubbing.

The stage statistics were enormous. YouTube is number one in US streaming watch time for the third straight year per Nielsen, the number one US podcast platform, with more than six hundred million people watching live content daily and over three hundred million engaging with posts daily — and posts are coming to television. The creator economy added over sixty billion dollars to US GDP in 2025, supporting five hundred forty thousand full-time-equivalent jobs, with brand deals on Shorts growing by well over half.

Cann's three takeaways: master the tools, the platform, and the craft — tools and taste, not tools alone. Treat your video library as your greatest asset, because old videos are about to get easier to optimize and monetize. And start now, because the best time to plant a tree was ten years ago and the second best time is today. Neal Mohan closed by declaring this the next chapter — the new YouTube — and insisting the breakthrough technology, the stories, and the culture will always be made on YouTube.

Open questions

  1. who owns the version history when videos become test subjects? If comments, timestamps, and viewer expectations attach to a cut that later loses the A/B test, the platform needs an answer for what survives — and viewers may reasonably want to know they're watching an experiment.
  2. when an AI answer stitches clips together and the viewer buys inside the answer, who gets the affiliate commission? The attribution rules for conversational search are the difference between a discovery engine and a middleman.
  3. does personalization at this scale fracture the platform? Custom feeds, per-viewer thumbnails, and multiple cuts of the same video mean there may soon be no single version of YouTube at all — which could be wonderful for relevance and disorienting for shared culture.

Mark Zuckerberg on how Meta's Muse AI agent will make people money

Zuckerberg tells Alex Heath why Meta is betting on Muse as the personal agent that earns its users money — and on long-lived, privacy-verified AI as the company’s defining product.

Mark Zuckerberg sat down with Alex Heath on Sources Podcast to talk about Muse, Meta's new personal AI agent — and the worldview behind it. The conversation, published September 8, 2026, ranged from Zuckerberg's long essay on AI's future to how he personally uses Muse, how Meta plans to make money on it, why he thinks keeping frontier AI in a few hands is dangerous, and what went wrong with Llama 4.

Heath opened on the essay. Zuckerberg's thesis is that the path to a positive AI future is to distribute the technology as widely as possible, resting on three principles: empowering people is the source of prosperity; AI's primary purpose will be inventing new things, not automation; and safety comes from checks and balances — a balance of power — rather than restricting access. He flatly disagrees with the Silicon Valley conventional wisdom that powerful models must be kept in a small number of labs. He is more worried about a handful of companies controlling something so capable than about broad access.

He illustrated the point with cybersecurity. The best antidote to an AI that can hack systems, he argued, is everyone having an AI to harden their own systems — pointing to the Hugging Face intrusion, where the team turned to open-source models precisely because they couldn't get the closed ones. Open-source software, because anyone can scrutinize it, has historically produced a more secure environment, and he thinks AI will follow the same pattern.

The data-center backlash came up next. Zuckerberg said Meta builds for decades, which changes the incentive: in Louisiana, tax revenue from a Meta project funded fifty-thousand-dollar bonuses for local teachers, and the company created America's Workforce Academy to train fiber technicians and electricians — guaranteeing graduates jobs on Meta infrastructure. He contrasted that with speculators who buy a plot of land just to flip it to a lab and have no interest in the local community. His message: if AI and its infrastructure don't create broad-based prosperity, "that's not going to be allowed to continue," so distribution of benefits is a precondition for scaling, not an afterthought.

The one-page personal superintelligence memo Zuckerberg published in the Wall Street Journal about a year ago is the moral core of the Muse project. His argument: when super intelligence arrives, who decides what it focuses on? A small number of experts would aim it at what they consider the big problems, but billions of individuals would choose differently — their health, their relationships, showing up for family, culture. He gave rare diseases as the example: pharma prioritizes the most common conditions, but a person with a rare condition would aim their agent at that instead. People should be able to direct the technology at what matters in their own lives.

Muse itself is the implementation. The unlock, as Zuckerberg described it, is a long-lived agent with a virtual machine behind the scenes — not one prompt in and one answer out, but goals and projects that the agent works on around the clock. It "studies overnight," consolidating its reflections into memory, and it proactively suggests new projects. He gave a personal example: he asked it to build a Civilization strategy guide for playing with his daughter, and it offered to expand the guide into historical lessons about each civilization. It can also learn anonymized insights across the whole fleet of agents — a network effect of learning that he claims no one else is doing.

He also described how he uses it himself: lining up weekend baking projects with his young daughter (the agent orders ingredients and learns from feedback that cake pops were too hard), snagging mountain-climbing permits for his older daughter, and watching cameras in his MMA gym to send coaching feedback. Early beta testers did wildly different things — one started running a homeschool on it within a day, another had it plan an entire trip in half a day. The differentiator, he said, is that people don't know what to do with AI, so an agent that suggests things for itself solves the biggest adoption problem.

On pricing, the pitch is aggressive: a huge amount of usage free — he said around a hundred million tokens a week — with an optional subscription. The long-term business model is a very small cut of transactions. Meta is working with Stripe on payments, and the idea is that someone running a small business can have the agent build the product, run ads on Instagram, and operate the business in a continuous loop. Zuckerberg said Meta is effectively standing behind the claim that the agent will make people money — that's how it pays for itself.

Three things he called out as differentiators. First, models designed from the ground up for personal superintelligence rather than post-trained for it — including capabilities like discretion, which gets trained in: the agent needs to know what's sensitive without asking a million questions, like booking a table with good mocktails for someone who's pregnant without announcing it. Second, Meta's social DNA: the company cares more about relationships than any other lab, and much of what people will use agents for is relational. Third, privacy and security: a confidential VM architecture so that even Meta cannot see what's inside your agent — a technically verifiable commitment. He personally recruited Moxie Marlinspike, the Signal founder, to architect it. Around that sit sentinel agents that watch incoming and outgoing traffic for prompt injection and force a human-in-the-loop approval for logins, payments, and sensitive transfers; connectors start read-only and expand only as asked, a least-privilege design.

Asked whether the personal-agent market is winner-take-all, he said probably not, but also not a free-for-all: he guessed fewer than a dozen companies will have the sophistication to do state-of-the-art work, with a power-law distribution around whoever is best at each use case. Meta, he argued, can be the best at both relationships and small business.

The lab-rebuilding story got real. Zuckerberg admitted Meta assumed LLM scaling would be like its other machine-learning work, and Llama 4 fell off the trajectory they needed. The fix was talent density: the smallest group of people who can keep the whole system in their heads, with each seat filled by the very best person. He spent enormous personal time on hiring, and the lab now literally sits around his office. Compute investment is ramping massively — many gigawatts, including the Prometheus gigawatt-scale cluster in Ohio. Muse Spark one point three is out, and "watermelon," a bigger pre-train, is coming soon.

On AI safety and government, his view is that the technology moves too fast for rigid frameworks — anything specific will be out of date in months. He'd rather partner closely with the government, telling them about major training runs proactively, and let liability and market incentives do the rest. He compared training an AI to parenting: clear, firm boundaries stop reward hacking, and those boundaries teach the model better values over time.

He closed on the smart-glasses privacy scare and the youth-safety settlement. The glasses, he said, were designed with privacy from day one — a visible light whenever recording, and tampering with the light disables the camera — but Meta stopped communicating that once the product scaled, and now has to say it loudly again. On teen safety, Meta is unilaterally limiting teen usage — time limits, notification windows, school and sleep hours — and hopes the settlement becomes a legally binding framework so YouTube and TikTok sign the same terms and no company is punished for going first.

Open questions

  1. If Muse really earns users money and takes a small cut of transactions, how does that change the relationship between Meta and the small businesses that already depend on its ad platform — partner or landlord?
  2. A technically verified "even Meta can't see inside" confidential VM is a strong claim; what happens to user trust if any implementation gap is ever found, and who audits the verifier?
  3. Zuckerberg wants every person to have a personal agent while also benefiting from fleet-wide learning — can anonymized learning across billions of agents actually stay anonymous at that scale?

How to Spot Exceptional Talent Before Everyone Else

Erik Torenberg, Josh Elman, Olivia Moore, and David Booth on a16z’s new reputation product Cosign — spotting exceptional talent before everyone else.

The episode opens with a provocation: the most important skill in the industry — maybe in the world — is your ability to identify things before the market. Cosign is a16z's attempt to productize that skill. It is a professional reputation platform for the startup community that makes one underpriced asset visible and durable: who believes in you.

The thesis lands through a thought experiment. What's more impressive to you: someone who went to Stanford, or someone who is followed by the operators you'd most want to learn from? Most people in the room would pick the latter. That's a peer-to-peer credential — an endorsement that this person is worth tracking — and it already operates as Silicon Valley's real currency. The problem is that it lives as exhaust: someone reads what you write, doors open for you, but the endorsement itself is invisible and ephemeral. Cosign tries to capture it.

Each profile is built around three core endorsements. First, who shaped your career — the mentor economy that Silicon Valley actually runs on. Second, who you'd work with anywhere, the people you'd go to the trenches with — a far more granular signal than a LinkedIn connection or a follow. Third, person to watch: your ranked call on who will ascend. The analogy is deliberate: building a great investor profile takes years of being right, and Cosign lets people build a parallel track record around people. The same logic extends to companies — conviction-staking for people who aren't investors, described on the show as something like fantasy investing with social capital.

Olivia Moore makes the information-asymmetry case the sharpest. After nearly a decade investing, she believes the most valuable data in Silicon Valley is knowing who has conviction — a real read on a person. Investors spend their days pinging their networks for it, and even they work hard to find the signal. An entry-level engineer choosing between five startup offers has no access to it at all, yet that choice can be the difference between a rocket ship and a dud. Moore ran a precursor with her twin sister Justine: a Sunday newsletter aggregating startup job postings that ran for nearly seven years, with campus scouts whose founders a16z later funded. It was, in her telling, the less scalable version of what Cosign is meant to be.

The product design splits private and public signal. Private signals are granular expressions of intent — I'd meet them, I'd fund them if they start a company, I'd hire them if they leave — matched only when both sides indicate interest, so introductions arrive with high intent and no awkward cold DM. Public signals are your durable lists: the designers you rate, the companies you'd stamp. The feed is deliberately narrow, focused on questions ("who's the best comms person for this?") and announcements (fundraises, job changes), because announcements are moments when reputation naturally accrues. The whole thing is positivity-by-design: a place to brag, accurately.

That framing carries through the long dissection of why LinkedIn and X fall short. LinkedIn is, in the panel's telling, an iconic product whose single signal — are we connected — has decayed from real trust into collecting. A connection tells you nothing about whether two people worked on the same team, whether one would vouch for the other, or whether they merely high-fived at a party. Ninety percent of "can you intro me to your connection?" requests, one guest jokes, end with an admission that they barely know the person. LinkedIn is about professional identity; Cosign wants to be about professional reputation. X has the opposite problem: its endorsement exhaust is incredibly valuable but ephemeral — a wave of "you should work with Josh" posts the day someone joins a firm, gone by morning.

Josh Elman, who worked at LinkedIn in 2004–05, adds the history. LinkedIn was built in three stages — growth, usage, revenue — and the network itself was always the substrate, with the "social resume" (a job application annotated by who can vouch for the candidate) as an early epiphany. But the granular connection strength never got built. The panelists also make the timing argument: in an age of AI-generated outreach where a hiring post draws two thousand responses today and will draw a hundred thousand soon, the scarce commodity is human endorsement, human conviction, human knowledge. AI is simultaneously what makes Cosign feasible — aggregating the internet's public signal at scale — and what makes it necessary.

On cold start, the approach is deliberately Wikipedia-flavored: seed third-party profiles from public sources, bragging on people's behalf, with an opt-out for anyone who doesn't want one. Everything pulled in comes from public sources — funding announcements, genuine positive comments surfaced from X. The guests stress this is an invitation, not a finished product: the community is meant to edit profiles, submit updates, and cosign the feedback itself.

The episode closes on Silicon Valley as meritocracy, and on the long-game answer to the obvious critique — why would I share my cosign, my alpha, publicly? Because people remember, at an order of magnitude, who believed in them first. The examples offered are canonical: Paul Graham naming Sam Altman among the best entrepreneurs in 2009, long before the exit; Ryan Hoover cosigning Erik Torenberg early in his career; Airbnb's IPO blog post writing about the first investor. Being first, publicly, is the strategy.

  2. The product leans on a16z's network effects — public third-party profiles seeded by AI. What stops the most powerful endorsement market from concentrating around the same insiders who already get discovered first?
  3. If Cosign succeeds at making reputation liquid and portable, does it flatten the "be so good they do business through you" advantage it celebrates — and would that be a feature or a bug?

How Silicon Valley Knows Its People

Erik Torenberg, Anu, and David Booth on how Silicon Valley really knows its people — belief, vouches, and the social graph that decides who gets backed.

How Silicon Valley Knows Its People, published by a16z News on September 25, 2026, is an essay on the mechanics of status — how private belief in a person becomes visible, legible, and compounding. It runs alongside the launch of a16z's new product Cosign, and the bylines credit Erik Torenberg, Anu, and David Booth.

The piece opens with a clean distinction that anchors everything after it. Belief is private conviction — the investments you make in someone with your time, attention, access, reputation, or money. Affiliation is what makes those investments legible to everyone else. What shifts as people read all these signals is status.

Then comes the essay's first reframing of a familiar VC trope. Everyone claims to want to be non-consensus and right, because that's the most lucrative bet. But being early matters far more than being non-consensus. If you're early enough, there's nothing to agree or disagree with yet. Someone gets captivated by a small signal, gives it attention or resources, sees some lift, doubles down, and soon others follow.

This is the essay's central engine: Silicon Valley knows people by watching other people know them. One person's visible belief becomes a signal to someone else, and the network updates its priors — a kind of live, recursive price discovery for people. Or, more romantically, the way all these signals build a person's lore.

The old status machinery — graduating college, getting into Stanford, becoming a Thiel Fellow, joining YC — still helps. But even these stamps and side doors matter less than they used to. People now collect status in bits, around the clock, often outside any formal system, and crucially, a lot of it is earned in public. Showing your work in public has become the new warm intro — which in turn gets you plenty of actual warm intros.

Then the two-move model of ascendancy: self-made wins and high-status vouches. The earlier someone is in their trajectory, the less proof exists, and the more meaningful the vouch becomes. Belief compounds. Being an early believer is itself a stamp of skill and taste — everyone wants to be the person who discovered the band while it was still underground. The examples land: Pharrell's viral reaction to Maggie Rogers was career-making; Paul Graham included Sam Altman in his Five Founders essay in 2009. As the piece puts it, "non-consensus" people are just unknown, until they aren't.

Vouches take many forms — a tweet, an invite, an introduction, an angel check — each its own version of a co-sign. Being undeniable and getting credible people to say so is how you become somebody in Silicon Valley.

The essay then zooms into the platform where this game is played most intensely: Tech Twitter. So much of the Valley's social life has been bundled onto the same graph, and the graph tells on everyone — who follows whom, who's mutuals with who. The game is described as MySpace Top 8 revived without the lock-in: Tech Twitter is Top 8 by soft, constant, often-public affiliation.

These affiliations spill into broader new media. Once someone gets noticed enough, they show up in newsletters and profiles, on podcasts and shows. The media itself becomes part of the affiliation graph — hosts and guests vouch for each other, and quotes and clips get fed back into the timeline as social proof. Insider media, the essay says, is the Valley's breaking news and reality TV, covering the action while making its people legible even faster.

Not all vouches are equal, and the essay is honest about the hierarchy. A Marc Andreessen tweet carries more weight than a tweet from the average reader. But some of the highest-signal vouches go unseen — in backchannels and group chats, where the question is the blunt one: are they good? How good? Top ten percent? Top five? Or simply: would I regret it forever if I passed?

The piece draws a sharp line between cheap and expensive signals. Likes are pennies now; in-person time is expensive. An in-person signal is stronger because seats are scarce, an invite puts the host's reputation on the line, and nobody can half-watch the conversation. But even offline vouches make their way back online — the timeline is the tabloid multiplier.

The punchline: Silicon Valley's bankable social graph is much less about who worked where, or even who knows whom, than who chooses to be associated with whom, how, and when. The affiliation graph is the visible layer of the belief graph. And the liberating part is that belief isn't tied to where you started — it's about what you're up to and where you're headed.

The AI era sharpens everything. As output gets abundant and attention stays scarce, it gets harder to tell what and who stands out, and the human layer matters more. Who noticed? Who believes? We start reading the second-order signals closer. Being illegible can be charming, but not for long — you still have to be good; it just helps enormously if people can watch you be good. And momentum is the new metric: having ten thousand followers isn't as exciting as seeing someone get them in a week. We're in a continuous-proof-of-potential world now — up and to the right, if you can.

The essay closes its strategic case on praise itself. The generous and strategic motivations behind praising someone are hard to separate — you might genuinely want them to win, you might like what believing in them says about your own taste, and if you're early and right, you get status for it. All three can work together: you get credit for seeing someone early, and they get seen. Alex Danco's framing appears here too — the "social subsidy" of angel investing, and the "Social Fog of War," the useful ambiguity around status that keeps the network open. The closing rules of thumb: from Survivor, treat everyone like they could be on top tomorrow; from Danco, treat people as if they might be the next great founder. Just as VC funds have anti-portfolios of companies missed, plenty of us have anti-portfolios of people we saw early, believed in fast, and wish we'd vouched for sooner.

The parting stance: it's fine if we know who has status, as long as we don't know who will next. Every new technical era reshuffles the order — who the promising unknowns are, who sees them first, who's best fit to play the newest game of legibility. We're in one of those moments now. And the final line is the whole piece in five words: if you believe in someone early, say so.

Open questions

  1. if vouches get systematized into products like Cosign — public, trackable, legible — does the signal get stronger or does it get gamed? The essay's whole economy of early belief depends partly on the Social Fog of War staying foggy.
  2. the piece argues AI makes the human layer of judgment more valuable, not less. But if output gets abundant and second-order signals get noisier too, what keeps the belief graph honest — what stops it from becoming pure momentum-trading?
  3. the playbook is brutally clear: be good, be visible, move fast, get vouched for early. For people who genuinely can't play the legibility game — the ones who do their best work in private — is there still a path into the network, or does the public-by-default machine quietly close that door?

Stanford Doctor: Get More Out of Your Next Doctor's Visit With AI

Stanford physician Dr. Jonathan Chen on how to get more out of your next doctor’s visit with AI — and the failure modes to avoid.

Silicon Valley Girl (Marina Mogilko) sat down with Dr. Jonathan Chen, a Stanford physician, computer scientist, and director of AI in medical education at Stanford Medicine. The conversation is practical from the start: how ordinary people should actually use AI for health questions, what doctors are good for when the machine knows more, and where the traps are.

Chen's team is famous for one study, and it comes up immediately. A couple of years ago they gave practicing doctors access to GPT-4, fully expecting the human-plus-machine combination to beat the machine alone. It did not. GPT-4 by itself outperformed the doctors who had access to GPT-4.

That finding still bothers him. It flies in the face of what he calls the fundamental theorem of informatics — the comforting idea that a human plus a computer will always beat either one working alone. "Maybe the human is getting in the way," he says. "Am I just slowing the computer down?"

Part of the answer is embarrassingly mundane. A third of the doctors in the study had never used a chatbot before in their lives. In follow-ups where researchers taught people to use the tools, the human-plus-AI combo got better — but still not better than the AI on its own. For question-answering and knowledge retrieval, he says, the models are "pretty freaking good," which is why health is the most popular category of question for AI chatbots.

The bigger problem is what happens when untrained people bring their own instincts to the chatbox. Chen cites a study from Nature Medicine that ran a triage scenario — should you go to the emergency room — with regular people instead of doctors. The people given AI did worse than the people given nothing. The mechanism is prompting yourself into a corner: you ask in a way that hints at the answer you want, and the chatbot confidently agrees and walks you down the wrong path.

He describes AI as a mirror. Do good diagnosis and it makes you better at it; start from a wrong hunch and it amplifies your ability to be wrong. "Very powerful alien intelligent technology landed on the planet," he says, "and just dropping it in people's laps doesn't actually solve all our problems."

Marina demonstrated the constructive side over the summer. She fed seven years of lab results, her 23andMe raw data, and everything her doctors had ever told her into Perplexity, then asked it what to ask each new doctor and what they should know about her. It even spotted a genetic detail she hadn't known about. Chen calls this a net good — your doctor would love to walk through all of it with you but will never do it at two in the morning. The time you save gets spent on what actually needs a human.

But the same power is a chainsaw. He tells the New York Times story he says he was waiting for: a former pastor asked a chatbot about a bad blood clot, was told everything was fine, stayed home, and nearly died. The keyword, Chen says, is accountability. The AI probably is smarter than most doctors, but knowing everything doesn't make someone worthy of trust.

His practical advice starts with what the models are genuinely good at. Have them translate your medical records and doctor's notes into plain language — they handle jargon and implications well. General medical knowledge is strong; once you're picking chemotherapy regimens, get more opinions, including AI in the mix. The revolutionary capability isn't the knowledge, though. It's the dialogue: you can ask follow-up questions, argue back, and explore your specific situation, which no static article can do.

How you ask matters enormously. His rule: describe objectively, never state your theory. Don't write "could this nasty rash be scabies, should I go to the ER?" — the model will follow your train of thought. Say your daughter has a rash, it's never happened before, she's scratching heavily, here's what it looks like. And know the models are tuned to over-triage: they'd rather send you to the emergency room unnecessarily than miss something and get sued.

A trick he likes for uncertain situations: ask the model to argue against itself. If it says go to the ER, ask it to convince you why it's not serious, and vice versa. It turns the chatbot into a sparring partner. This gets at his deeper point about judgment. A computer can tell you chemotherapy might extend a terminal patient's life by two months with terrible side effects — but "is it worth it?" is a question of values, not facts. Only a human can answer that.

On which AI to use, he's honest about his own habits. The big frontier models are all good at general medical knowledge, since they digested the same internet. He noticed some are more forthcoming — he names Grok as one — while others refuse topics his patients then take elsewhere. Doctor-specific tools like Glass Health and OpenEvidence exist but require medical licenses. His own lab is building a test framework to evaluate every model's safety, because, he admits, "I don't even really care which one is best, I just want to use it so I can help patients — but I need to know which one is safest." He uses ChatGPT himself, plus Claude for writing work. ChatGPT for health, he clarifies, is mostly a privacy and security play — separating your health data from your dinner recipes.

One prompting trick actually works, but not for the reason people think. Telling the model "I'm a doctor" doesn't make its answers more accurate; it formalizes the style, producing structured, technical responses. That's gold if you're an analytical person who wants detail, and overwhelming if you're not. His advice: don't ask it to make decisions — ask it to reformat. "Translate this into a form I can absorb" is where these tools shine.

Then the failure modes most people don't expect. Chen says the characteristic error in medical summarization isn't hallucination — it's omission. Ask a model to compress a thousand pages of hospital notes into one paragraph and it will confidently leave out the one lung nodule someone should have followed up. That's not an AI problem, he says; it's a communication problem. The full drug label is ten pages long, which might as well be nothing.

Stanford plugged a chatbot into its Epic medical records system, and it exposed another weakness: the model doesn't know what numbers and dates mean. Feed it a decade of notes and it will happily discuss a seven-year-old lab result as if it's today's — context rot. Worse is what he calls "chart lore": once a stigma or diagnosis lands in your chart, every doctor copy-pastes it forward forever, and the AI confidently repeats it. A homeless man labeled schizophrenic by assumption kept the label through every summary even after psychiatry ruled it out.

The episode takes a spicy turn on testing. Full-body MRIs and cancer blood tests for healthy, asymptomatic people almost always fail the math — the diseases are rare enough that the numbers don't work. The Galleri test isn't accurate enough to change anything. He wishes pancreatic cancer screening existed; five people in his family died of it, and nothing works. Even the annual physical doesn't move outcomes, though the lab checks for cholesterol and blood sugar do.

His framing is a pie chart of what actually determines your health: roughly half is behavior, about thirty percent is genetics, and only about twenty percent is medical care. Doctors, he admits, are terrible at the biggest slice. They hand you a statin and walk out the door. The optimistic flip is that AI companions — the ones you talk to every day — might succeed where the doctor you see twice a year can't, supplying the nudges and accountability that actually change behavior.

Genetics is high-leverage only in specific cases. Finding familial hypercholesterolemia can prevent a heart attack before fifty with existing medications — most people discover it only when the attack happens. For the other ninety percent of the population, the results are interesting but not actionable.

On early cancer detection, he's measured. Tracking biomarkers over a decade might reveal signals humans could never synthesize — that's the hope. But detection isn't cure: many tiny lesions your body would kill off anyway, and catching them only creates panic. Prostate cancer is the classic case — most men who die with it would have been fine doing nothing.

For people bouncing between doctors with no answer, he offers a prompt template: here's what's been tried, what might everyone be missing, is there another test worth asking about? Don't lean toward any theory — just ask for another opinion. Before an appointment, ask what four bullet points the doctor needs to know, so you don't waste the visit rehashing history.

Then the uncomfortable chapter: deskilling. In a colonoscopy study, doctors using AI vision found more polyps — and when the system was turned off, they performed worse than before they'd ever used it. They had delegated the skill and lost it. Which skills are we okay surrendering, he asks, and which must stay foundational? Nobody has a simple answer.

That question shapes his answer for what a doctor is at all: competence, communication, and character — all three in one person. Medical students still need knowledge, he argues, because judgment grows out of knowledge; you can't evaluate what you can't parse. He gently disagrees with Bill Gates's claim that AI will replace most doctors within a decade: if a teacher is only an answer-transmitter, sure, the computer wins. But mentoring, advising, and being a role model are different jobs entirely.

The automated future, though, is already arriving. He got a prescription through a few clicks of a chat window for a minor skin condition. Utah is running a regulatory experiment where companies handle medication refills with no human in the loop and no human double-check — "the Trojan horse," he calls it, the foot in the door.

Will AI cure all diseases in ten years? "You're going to make me be such a downer," he laughs — no. Biology is smarter than any AI we'll build. But the front door to the doctor's office is going to get dramatically easier, boringly normal, within a few years. His deepest optimism is about access: expertise is the scarcest medical resource, and AI manufactures it. His own breakthrough this year wasn't medical at all — it was agentic workflows, which he calls "actual magic" after his first real attempts.

The throughline for patients is stakes-based. AI can summarize your information and surface questions you'd never think of. The final decision still belongs to you and your doctor — not because the doctor is smarter, but because they hold the context of your values, your risk tolerance, your life. The people who get hurt are the ones who can't exercise judgment to catch the machine's mistakes. Know the caveats and you've got concierge medicine in your pocket.

His closing trick: never settle for one model. Cross-examine. Ask one chatbot's answer to be checked by the others, then have them synthesize. More opinions smooth out the errors — with AI, strangely, just like with doctors.

Open questions

  1. if the human-plus-AI combination keeps losing to the AI alone, what exactly is the doctor for — and does medicine's answer change if the gap keeps widening as models improve?
  2. when automated systems are already prescribing without any human in the loop, what should the regulatory line be between refills of safe drugs and the first genuinely consequential automated diagnosis?
  3. if AI companions prove better than doctors at changing health behavior — the largest slice of the health pie — who bears responsibility when the companion nudges someone toward harm?

For now humans still decide what's worth testing and check the results; he doubts that lasts long. "AI going well looks like millions of tireless researchers," he wrote earlier this week. Sarah Paine — How to win a war

Historian Sarah Paine’s finale lecture on how to win a war — Clausewitz, the Russo-Japanese War, and the culminating point of victory.

On the Dwarkesh Podcast, on September 25, 2026, historian Sarah Paine gave a finale lecture on the hardest problem in war: not how to fight one, but how to get out of one. Her argument is that getting into wars is easy; getting out of them with what you wanted is extremely difficult. The whole lecture is an anatomy of war termination, built around the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905, which she calls a model case — and then two pathologies that turn regional wars into disasters.

She starts with the math that made Japan's victory so striking. By every quantifiable measure — territory, population, industrial base, resource endowment, army size, navy size — Russia was a multiple of Japan. Japan won because it defined limited objectives and stuck to them: Russia out of Korea and Manchuria. Neither side sought regime change. Japan then exceeded its goals, taking the southern half of Russia's Manchurian railway concessions, the naval base at Port Arthur, and the southern half of Sakhalin Island. The metric of success, Paine stresses, is not winning battles — the United States won most conventional battles in Vietnam and nobody calls that a win — but achieving the objective for which the war was fought.

The intellectual spine of the lecture is Clausewitz. Paine walks through two paired concepts: the culminating point of attack, an operational term covering a battle or campaign, and the culminating point of victory, which applies to the entire war. Go too far past either and the reaction comes back stronger than the original attack. She illustrates the first with the siege of Port Arthur. Japan had only four armies and one was pinned at Port Arthur for nearly a year; General Nogi threw four frontal assaults at the fortress, losing some 45,000 men — the size of an entire Japanese army. Paine argues the culminating point of attack arrived when Japan took 203 Meter Hill, from which spotters could direct siege guns to sink every Russian ship in the harbor within three days. Port Arthur was pivotal rather than decisive — it foreclosed Russian options and opened Japanese ones.

For the culminating point of victory she offers five candidate explanations for why Russia gave up, and invites listeners to choose: the domestic implosion of the 1905 revolution after the massacre of peaceful petitioners destroyed the Tsar's legitimacy; the land battle of Mukden, where Japan threw in literally everyone while Russia's army kept growing via the Trans-Siberian Railway; money — Japan could still borrow, Russia was broke and no one would lend to it; the naval battle of Tsushima, a lopsided Japanese victory that grew the Japanese fleet with captured Russian ships; and the seizure of Sakhalin Island at the end, a prestige wound Nicholas II couldn't absorb. Running through all of this is Clausewitz's image of the Bologna flask — a glass vessel you can hammer nails with, but which shatters if its inner surface is scratched. You never know going in whether the first shock of battle will stiffen the enemy's resolve, as with Putin in Ukraine, or shatter it, as with Nicholas II.

Paine's deeper point is about exit strategy. Japan picked its peacemaker — Theodore Roosevelt — before the first shot was fired, lining up Harvard-connected intermediaries so that at Mukden, when Field Marshal Yamagata saw Japan had exhausted its forces while Russia's were still growing, he could call in the Americans to mediate. The Gulf War gets the same treatment: George H. W. Bush stated explicit limited objectives in National Security Directive 54 — Iraqi withdrawal from Kuwait, the restored Kuwaiti government, protected American lives — achieved all three, and barely paid for it. Thirty-four allies sent over 200,000 troops and paid more than 50 billion dollars to fund the American deployment, plus another 14 billion to coalition partners. Coalition deaths totaled 392; Iraq lost 20,000 to 35,000 troops. Compare that with Korea, where the United States exceeded the culminating point of victory twice: first Kim Il-sung, who was roaring down the peninsula until the US–UN intervention turned a civil war into a regional war; then MacArthur, who after the Inchon landing pushed to reunify the whole peninsula until China poured hundreds of thousands of troops in and he was relieved of command. The political objective must always be primary, she says, quoting Clausewitz — the military objective is never the master.

Then she turns to unlimited objectives and Sun Tzu's concept of death ground — terrain where an army fights with the courage of desperation because there is no alternative. An unlimited objective means regime annihilation, and people on death ground fight to the death. Ukraine, Paine notes, is the live example: Russia put Ukrainians on death ground, and they defied everyone's expectations. Unlimited wars are conceptually simple and brutally hard to execute; limited wars are conceptually tricky but much easier to carry off.

The two pathologies follow. First: regional wars that escalate into global wars. The American Revolution, where Britain wanted regime change in the colonies but the colonists didn't, protracted the war and pulled in France, Spain, and the Dutch — a regional revolt became a global war Britain chose to exit, keeping the Caribbean and dropping the colonies. World War I, where every ally wanted different things and no settlement could satisfy even one side's own alliance. Japan's war in Asia, where the invasion of Manchuria slid into all of China, and the attempt to cut off foreign aid to the Nationalists led straight to Pearl Harbor. Common threads: a theater too large to close off, third parties with time to intervene, and enemies on death ground who never stop.

Second pathology: victory denial through insurgency. Defeat someone conventionally and they may simply refuse to stop, bleeding you forever. Paine traces the playbook to George Washington — a conventional Continental Army forcing British concentration while militias roam the countryside — through China against Japan and Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan against the United States. It's a cost-imposition strategy that bets the occupier's value of the objective is lower than the defender's. She adds a warning about mosaic societies like Iraq, where primary identities are ethnic rather than national: after regime change, the "loaded diaper" is yours, and border arithmetic alone should have given pause — Gulf War one policed 158 miles of border; taking the whole country meant over 2,000 miles, with Saudi Arabia, Iran, and Turkey all invested in the outcome.

She closes with the questions to ask before any war: who is involved and who is interested; who has limited versus unlimited objectives against whom; for whom is the objective existential and for whom merely nice to have; who has geographic access and sanctuary. Ukraine, she notes in passing, matters more to Ukrainians than to Russians — it's where they live.

Open questions

  1. What would a culminating point of victory even look like in Ukraine — for either side — and is it reachable, or are the objectives and geography too tangled to admit one?
  2. How should the "Bologna flask" question be answered for a nuclear-armed autocracy — is there any way to tell whether pressure shatters resolve or stiffens it before the war starts?
  3. Which of Paine's five candidate explanations for the Russo-Japanese War's end is right — and what does the answer imply about which lever matters most in forcing a modern war to close?

How to scale intent, quality, and artistry with AI | Katie Dill (Stripe)

Stripe design leader Katie Dill, live at the Lenny and Friends summit, on scaling intent, quality, and artistry in the AI building boom.

Published September 25, 2026 on Lenny's Podcast, in a talk recorded live at the Lenny and Friends Summit in San Francisco on September 10, 2026. Katie Dill, Head of Design at Stripe, gives a roughly twenty-two-minute keynote on how product teams can use AI without drowning in sameness — and why craft matters more, not less, when anyone can build.

Dill opens with an architectural warning. Right after World War II, America went through the largest building boom in history. Builders copied the modernist style of the 1920s — clean geometries, bare surfaces, no adornment — without any of the intentionality behind it. The thinking got thinner and thinner until all that was left were generic patterns ill-suited to their context: zombie buildings, she calls them, with no care for the user, the neighborhood, the brand, or the context.

We are in another building boom now — the AI building boom — and the echoes are real. Teams of three can do what used to take teams of thirty. But the same copying-without-thinking is starting to show up on screens, and Dill names three watch points for all of us.

First, large language models are great at telling you the most probable answer — what has been or is popular now, what has worked, what was in style. They are much worse at telling you what is original or specific to you, your brand, and your users' context. Her example: a website that looks like generic software company output but actually sells Korean barbecue. Fine as a site; no character, no context.

Second, there is the temptation of done. AI makes things feel finished very fast — often prematurely. Dill confesses to a microwave-burrito habit: frozen rock to lunch in ninety seconds, and she is willing to overlook serious flaws because she is so enamored of the speed. The same happens with AI-generated interfaces: a sentence of prompting produces a nice little interface with rounded corners and drop shadows, and it feels done. But an apparently polished state can be misleading. Does it really solve the problem? Does it differentiate?

Third, work that is this easy to do starts feeling disposable, and the responsibility of our decisions falls by the wayside. Nobody asks who will maintain this thing in two years. The three watch points together point at a future full of zombie UI — monotonous, vacant, uncared for.

Care is the antidote, and it shows up in small things. Dill gives Stripe examples: the Grokbot and its little animation that brings it to life, the date shown in a calendar tab that signals the builder thought about you, the Link agent wallet that anticipates the problems an AI agent will have while buying online and builds troubleshooting in for a user that isn't even human. The character of the brand shows through, and the work has soul. We can do this with AI — she has four recommendations for how.

The first: you have to have a point of view. If you don't, AI will give you one, and it will be generic and backward-looking. Define what your brand is for you and for your users — who you want to be to them, what they care about — and let that become your standards. At Stripe, the team cares a lot about optimism, and it shows up in details big and small: the colors, the writing, what they write about, the products they ship to support entrepreneurs.

This matters especially when ownership is diffuse. Everybody is contributing to the product in more ways than ever, and AI makes it easy to abdicate. Dill tells the story of a design crit over an ad featuring Stripe's parallelogram with customer visuals. Something was off, and a cross-functional partner in the room wanted to ship it fast. The discussion surfaced a strange question — what is the quality standard for something made with AI? — and the answer became a principle: it doesn't matter to users how it was made. It matters whether it's good. That principle drove the work, and the team left with seventeen bullet points of improvements. The team even coined a verb for meticulous craft: "Pepsi bubbling." The goal is not perfection; it is going one level deeper than the customer can see, and letting that meticulousness show.

Developing a point of view, Dill says, is really about getting good at noticing — what your users need versus what they say, what signals good and great and meh in the products around you, and in analogous situations like art and science that broaden your taste.

The second recommendation: encode your standards into the machine. Humans are no longer in every decision loop — interfaces get built with no designer in the room, agents find problems and fix them overnight, generative UI is built in real time for users. That is why design systems are having a moment again, but the system of yesterday is different from the system of today. It used to be that you didn't have to write everything down because a designer was always in the room to fill in the blanks. Now the decisions have to be easier to define, and the object of design is no longer the screen — it is the system itself.

Dill tells the lesser-known part of the Gutenberg story: he didn't just invent 26 lowercase and 26 uppercase letters. He cut 290 unique characters — different widths, abbreviations, ligatures — so that fully justified type wouldn't have the rivers and lakes of white space that erode the beauty of the page. He made the system both extensible and opinionated enough to feel handmade, even though it was machine-made. That is the bar.

At Stripe, the team started with an MCP server that understood their design documentation, and the results weren't great — not specific enough, and three people with the same prompt got three different results. They evolved to a CLI built on the design system, a harness that makes the AI far more obedient: it's where the builders already are, and it consumes the documentation at the right time and place to avoid context rot. Crucially, this system isn't just components and atomic parts — it is full templates and flows, so the system knows how the product is supposed to behave end to end. The standards are embedded into the means of production, and the product is more coherent. But that is just the baseline. Dill quotes Christopher Alexander: a system can satisfy every rule and still be dead.

Hence the third recommendation: refuse to confuse done with good. The quality filter used to be built into every stage of product development — twenty ideas, staffing for one, poking and prodding and pruning along the way. Now teams can build twenty ideas in a week. That is liberating, and it means finally reacting to the real thing instead of theorizing about hypothetical products. But the filtering that used to happen throughout the process now has to happen after the build, when it is much harder to say no. That is where the editor role comes in — someone looking end to end and asking whether the thing is actually a whole. Built does not mean done, and done does not mean good.

The most important editing skill, Dill says, is experiencing it like a user would. Does it actually solve the problem? Is it attuned to how the user thinks? Is it coherent? Lots of things look good in isolation and feel disconnected across the full journey. And it's not just about yes or no — it's about pushing something incomplete to completion. She cites an article by Nabil Koreshi on what makes art great: unexpected details, the things that make you say "I can't believe they thought of that," and the deeper meaning or continuous themes that tie the surface together into a whole. AI is not great at those. But knowing the gaps helps us fill them — as Koreshi says, what offends us about AI slop is the sense that the details don't matter, that the cup is green but might as well have been blue. An editor takes accountability for every decision, which is in many ways every pixel.

Her example is from the summit itself. Her team designed the event; Stefan built the opening animation, starting from a 3D model of the scene and using AI for the animations. The first version was cool but felt subtly off — the motion wasn't quite right, it didn't quite show enough of the scene. Fifty-six iterations later, it became something truly beautiful, with more realism and life. Had he not used AI, he might not have attempted such a complex scene or tried so many viewpoints. It was not one-and-done — it took scrutiny and wherewithal — but AI opened the possibility space.

The fourth recommendation is her favorite: unleash creativity and artistry. AI can manufacture monotony, but it is also the greatest creative catalyst we have ever had. This matters because when everybody and their mother is building something, differentiation gets harder and more important. The interfaces of today — chats, charts, CLIs — are surely not the epitome of great interaction in the modern era. It is time to invent new interfaces and new aesthetics, the way multi-touch made wholly new interactions possible and the synthesizer made wholly new sounds possible. The best practices have not been written yet.

At Stripe, the team uses AI to amplify the creative team. The cover of Built to Grow was made by a human marbler working through stunning iterations, with AI fine-tuning the details — colors and lines in all the right places — scaling good human judgment into something stunning. Dill's advice for making AI a stronger creative partner: improve your inputs (specific prompts, your source material, your standards — tell it what you believe, what good is, what you care about), stress your outputs (never accept the first version; push one step further), and use adversarial agents to critique and rough up the work. But the harder thing is cultural: it is easy and safe to follow cookie-cutter patterns, and harder to protect the strange. If you're leading a team, don't just tell them to use AI — give them room to explore. AI lowers the cost to create; spend some of the savings on making something truly special. Use AI to raise the ceiling, not just the floor.

She closes with Gothic architecture. In 1850, John Ruskin wrote about quality and craft and held up Gothic architecture as the epitome: no two Gothic buildings are alike — not even one column like another — each detail uniquely crafted, showing the hand and mind of its maker, feeling truly cared for. Users are not impressed that we animated something with 3JS and Blender in thirty minutes. They are impressed when we solve their problems, when we anticipate their needs in the details, when the brand has character behind it. We have a choice in this building boom: make the digital equivalent of zombie buildings, or make it a creative renaissance. "Let's make some products that are more powerful and show the hand and care of the maker."

Open questions

  1. when every builder has the same powerful models and the same design-system harness, where does durable differentiation actually come from — is taste a learnable skill, or a kind of capital that concentrates?
  2. Dill's editor role is doing the quality filtering post-build now — but as agents build more of the product while we sleep, who gets to be the editor, and how do they stay sharp enough to judge work they didn't touch?
  3. if the goal is to raise the ceiling and protect the strange, how should teams measure that — what stops "raise the ceiling" from becoming a slogan that covers the same zombie UI?
