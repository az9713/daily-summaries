Lenny's Podcast · Rob Snyder · The PULL framework for product-market fit

How to find product-market fit (but, actually) | Rob Snyder (Author, The Power of Pull)

On Lenny's Podcast, the episode "How to find product-market fit (but, actually) | Rob Snyder (Author, The Power of Pull)" is a long conversation between host Lenny Rachitsky and Rob Snyder, whose book and framework grew out of his own bruising years as a founder. Snyder has since helped roughly fifty startups go from zero to over a million dollars in annual revenue, and his whole approach can be stated in one line: stop trying to convince people to want your product, and instead find the people who are already trying to pull it out of your hands.

His origin story explains the conviction. As a founder he did everything the standard advice prescribes. He found hair-on-fire pain points, offered a differentiated solution with real value, and had design partners using the product every day, and still spent two years smashing into a wall, pivoting without understanding why. A later, almost accidental pivot took off, and he concluded that pain points and value propositions were never the thing that makes someone buy. What makes someone buy is a priority: a project they are actively trying to get done right now that their existing options cannot get done.

That is the pull framework, and it is an acronym. P is a project on someone's to-do list, something like hiring more employees rather than buying recruitment software. U is unavoidable: the project has to happen now, for some concrete reason, not eventually. The first L is the list of options the person has already considered for getting the project done, which might be doing it themselves, hiring someone, or using tools already on the market. The second L is the limitations of those options, the reasons none of them are good enough. Snyder is emphatic that all four have to be present at once. Find a project without the unavoidable, and people will complain and not buy. Find the project and the urgency but with options that are good enough, and they will simply do it themselves.

His favorite worked example is Jump, an AI notetaker for financial advisors. The project on every advisor's list is minimizing the time spent taking client meeting notes and entering them into a CRM. It is unavoidable because the notes are legally required, with real regulatory exposure if they are skipped. The options advisors had tried, generic AI notetakers, were limited in a specific way: they did not integrate with advisor CRMs, and it was unclear whether they complied with SEC rules. So when Jump showed up built exactly for that gap, advisors ripped it out of the founders' hands, after the same founders had spent eighteen months hitting their heads against the wall. The contrast case is insurance agents, who have the identical project but no unavoidable, since nothing requires them to log meeting notes at all. Same product, same project, no pull.

Two refinements matter. First, pull is a property of a person, not a company. You are never selling to SpaceX; you are selling to the head of legal at SpaceX, who has a specific project stuck on her to-do list. Second, the test of pull is behavioral. A person with pull behaves as if it would be weird not to buy: they ask how to get started and whether the product exists yet. Everyone else is, in Snyder's terms, weird to buy, and they will say enthusiastic, complimentary things that sound encouraging and mean nothing. Pull, crucially, exists before you arrive and independent of you. You cannot manufacture it by persuading harder. You can only go find it, which he describes as liberating, because it replaces self-blame with a search problem.

For coming up with ideas, his advice is to earn the insight firsthand rather than brainstorm product concepts, which he admits tend to sound unhinged on paper. One friend had no product idea at all, only a wish to serve small businesses, so he shadowed industry after industry, riding along with solar panel installers before landing on independent physical therapists, spending days watching over their shoulders and even considering buying a franchise to see the books. The pull only became visible by observation: a specific cost the therapists were trying to cut and could not cut with the options available. Across the fifty or so companies Snyder has worked with, he estimates about ten percent of the ideas came from the founder's own experience and ninety percent from going to get firsthand experience. Solving your own problem qualifies, with a catch: it only counts if you honestly would have paid someone to solve it for you, the way Emily at Clearly AI, formerly a security engineer at AWS, would have paid for the thing she ended up building. Sara Blakely tearing the feet off her pantyhose because she needed them gone before an event is his consumer-side example.

Once you have an insight, you turn it into a pull hypothesis, written in a fixed template: there is a project on your to-do list, it is unavoidable right now for this reason, the options you have looked into are these, and they fall short in this way. Then you show it to the person, ideally on two slides, one stating what you heard and asking whether you have it roughly right, and one sketching how you could help. What you are watching for is what he calls a pull-shaped action: they try to buy, asking how it works and how to move forward now, rather than leaning back with praise and a request for more information to review later. And you check that it repeats, talking to a handful of similar people, so you are not building a company for an audience of one.

When a conversation lands lukewarm, which is most of the time, he throws what he calls the Hail Mary: is there anything on your to-do list that you would pay me to do right now? It sounds like a surrender and works like a probe. One company trying to sell an applicant tracking system to governments heard, after the Hail Mary, that what the buyer would actually pay for was help with job descriptions, and that became their first contract and the seed of a fast-growing company. Snyder, a self-described mega introvert, says the question also reframes selling itself: you are not pressuring anyone, you are simply checking whether they are stuck on something you might help with. If they are not, you have learned something cheaply.

The traps he warns about are building first, heads down, because you believe people have to see the thing to get it, which delays the cold wind of reality for months, and design partners. He has yet to see a design partnership help. In his telling, design partners agree to your vision, give you generous feedback, play at being Steve Jobs, and never buy, because people who genuinely have something urgent to get done do not want to co-design your product. They want it done.

Getting the first calls is its own craft. His target is two to three customer conversations a day, and his cold outreach is deliberately not a pitch. He calls it founder magic: one or two sentences saying something true and interesting about you, with no value proposition attached. One startup's entire message was that Snoop Dogg had invested in them and they thought the recipient might find that interesting too. Others have used dropping out of a PhD, having been Sam Altman's chief of staff, or having worked at the family plumbing company in high school, matched to who the customer is. He has not seen AI personalization work at this stage, because it tends to tell people things they already know about themselves and reads as machine-written. On LinkedIn he suggests adding a hundred or more people a week and messaging those who accept; his benchmarks are that thirty to forty percent should accept, a good share of those should reply, and most replies should be an easy yes. If your numbers fall well short, the message or your profile is the problem.

The call itself runs about thirty minutes and has two steps, discovery and then a small conceptual pitch. His opening question is disarming: why did you take this call with me today? Often the answer fills in the whole pull framework unprompted. Then he sets an agenda with the customer and asks permission to ask a few questions about their context, which earns him the right to run discovery without it feeling like an interrogation. The discovery questions carry the framework inside them: is there anything you are trying to do that you have been unable to do with your existing tools, methods, or team? The two frightening follow-ups are whether it is actually okay to leave the project undone for now, and whether the options they have already looked into are, in fact, good enough. Hearing yes to either saves you from spending years pushing something nobody needs. Once pull is established, the pitch stays small: restate what they are looking for in plain terms and let them pull more detail out of you. His cadence is roughly five calls, then stop and ask honestly whether any pull appeared and what was learned. The mindset throughout is selling to learn, not selling to push, and the feeling of pushing, of having to convince, is itself the red flag.

On quality bars, he rejects the idea that a product must be ten times better than the incumbent. If a customer's options cannot get the job done at all and yours can, you are not slightly better, you are infinitely better, because you unblock them. The popular reading of Linear beating Jira on craft only works, in his analysis, if some users were actually blocked by Jira's experience; he tried the better-experience play himself in HR software and failed, because the buyers there simply did not care how dated their systems looked. The stronger the pull, the faster everything moves: he has watched enterprise deals close in two weeks that would once have taken eighteen months.

Closing, when there is pull, is mostly concept, plan, and project management. One founder who heard a Hail Mary answer from a pharma executive about slow market research went away with no slides at all, mocked up a rough version of the thing within days, got feedback, and moved to price. Price itself is bounded by the framework: the value of getting the project done, or the cost of the current alternatives, such as what the customer already pays consultants each year. Early on he cares more about testing willingness to transact than willingness to pay, and when a big number is needed he floats it as a case study for the buyer to react to, noting that others have landed anywhere from a modest project fee to a million dollars, and asking how they are thinking about it. What he will not do is give the product away in a design partnership, which converts someone who needs a problem solved now into someone helping you build, a negative signal to exactly the customers you want. You may sell something service-shaped at first, delivered partly by humans while the product is built behind it, which validates the pull while you learn what to automate. For larger deals, his project management trick is to ask the champion when the project must be showing results by, then work backwards from that date, turning it into a forcing function for every step in between.

If the first batch of calls finds no pull, the loop repeats: review the calls, salvage what the Hail Marys surfaced, form a new hypothesis, and run five more calls. Getting it right the first time has happened to him once or twice. His break-glass option is what he calls goblin mode, doing unreasonable things to get in front of customers right now, like flying to a conference that starts tomorrow or, in the case of the dental staffing company Tiro, having the founder's parents go door to door to dental offices. Staying stuck, he argues, is the worst outcome, and time in the zero-to-one pain cave is the thing to minimize.

What happens after the sale surprised him most. Every customer, he says, is guaranteed to churn until they cross some threshold after which it would be weird for them to leave, and all of delivery is about finding that threshold and building a bullet train that gets every customer to it. For Slack the indicator was a volume of messages sent early on. For his own restaurant retention software, it was the customer receiving, within the first two weeks, a warning that an employee was about to quit which they then prevented; after that moment they told their friends and never left. So he advises delivering firsthand as well, and continually asking what would make this customer weird to churn. When churn is rampant instead, the pattern is diagnostic: one horizontal AI workflows product losing almost everyone discovered that freight forwarders kept using it for one specific workflow, and pivoted the entire company into a premium product for that niche.

All of this folds into his final model, the case study factory. A customer with pull who gets unblocked and stays is, structurally, a case study: they were trying to do something, their options fell short for a specific reason, and you offered the thing that got it done. So a startup, in his model, is not a pile of functions but a machine for repeating one case study indefinitely, with three stations: pipeline, which gets people talking to you; sales, which turns them into customers; and delivery, which makes them weird to churn. Founders who see the factory can borrow the theory of constraints from manufacturing and ask which single station is the bottleneck right now, then put everything into that one until it moves. The case study also becomes the sales tool, since you can show a prospect the story and ask whether it is relevant to them, and a disqualification tool when it plainly is not. His summary line: we do not make software here, we make case studies. The customers he is hunting throughout are what he calls hell yes customers, people who are weird not to buy before the sale and weird to churn after it, and the job is simply to find more people who resemble them.

The framework is not only for startups. He is helping established companies launch second products whose founders are running the same play against existing customers, selling features before building them, and he notes that at one mature company every product idea that worked originated in a sales call where a customer asked, in effect, could you also do this other thing for us, while every whiteboarded idea failed. His closing image is a metal detector on a beach. The framework does not guarantee treasure where you are standing, but it beats searching the sand with a spoon.

In the lightning round, his most recommended books are Christopher Alexander's four-volume The Nature of Order, his comfort rewatches are the most recent Pride and Prejudice and The Penguins of Madagascar, the AI product he reaches for is Muse, and his motto is that life is what you make of it. His tarpit idea is entering a space with a clear winner and no theory of how you would be different, the founder's version of assuming a modestly better product is enough. As he puts it, you could offer him a printer for his house that was a hundred times better and he would not buy it, because he is not blocked.

Open questions

  1. The framework says pull cannot be created, only found. How should a founder distinguish, in practice, between a market where no pull exists yet and one where pull exists but their outreach or hypothesis is simply pointed at the wrong person?
  2. Snyder's benchmarks for cold outreach, and the five-call check-in, come from B2B software. Do the same numbers hold for founders selling into slower, relationship-driven industries, or does the loop need different calibration there?
  3. The case study factory model assumes one repeatable case study is the goal. How does a company know when repeating the case study is leaving a larger adjacent opportunity unbuilt, and when should it deliberately start a second factory?

David Senra · Nik Storonsky · Competition and building Revolut

Competition, Intensity & Building a $115 Billion Global Bank | Nik Storonsky, Revolut

On David Senra, the episode "Competition, Intensity & Building a $115 Billion Global Bank | Nik Storonsky, Revolut" is a conversation with Nik Storonsky, the co-founder and chief executive of Revolut. Senra opens by repeating what other founders say about him: super competitive, excessively fast, a conqueror. Storonsky does not push back on any of it. He has been extremely competitive since he was six years old, he says, in sports and in academic olympiads, and he is genuinely angry when he finishes second at anything he has chosen to do.

That temperament explains the ambition. Asked about a remark that he was coming for Jamie Dimon's lunch, Storonsky corrects it: he is coming for his dinner. Dinner is the bigger meal, and by it he means not just retail banking but enterprise business and investment banking too, which he calls still manual, still expensive, and open to disruption. Revolut began as a simple card for spending abroad at interbank rates, then expanded into credit, wealth, trading, crypto and business accounts, plus services beyond finance such as booking stays and buying eSIMs. It is now live in 38 countries, most of them with banking licences, and the stated goal is to be the number one bank in at least 100 countries, adding 10 to 15 new markets every year or two.

His account of competition is unusual because he barely watches competitors. He competes against himself: how would he beat himself, outsmart himself, from the outside. The daily standard is to be at almost 100 percent every day, so that even a loss cannot be blamed on effort. His real edge, he argues, is that the people across from him are mostly not founders. Professional chief executives serve three or four years, collect bonuses, play defense and optimize for keeping their jobs. A founder thinks of himself and the company as one entity, plays offense, and keeps asking which product or industry comes next.

The clearest early lesson was about regulation. Revolut took light e-money and payment licences first because they took a year instead of three or four, and by the time it applied for a full UK banking licence it already had 7 to 8 million UK customers, something Storonsky says had never happened before. The process took almost five years, and he calls the sequencing his worst mistake: a regulator faces far more blame for licensing a giant than a company with no customers at all. He now treats a banking licence as a must for any company that wants real scale, because customers only move salaries and wealth to an institution they trust. American fintechs such as Chime and Cash App, which never became banks, are in his view permanently capped at early adopters and niche audiences. In Ireland, by contrast, four out of five adults use Revolut, and penetration sits around 20 percent in the UK and France and 30 to 40 percent in several other markets.

Legacy banks, he says, are stuck because their managers are risk averse, non-technical, and protective of what they have. They will not replace an old third-party core banking system because migration is risky, so nothing changes. Against that, Storonsky describes his company as a machine: small independent teams building on one shared platform, with the founder watching the whole system and intervening wherever a bottleneck appears. When growth slowed, he split the company into small teams; when regulation became the constraint, he built a regulatory and government affairs muscle; when hiring became the constraint, he built a recruiting process aimed at the best people.

His years as a bank trader left him with a discipline of quantification. Everything at Revolut runs on metrics and KPIs, which he says kill internal politics because the numbers, not the argument, decide whether someone achieved their goal. The same thinking drives what he calls bets. A small team, usually no more than ten people, gets funded to build a new product within 9 to 18 months. Roughly 50 bets have launched over ten years, judged on gross profit growth. The top performers, growing 10 to 20 percent month on month, get more teams and can grow into departments and major profit lines. A bet that lands in the bottom quarter of the historical cohort is shut down. Storonsky says he does not trust his own intuition about which products to build, having been wrong too many times; the rule is algorithmic. Fund the top, cut the bottom.

AI is now reshaping the machine. About 90 percent of customer support is automated, down from a team of more than 5,000 people; engineering speed is up about 30 percent; translation and cross-selling run through language models. His goal is a full AI employee with email, chat access and entry to internal back office systems, able to join calls and do work rather than answer prompts in a chat window. Because one developer can now cover front end, back end and data work, he thinks bet teams should shrink from ten people to two or three.

The organization matches the philosophy. Storonsky has about 51 direct reports and also talks directly to designers, product owners and developers deep in the structure. He wants self-guided people, his phrase is self-guided missiles, who identify the goal and reach it without management time, and he prunes average performers as well as below average ones. Executives who are liked by their teams are a warning sign, not a virtue: likability is never the goal, achieving the goal with the minimum resources is. He is equally blunt about work culture. In the co-working space where he started, 140 companies became three survivors in five years and two in ten, and the survivors worked weekends. China competes on a 996 schedule, and he doubts Europe or the United States can outcompete that on talent alone.

He argues that hardship built Revolut. Europe caps interchange far below American levels, caps credit card rates, restricts lending, requires a local presence country by country, and layers tough employment law on top. Revolut ran at negative gross margin for its first three to five years before optimizing its way to roughly 80 percent. Cheap American money, by contrast, lets founders solve problems by spending, which he thinks leaves them undeveloped as entrepreneurs and unprepared for the day the money disappears.

On investors he is just as direct. He compares venture capitalists to cheerleaders who try to play the match: they risk other people's money, almost none have built a company, and their advice was repeatedly wrong for him, including a push to hire experienced executives (of more than 50 he hired in the first five years, he kept two) and a fight with one investor who wanted him to fire his best person for being too hardcore. Round by round he removed venture investors from his board; he now has none, and advises founders to keep investors as purely financial players with no voting rights or decision seats.

His revenge was to compete with them. QuantumLight, his separate investment firm, picks companies with machine learning models rather than human judgment, investing equal amounts in 30 to 40 companies per fund. One signal is the age of the partner leading a deal: partners between 30 and 40 identify the best companies, a statistical echo of his prime-age hiring rule. The first fund, $250 million, is fully invested at a 56 percent IRR; a second, $500 million fund is under way, and the ambition he names is a BlackRock for private markets.

Marketing followed the same pattern of delayed, then total, commitment. Revolut spent nothing on marketing for its first seven years and survived a COVID collapse that cut revenue about 70 percent because the travel card business vanished. When he finally built marketing, he hired from gaming companies, the world's largest and most careful performance advertisers, and then arbitraged trust: a digital bank has no branches, so physical presence is credibility. Revolut bought advertising across roughly half the airports in Europe, where impressions are cheaper than digital ones, and opened a physical experience store in Barcelona.

The endgame is bigger than banking. Financial products, he says, are mostly invented already; Revolut's work is rebuilding them cheaper and better on a single ledger and one configurable system, where entering a new country means changing configuration rather than building a new bank. The cash flow from that machine is meant to fund deep technology: Pragma, Revolut's own foundation model trained on transaction data rather than words, learning from 30 million transactions a day, with possible uses in fraud, credit and investing, and eventually its own models and data centers. Against JPMorgan, his pitch is scope: a local bank gives you local products, while Revolut gives global accounts, instant transfers across countries, and a technology cost base the legacy banks cannot match. Over time, he says, it becomes an unstoppable machine.

Open questions

  1. Storonsky's system depends on one founder who watches the whole machine, prunes relentlessly and holds about 51 direct reports. How much of it survives him, and what breaks first if the company has to run on professional management of exactly the kind he criticizes?
  2. The bets discipline cuts any product in the bottom quarter of the historical cohort within about 18 months. Which genuinely new categories would fail that test early, and does an algorithmic kill rule sit comfortably next to his plan for moonshot bets that may not pay back for five years?
  3. His case against American fintechs is that skipping the banking licence capped their growth. As Revolut enters the United States, will US regulators hand a licence to a foreign challenger moving at his speed, and on what timeline?

MTS · Prinz · OpenAI math results and recursive self-improvement

OpenAI Math Breakthroughs Show We Are Near Full RSI | Prinz

On MTS, the episode "OpenAI Math Breakthroughs Show We Are Near Full RSI | Prinz" is a conversation between host Theo Jaffee and Prinz, an anonymous AI commentator who is a lawyer by profession and a returning guest on the show. Two threads run through it: a possible cyber crisis unfolding in real time, and the question of whether recent math results from OpenAI's internal model mean that recursive self-improvement, machines that improve themselves, is now close.

The cyber discussion comes first, and it is uneasy. Prinz says that when a major cyber event is followed within half an hour by another, and then another an hour later, it starts to feel as though some threshold has been crossed. He compares the moment to February 2020, when COVID-19 first appeared and nobody could say whether it would be a minor epidemic or something far worse. His hope is that the United States government, which he says has had access to frontier AI models since February of this year, has quietly used that time to harden the most critical systems. His expectation is less comforting: anything that is vulnerable will be attacked, by private groups and by state hackers alike. His practical advice to listeners is to secure their own accounts now, with two-factor authentication and fresh passwords.

Theo points out that COVID, on the historical scale, was moderately bad rather than catastrophic. Prinz answers from his own biography. He grew up in Eastern Europe under a communist regime that was supposed to last for decades, and then watched the whole system collapse within a couple of years. The cyber moment would be the third such discontinuity in his life, he says, and his conclusion is simply that preparing is the right move.

The main topic is RSI, and the two of them start with definitions. Theo lays out the spectrum: at one end, RSI is already happening in mild form, with researchers at the big labs using models to write experiment code and speed up their own work; at the other, it means AI fully automating AI research and producing a fast, discontinuous takeoff to superintelligence. Prinz, the lawyer, says both descriptions are true and the argument is about definitions. His own test turns on the word "self" in self-improvement. A model counts, for him, only when it can train a successor that is more capable than itself, without a bigger model supervising it, and when it can find genuinely creative ways to do so, the kind of brilliant leap associated with researchers like Ilya Sutskever. He does not believe any lab has that today, in public or, so far as he can tell, internally.

What has changed his mind about the timeline is creativity in mathematics. Until recently, he says, model successes in math mostly came from brute force. He describes using a model he calls GPT-6 Astra on old, unresolved ciphers from the First World War era: it cracked them, but only in cases where the key effectively existed and the machine could search the whole space of possibilities. It never produced the key from a novel insight of its own. The recent results from OpenAI's internal model look different to him. As he recounts it, Terence Tao described one of the solutions as a combination of three or four existing ideas arranged in a very unusual way, which is the sort of move people associate with human genius.

That, for Prinz, is the signal. Abilities of that kind tend to generalize, and if they reach AI research itself, models will start producing the creative advances that unlock much stronger models. He is careful about the limits of the claim. He notes that others in the labs have publicly cautioned that creativity in math may not transfer directly to creativity elsewhere, and he does not think OpenAI's current internal model can already improve itself. But the pattern of the last few years is that once a model can do something at all, it becomes very good at it within about twelve months, and cheaper and faster besides. His verdict is that we are on the brink.

Theo presses on a different point: would RSI iron out the jaggedness of today's models, brilliant at some tasks and oddly weak at others? Prinz thinks the first thing he would aim a self-improving system at is a successor to the transformer, the architecture the field stumbled onto and has used ever since. Whether that is low-hanging fruit or years of work, he would not be surprised if the standard architecture five years from now looked completely alien. But he agrees with Theo that some jaggedness is not about intelligence at all. Put Einstein in a laboratory and order him to cure cancer by tomorrow, and he still fails without the right data and experiments. Biology, in particular, will not be solved by computation alone.

His home turf offers a smaller example of the same gap. Asked about legal research memos, Prinz says today's models hand you a haystack with a needle in it. For a specialist who knows what to look for, the needle is there and saves hours. For a junior lawyer, the rest of the haystack is close to useless, and an answer that needs two sharp pages arrives as a thirty-five page memo. He thinks the problem is that models learn non-verifiable material less efficiently than humans do, and that a better architecture, one that learns in a more humanlike way, could close much of that gap.

The conversation ends with the economy. Theo cites a reported figure: a median expectation among Anthropic employees of roughly seventeen percent annual growth by 2030. Prinz hopes it is right, but notes that Western growth has been small for decades partly because these economies are already highly developed; squeezing a little more efficiency out of a supermarket's logistics will never add up to seventeen percent. The arithmetic changes, he argues, if models begin making major scientific discoveries at scale, in drug discovery, materials and longer, healthier lives. China after Mao grew at ten percent or more a year, largely because it started from so far behind; substitute a stream of real discoveries for the fast food restaurants of that analogy, he suggests, and very rapid growth becomes plausible. He will not commit to seventeen percent by 2030, but major growth would not surprise him.

Open questions

  1. Prinz's test for real RSI is a model autonomously training a more capable successor, complete with genuine creative leaps. From outside the labs, what evidence could ever confirm that this has happened, and how would it be distinguished from a strong model trained under heavy human direction?

Per-seat vs per-token AI pricing

Are You Buying AI Per Seat Or Per Token? That's the Wrong Question. Ask This Instead.

On AI News & Strategy Daily, Nate B. Jones argues that the argument enterprise buyers keep having about AI pricing, per seat or per token, is the wrong argument. Tokens are a way to meter compute, and seats are a traditional software measure. What companies are actually buying is intelligence, and intelligence is a new economic input alongside the two businesses have always had, people and capital.

His opening example is Cedar, a healthcare payments company whose AI agent, Cora, answers patient calls about medical bills, even at two in the morning. Cora verifies who the caller is, walks them through the charges, confirms insurance, and takes payment on the call. The hospital, Jones notes, does not care how many tokens that call consumed. It cares that the bill got paid, and that gap between the meter and the outcome is where his whole case sits.

The standard contracts, per seat or per token, quietly hand the buyer a burden: you purchased the intelligence, now capture enough value to justify the cost. Jones says most companies cannot yet do that reliably, which is why they cap and constrain AI spend to limit the downside. His verdict is blunt: they are underinvested. The missing piece is rarely the price. It is the know-how to decide which work intelligence should do, how the surrounding processes change, and how to tell when an answer is good.

That is why he keeps returning to the individual. Anyone using these tools at work is already allocating a company resource with real economic impact, whether or not they hold a management title. The shift he wants is in the asking: instead of asking an agent to finish a regular report faster, ask it to investigate the root cause of a customer problem and start fixing it. People who make that shift stop being users of AI and become, in his phrase, managers of AI.

A longer example follows an engineer at a hypothetical equipment company, closely matched to real stories he has seen. An agent pulls maintenance records and compares past cases, but the engineer knows which operating conditions matter, which records are unreliable, and when two different faults are hiding in the same language. The judgment about when to dig deeper with the AI and when to stop and talk to a human is the work, and repeated across enough cases it can become a service customers pay for: ongoing advice that prevents failures.

Where companies cannot supply that judgment themselves, Jones sees forward-deployed engineering filling the gap. He points to OpenAI's deployment company, whose engineers work with business leaders and frontline teams to choose where intelligence applies and then build and run the systems, and to Anthropic's joint venture with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman and Goldman Sachs, aimed at businesses such as mid-size manufacturers and regional healthcare systems. His caution runs both ways: kept as a permanent service, the customer never learns; pushed as a product too early, nobody learns enough.

Some partnerships go further. OpenAI took an ownership stake in Thrive Holdings and is embedding research, product and engineering teams inside its companies, starting with accounting and IT services. At that point, Jones argues, the CFO's decision changes character. It is no longer a procurement negotiation over a software discount. It is a judgment about how much value a committed partnership could create, what the firm would retain, and what it would have to give up to get there.

Outcome pricing is the lighter version of the same idea, and Sierra is his example of a vendor actively selling it. It fits closed loops best: a bill explained and paid, a support case resolved, the kind of completed interaction companies like Fin have priced per outcome in customer service. It fits badly, he concedes, when the work is a sprawling program with no single transaction to price, and outcome deals are hard to measure even when they do fit.

None of this kills per token pricing, which he expects to stay. Companies fluent enough to apply intelligence well may prefer buying usage, because it leaves room to experiment and lets them keep the gains. He cites Shopify, which has held headcount roughly flat for about two years while revenue grew 34 percent, lets engineers use the best models for each circumstance, and monitors spend while distinguishing adoption from impact. Block, he adds, has reported gross profit growth of 25 percent with a much leaner team. Fluent firms show returns; the commercial arrangement that suits a firm depends on how fluent it is.

He closes with a direct appeal on both levels. Individuals can become managers of AI before anyone grants them the title, and the scope of work they can direct grows accordingly. Leaders evaluating the next big AI purchase should put their company's ability to create value on the table next to the price, and test the investment with a revenue project they could not previously resource, attributing the return when it lands.

Open questions

  1. Outcome pricing works where a result is easy to count, but many outcomes are jointly produced by the vendor's model and the customer's own people. When an outcome disappoints, who absorbs the loss, and what measurement would both sides accept in advance?
  2. Jones says returns hinge on frontline employees feeling motivated, equipped and trusted to direct intelligence. How does a company actually assess that kind of fluency, and reward it, rather than simply measuring seats adopted or tokens consumed?
  3. Equity stakes and embedded teams blur the line between vendor and customer. What happens to those arrangements when the customer's strategy changes, or when a lab ends up holding stakes in companies that compete with each other?

Kimi K3.1 Pro and the new cyber reality

Podcast: Kimi K3.1 Pro, the new cyber reality, and Muse musings

On Interconnects, the podcast episode "Podcast: Kimi K3.1 Pro, the new cyber reality, and Muse musings" is a conversation between Nathan Lambert and Florian Brand, the evaluations researcher who recently moved to San Francisco. Recorded after a busy week in AI, it moves through three big topics: the strange reception of Moonshot's new flagship open model, a wave of security incidents that has both hosts rethinking the risks of open weights, and a lighter close on junior hiring and Brand's deepening reliance on a personal AI agent.

The headline puzzle is Kimi K3.1 Pro. On the Artificial Analysis index, the model sits near the very top, ahead of GPT-6 Astra and behind only the newest Claude models, scores Lambert calls crazy for an open release. Yet the reaction has been mixed, because the long tail of results is jagged: on Terminal-Bench Science it trails GPT-6 Astra by about twenty points, and ProgramBench tells a similar story.

Brand's explanation is about time horizons. The benchmarks Artificial Analysis leans on are comparatively short and reward a model that exploits a well-specified goal, while ProgramBench asks a model to explore for a long time before it even knows what it is doing. Kimi's reinforcement learning, he argues, is still tilted toward the exploitative side, and for a model with three trillion parameters, robustness at exploration is exactly what you are paying for.

The size cuts the other way too. Fitting a three trillion parameter model takes B300 accelerators, one node per replica, where smaller rivals run on hardware people already own. The launch also felt different from Kimi's earlier drops: more professional, with day-one support from inference frameworks, a period of exclusive access before the weights land by November 4th, and a social campaign that drew criticism after leaked press instructions reportedly told posters not to disclose a paid promotion. Neither host treats that allegation as confirmed, and both note that plenty of American launches are astroturfed too.

The most praised part of the release is Kimi's stated open-weight principles. The company says it deliberately held the model's offensive cyber capability below what its own pipeline can produce, in part by down-weighting cyber material in mid-training, while still releasing a model that is strong for defense. Brand calls that style of release really good: broad access helps everyone find and patch bugs, while genuinely dangerous exploitation needs the long-horizon persistence where closed frontier models remain far ahead.

That leads into the week's security mood, which the hosts describe as a "you can hack everything" era. Lambert's central observation is that cybersecurity experts and AI experts talk past each other. The cyber community works from an ecosystem view, where vulnerability discovery and time to exploitation were already on exponential trends before ChatGPT and show no clear step change since, while AI commentators mostly argue about open versus closed models and headline releases like Mythos.

Lambert also revisits the Mythos moment and his "boy who cried wolf" exchange with Dean Ball, arguing that years of dire forecasts from AI safety voices have dulled their credibility even as the underlying vulnerability flood is real. The key unknown is translation. Vulnerabilities are being found en masse and visible hacks have ticked up, yet reported cyber damages attributed to AI have historically been a very small share, and ransomware remains the metric the cyber community watches. He cites Joshua Saxe's argument that the attacker supply chain is weak and self-selected, while granting that nation states are surely using AI for quiet infiltration at scale.

The concrete cases are stark. A remote code execution bug sat in PHP, the language under WordPress and much of the web, for years, until OpenAI's Patch the Planet program and other researchers found it, coordinated with the PHP Foundation, and shipped a fix that briefly took sites offline so they could patch. The feared mass exploitation has not materialized, and there are even unverified reports of a so-called DeepSeek swarm patching sites at scale. LiteLLM, by contrast, was compromised again this week, apparently because an API key sat readable in a GitHub repository for five months before someone used it to spread malware.

Lambert's synthesis is uncomfortable. Offense is getting cheaper, but offense was never the main bottleneck, so attacker output has not multiplied. Defense is the worry: coding agents are producing exponentially more code, flaws arise from interactions between stacked systems that a model cannot fully foresee, and the result is a backlog that likely means years of elevated vulnerability. He points to Azeem Azhar's framing of this moment as day zero of a new internet, one that may never be as secure and open again.

Both hosts are scathing about companies such as Abliteration.ai and Morph that serve safeguard-stripped, abliterated models over APIs, marketed partly for defense work. Lambert calls it a bad judgment call that invites government lawsuits, and notes the contradiction of sending your sensitive code to someone else's API in order to keep it secure. The real story, they argue, is access: defenders reached for open models partly because the labs' defensive cyber programs were hard to get into. OpenAI's defensive program is now comparatively easy to join, with an ID and hardware keys, and Anthropic is broadening access as well, which they see as the change that actually works through the backlog of insecure code.

Closed-model safeguards remain a gray area. The labs have pushed updates that block attempts to extract the PHP exploit, and some researchers complain they cannot get agents to do legitimate cyber work at all since Opus 4.8. Yet headlines about closed models being used to hack companies keep appearing, and Lambert thinks the community has not fully updated on how porous the closed side has been.

The lighter segment starts with Anthropic's reported decision not to hire junior employees. Brand calls it a gift to everyone else, recalling that a takeaway from visiting China was how fast you catch up when you hire cohorts of hungry young PhDs and hand them a cluster. Lambert is harsher: for a company that talks constantly about preparing society for powerful AI, refusing to train the next generation is another red flag about its safety culture, and one that will look worse if an AI-driven jobs correction arrives. Brand adds the empirical caveat that no mass displacement has shown up yet, since coding agents so far seem to make teams hire more, not fewer.

Looking ahead a few weeks, they expect the current trends to compound: more vulnerability disclosures, more patching at scale, a run of open model releases from American labs, new releases from Chinese players, and possibly an IPO in the mix.

The close is given to Muse, Meta's personal agent, which Brand has adopted heavily since moving to the United States, partly because it simply was not available to him in the EU. He describes it messaging him proactively about the paperwork of an international move, telling him which forms he needs for which appointments and where to download them, and running his household shopping: he hands it a list and his card, it builds carts across unfamiliar American stores, and checkout runs through a Stripe link that generates a one-time card, so a mistake never touches his real one. He is, by his own account, somewhat annoyed at how good it is.

The frictions are real too. It is slow, its browser sometimes crashes and costs twenty minutes of rebuilding, and some websites block its data centers, though CAPTCHAs do not slow it down. Brand's trust rests on incentives: he argues that Meta's legal and reputational exposure, if anything leaked, is exactly what makes the product careful. He imported his ChatGPT memories through an export prompt, worked around the missing iCloud mail integration by forwarding to Gmail, and draws one firm line: whenever a task would touch a real person, he takes over and writes himself, out of mutual respect, while forms and support chatbots are fair game for the agent.

Lambert is more hesitant. He likes grocery shopping, recently got married, and jokes that his first real delegation may be sending Muse to wrestle customer service for a domain stuck at his wedding website provider. He also pays for a service that flags AI-written email, and wonders how agent-to-agent contact gets started at all, perhaps with a paid front door where another agent pays a small fee to reach your inbox. Brand counters that, as a newcomer who knows nothing about anything in a new country, the help has saved him enormous time, and every cross-check he ran came back spot on. They close where they started, agreeing the cybersecurity questions are set for a while, and Lambert signs off hoping nobody gets hacked, with a parting reminder, whatever happens, not to blame DeepSeek.

Open questions

  1. Vulnerabilities are being found en masse while measured cyber damages stay comparatively flat. Which signal should govern the open-weights debate over the next year, disclosure volume or realized damage, and what evidence would actually settle it?
  2. Kimi says it capped its model's offensive cyber capability during mid-training while keeping it strong for defense. How could outsiders verify that separation, and how much of it survives once the weights are public and anyone can fine-tune?
  3. Brand's willingness to hand a personal agent his card, calendar, and mail rests largely on the provider's legal and reputational exposure. Is that a durable basis for trust as these agents gain more autonomy, and where should the line sit between agent-handled chores and contact with real people?
