MTS · Eugenia Kuyda · Wabi and proactive agents

AI Agents Will Proactively Build Apps for Your Life | Eugenia Kuyda

On MTS, the episode "AI Agents Will Proactively Build Apps for Your Life | Eugenia Kuyda" is a conversation with Eugenia Kuyda, the founder and chief executive of Wabi, recorded on the day Wabi 2.0 went live for everyone. The app is now on the App Store, the Play Store, and desktop, with no invite list. It looks like a messenger, because that is the point: you talk with other people and with an agent in the same place, and the agent can build small personal apps for you.

The change from the first version is who does the building. In Wabi 1.0, users built their own personal apps. The lesson the team took from that, Kuyda says, is that people love personal apps but do not necessarily want to build them. So in 2.0 the agent builds everything, and does it proactively. You can still ask for something specific, but you do not have to ask at all.

Her case for a messenger starts with how much of life is not single player. People manage kids, family, and shared plans together, and bolting an agent onto a group chat after the fact is tedious. A single scrolling text thread is also a poor way to follow an agent's work, so Wabi's agent can open separate threads for separate topics and build a visual app when a picture beats a paragraph.

She gives her own health thread as the example. The agent built her a weight tracker, a weightlifting log, a supplement tracker, and a calorie tracker, and she logs into them by text or voice through the day. When she logged a workout but then slept badly, the agent reached out in the morning on its own: her sleep score was low, so here was a simple twenty minute workout instead.

Kuyda is blunt about the state of personal agents generally. She calls the current moment the DOS era of AI agents: one text thread, aimed mostly at productivity. Her argument is that agentic capability belongs in the rest of life too, helping someone be a better partner, stay on top of friendships, feel inspired, and get closer to the goals they set for the year, rather than only chasing refunds and filing claims.

Proactivity is the design principle she keeps returning to. Early users, she says, call Wabi the most proactive agent they have tried. Instead of greeting the user with an empty text box, Wabi reads what the user connects and gets to work. If it sees a trip in your email, it opens a thread about the trip and builds the apps you might want there, such as a map of interesting spots and restaurants near where you are staying, or a list of events happening nearby.

Asked what data a personal agent should have, her answer is: as much as you can give it. Connect Gmail and Wabi immediately looks for money owed to you, and some users, she says, have gotten a few hundred dollars back that way. Connect bank accounts through Plaid and it can find subscriptions worth canceling and infer still more context. The more you connect, the more it can do without being asked.

Her prediction for the year ahead is about moats. Today's AI products are mostly single player, she argues, with little to lock anyone in: moving between Claude and ChatGPT is easy, and with a personal agent the context sits in your own Gmail anyway. Within a year, she expects one of the biggest consumer AI platforms to be multiplayer, with real network effects, built around people talking to each other and not only to an AI. The single thread with just you and an agent, she predicts, will look archaic in hindsight.

Open questions

  1. How much unprompted outreach will people welcome before an agent that reads their email and bank accounts starts to feel intrusive rather than helpful?
  2. Will multiplayer network effects really give a consumer agent the moat Kuyda predicts, or will users keep treating agents as interchangeable because the context lives in accounts they control?
  3. When the agent builds the apps on its own, who checks them: how does a user verify that a proactively built app is handling their money, health, or travel plans correctly?

MTS · Jonathan Webb · Nuclear build costs and AI

We Can Cut Nuclear Build Costs With AI | Jonathan Webb

On MTS, the episode "We Can Cut Nuclear Build Costs With AI | Jonathan Webb" has host Sophia Dew interviewing Jonathan Webb, the chief executive of The Nuclear Company. Webb calls in from a field in Lewis County, Kentucky, the site of Prosperity 1, a proposed 6.6 gigawatt nuclear campus his company announced that morning.

The site is roughly 4,000 acres on the Ohio River, with the Appalachian foothills behind it. A 765 kilovolt transmission line, the largest in the country, runs past the property and connects it to PJM, the grid operator for the Northeast. The river carries far more cooling water than the reactors would need. The Nuclear Company is a little over three years old and started in Kentucky, a coal state where plant closures have left the region short of power. Webb says the team reviewed about a dozen states and some 1,500 properties before choosing this one.

The campus would use Westinghouse AP1000 reactors, the same design built at Vogtle in Georgia. Webb's argument starts from that project's autopsy. Vogtle's design was not finished when construction scaled up, and roughly a thousand design changes landed mid-build. About 10,000 people worked on the asset over 12 years, and by his estimate they worked at only 15 to 30 percent labor efficiency. The finished Georgia units, he notes, now run at about a 93 percent capacity factor, among the best anywhere. The machine is proven, he argues. The build process is what failed.

His fix is the episode's title claim. Working with Palantir, the company has built what Webb calls a nuclear ontology for deployment: millions of data points drawn from logistics and the supply chain, organized so the information reaches the person he calls the real client, the frontline worker, in real time. If labor efficiency rose even to 60 percent, which he concedes is still low, the schedule would shrink dramatically, and cost would fall with it.

The timeline he sketches is aggressive. The AP1000 design is now finished, federal leadership from the president to Energy Secretary Chris Wright has made nuclear a priority, and the company plans to work through Nuclear Regulatory Commission permitting on a greenfield path. Webb aims to have non-nuclear construction under way in 2027 and the full build in 2028.

The demand is already gathering nearby. A large data center campus has been announced 10 miles west of the site and another 20 miles east, and the Department of Energy campus at Piketon, Ohio sits about 50 miles north. Nucor is building a large steel campus in West Virginia. Coal retirements are taking baseload off the regional grid just as these loads arrive, and Webb says off-take conversations with buyers are under way.

Webb also makes a regional bet. The workforce he is counting on comes from coal country and the Rust Belt, the men and women he credits with powering the Industrial Revolution and two world wars. The site adds rail and barge access to the water and transmission. Beyond the plant, architect Bjarke Ingels is working with the company on housing and place-making aimed at what Webb calls the next great American river cities, and actor Boyd Holbrook, whose father was a coal miner in the region, flew in for the announcement day's community work. The local sentiment, Webb says, is enthusiastic.

He closes on the stakes. The one competitor, in his telling, is China, and China is winning. The United States will build micro, small, and large reactors, and this site is meant to prove it can still build big things on cost and on schedule without compromising quality or safety. Crews begin confirmatory analysis on the site within days, with laydown yards and rail and barge logistics to follow.

Open questions

  1. Vogtle's overruns were measured after the fact; can an ontology feeding real-time information to frontline workers actually hold a first-of-a-kind greenfield build to a 2027 and 2028 construction timeline once permitting and construction meet reality?
  2. Prosperity 1's economics lean on data center, steel, and federal loads that are announced nearby but whose off-take agreements are still being negotiated; what happens to the campus schedule if those buyers delay or sign elsewhere?
  3. Webb frames the contest as the United States against China across micro, small, and large reactors; does a bet on a fleet of large AP1000s pay off if smaller designs reach the grid sooner?

Reducto, a document intelligence startup

24hrs Inside a YC Startup That Raised $108M to Solve a Major Enterprise AI Bottleneck

On the Will Phillips channel, the video "24hrs Inside a YC Startup That Raised $108M to Solve a Major Enterprise AI Bottleneck" spends a Friday inside Reducto, a San Francisco startup that turns complex business documents into data AI systems can use. Phillips travels in with co-founder Adit, sits in on sales and engineering meetings, and watches the company's weekly all-hands.

The premise is that enterprise AI is only as reliable as the data it can reach, and much of that data still sits in paperwork that models read badly. Reducto's answer is a parsing layer built around its R1 model, which preserves a document's structure, including tables that split across pages, instead of flattening it into text. The company says it now processes more than a billion pages a month, that it passed that mark for the first time in September at roughly six times the prior year's volume, and that it has raised 108 million dollars in total, including a 75 million dollar Series B led by a16z.

Adit argues the parsing layer complements frontier models rather than competing with them. In one internal test, he says, a weaker model given Reducto's output was more accurate than a stronger model reading the document itself, while using fewer reasoning tokens, because it could spend its effort on the task instead of correcting its own reading of the page. Predictable pricing is part of the pitch too: Reducto charges by the page, while a customer paying a model provider by the token cannot easily forecast what a complicated invoice will cost to process.

The demand side is unglamorous. Companies in healthcare and finance, Adit says, are limited by how many people they can put on paperwork: staff read documents, enter data, and label it, sometimes at accuracy below fifty percent, with extra layers of people checking the work. Some customers cannot take on more patients or claims simply because the paper does not move fast enough. Against rivals such as Microsoft's Azure document tools, the sales team leans on extraction accuracy plus the tooling built around the pipeline.

The engineering conversation is about what comes after parsing. Verification agents that check extraction results lifted one customer from about ninety six percent accuracy to ninety nine point five. The stack itself has been rebuilt as models improved: Reducto began with dozens of specialized models working together, and R1 is its first single model that parses a document end to end. The team is now adding products around that core, including extraction and agentic document workflows, and says the hardest problem is evaluation: turning the tribal knowledge inside a customer's process into tests that show whether an automated workflow is actually good.

The company is scaling quickly. The team has grown from about twenty people a year ago to ninety, Adit still interviews every hire, and he expects roughly one hundred twenty by the end of the year. His closing advice to founders is to stop avoiding the things they are bad at, since a skill gap can be coached, and to treat a co-founder relationship as something maintained deliberately, because building a company is a long journey rather than a one year project.

Open questions

  1. As frontier models get better at reading documents natively, how long can a dedicated parsing layer stay far enough ahead on accuracy and cost to remain the default choice?
  2. Will page based pricing hold up as an advantage, or will model providers simplify their own pricing and bundle document reading until the difference stops mattering?
  3. Can Reducto turn evaluation, the tribal knowledge problem it describes, into a durable product edge, or will customers build that layer themselves once the tooling matures?

YC Paper Club, an inference night

Why Inference Matters 12x More Than Training | YC Paper Club

On Y Combinator, the YC Paper Club episode "Why Inference Matters 12x More Than Training" is a recording of an inference night: four talks on what it actually takes to serve language models well, framed by a host who argues that inference is where the money and the difficulty now live. The talks cover the full path of a request, from the API call down to the kernel on the GPU, and they keep returning to one point: the same model can be cheap or ruinous, fast or sluggish, faithful or quietly degraded, depending entirely on how it is served.

The host opens with the arithmetic. DeepSeek is the one lab that publishes its numbers: training DeepSeek V3 took about 2.8 million H800 GPU hours, while serving it takes roughly 43,000 H800 hours every day. That is about six times more compute spent on inference than on training for each year a set of weights is live, and about twelve times more over a two year life. His conclusion is that papers and model comparisons should be organized around equal inference cost rather than equal training cost, because that is where most of the compute, and most of the cost of goods sold, actually sits. Done well, inference has produced some 220 billion dollars of market cap in a decade, and DeepSeek's reported economics imply margins near 80 percent, which he credits to inference innovation rather than to the model itself.

He also explains why serving is hard. A request first runs prefill, processing the whole prompt at high utilization, and then decode, generating one token at a time in a memory bound loop that may use only about 5 percent of the GPU's rated compute. The numbers he cites from DeepSeek are striking: 60,000 tokens per second per GPU on prefill before cache hits, a 96 percent input cache hit rate, and 1.5 million tokens per second once cached prefixes are counted, against only 14,000 tokens per second on decode. Decode is the bottleneck. He closes by sorting inference innovation into three buckets: model design, such as mixture of experts, state space layers, latent attention, quantization, and distillation; kernels and runtimes, such as FlashAttention, RadixAttention, and PagedAttention; and the harness around the model, meaning routing, speculative decoding, and context compaction.

Dan Fu, who runs research at Together AI, then walks through the life cycle of a single inference request. A request is routed to a replica, and routing is an art: a naive load balancer spreads requests evenly, but sending a returning conversation to the GPU that already holds its KV cache is far cheaper. The request is tokenized, which is less simple than it sounds because tool calls ride along with it and have to stay correct. Then it enters the scheduler's batching loop, runs prefill and decode, often on different GPUs, and streams tokens back. Open models are the growth story here, he says: Together was serving about 400 trillion tokens a month, roughly an eighth of the traffic Google reported for Gemini, at prices up to ten times cheaper than closed models of similar quality.

Fu describes three of the levers his team pulls. The first is kernels, the GPU programs where the math runs, and Together is now using agents to optimize them, driven by real production traffic in what he calls the beginnings of a recursive self improvement loop for inference. The second is parallelism: very large models have to be split across GPUs, and the new Blackwell racks that wire 72 GPUs together at high bandwidth let each expert of a mixture of experts model live on its own GPU, which raises arithmetic intensity and throughput, at the price of making reliability engineering much harder, since one failed GPU can threaten a whole replica. The third is quantization. Models are trained at 16 bits per weight and served at 8 or 4, and the savings are real, but so is the risk of quietly damaging quality. Together's answer is a measurement regime: public benchmarks, layer by layer divergence checks, hundreds of accumulated test cases, agentic evaluations, and a loop in which models continuously write a Flappy Bird clone, on the theory that if quantization broke something, the bird stops flapping. He tells one success story: working with MiniMax, Together's training free quantization recipe brought an NVFP4 version of the M3 model back up to the quality of the original FP8 version after MiniMax's own attempt had fallen short, and the cheaper serving is what lets a lab afford to train its next model.

Jeff Morgan, co-founder and chief executive of Ollama, argues the point captured in his segment's title: not all open model tokens are the same. With hundreds or thousands of inference providers coming, the same weights are served at wildly different quality. The gap between the best and worst provider of one open model, he says, is larger than the gap between open and closed models. Providers differ in precision, in which version of the weights they run, in how they parse tool calls, in sampling, in KV cache precision, and occasionally in whether the model behind a name is the model at all. The problem compounds in agent workloads: a coding task of fifteen steps, with a ten percent chance of deviation at each step, can end up wrong nearly 80 percent of the time.

Morgan surveys how a builder can police this. Running full evaluations against every provider works but is too expensive to do continuously. Model makers can ship verifiers, as Moonshot did with a tool calling scoreboard for its models. Cheaper statistical tests compare a provider's output distribution against a reference model for about a dollar a test, and a single token log probability check can flag when a provider has silently changed its stack. Routers like Ollama's own cloud also watch the shape of traffic, counting tool call failures and output lengths, without reading the content. His longer term hopes are that labs ship their own serving stacks, as DeepSeek just did, that labs publish official quantized weights rather than leaving quantization to third parties, and that hardware attestation might one day let a GPU prove which weights it is running.

Joe Fioti, co-founder and chief executive of Luminal, describes a compiler built on equality saturation, a technique he calls the largest industrial application of its kind. Luminal reduces every model to a small set of about fourteen primitive operations, simple adds, multiplies, and reductions, a set he describes as PyTorch complete without being Turing complete. Instead of hand written heuristics that decide how to lower a program onto a chip, the compiler writes down small rules about which expressions are equivalent, grows an e-graph of every equivalent form it can find, extracts candidate programs, and times them on real hardware. The best cost model, he says, is no cost model: you run the candidates. A multi level intermediate representation lets the same core target Nvidia and AMD GPUs, TPUs, Trainium, and other accelerators, and lets the search assemble mega kernels where they help. The economics are inverted on purpose: compiling can take hours, but a compiled model serves for months, so Luminal happily trades compile time for runtime. The company runs on the order of 80 to 90 trillion tokens a month with eight people, including protein folding and image models alongside language models, and Fioti is careful on one point: numerical accuracy is modeled explicitly, because a compiler that reorders a sum carelessly becomes one more way to degrade the tokens a provider serves.

Lianmin Zheng, a core developer of SGLang and second author of its paper, closes with the memory problem that agents create. SGLang began in late 2023 with RadixAttention, which stores the KV cache in a radix tree so that many agents and sub-agents sharing a long system prompt or context can reuse the shared prefix instead of recomputing it, with cache aware scheduling and eviction built around the tree. Since then the engine has added overlap scheduling, which prepares the next batch while the GPU is still busy so the processor stops leaving bubbles, worth up to about twice the throughput on Blackwell systems; and a hierarchical cache that spills prefixes from GPU memory to host memory to storage, which cut time to first token by more than half in their experiments. His current subject is the mess new architectures make: sliding window attention and recurrent state layers each want their own memory pool with their own sharing rules, and hybrid models mix all of them. SGLang's answer is a unified radix tree keyed on whole tokens that coordinates every pool, plus unified memory pools that allocate from one physical space and compact it as workloads change, so a 4K context user and a million token user can be served by the same engine without fixed partitions. He ends by noting that SGLang now also sits inside reinforcement learning training loops, generating the rollouts that training consumes.

Open questions

  1. If inference really deserves twelve times the attention training gets, will model research actually reorganize around equal inference cost comparisons, and what changes in which models get built if it does?
  2. As open models are served by hundreds of providers of measurably different quality, who ends up certifying that the tokens a customer receives are the tokens the model would have produced: the labs, the routers, or someone new?
  3. Agent workloads multiply small serving errors across long chains of calls, so will continuous quality monitoring keep up with the providers' race to the cheapest token, or does silent quality drift become the normal cost of open models?

Parallel, the search layer for AI agents

AI Agents Could Drive 10,000x More Web Activity | Former Twitter CEO

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, the episode "AI Agents Could Drive 10,000x More Web Activity | Former Twitter CEO" is a conversation with Parag Agrawal, who spent eleven years at Twitter as an engineer, then chief technology officer, then chief executive. He now runs Parallel, a company building the search and web access layer that AI agents use. Parallel closed a 100 million dollar Series B round last month at a two billion dollar valuation, and the interview was recorded at the San Francisco office of Index Ventures, one of its investors. It opens with news: Parallel now powers search inside Browserbase, the browser platform agents use to act on the web.

Agrawal's founding claim, made nearly three years ago when working agents barely existed, was that agents would use the web a thousand times more than humans ever have. He now says he was wrong in the other direction: the thousandfold figure is too low. A person makes on the order of ten web searches a day. His own agents, which he tracks, make several thousand a day, putting him personally near that thousandfold mark already. Yet he estimates agent web usage overall is still only ten to twenty percent of human usage, which by his math leaves five thousand to ten thousand times more growth ahead. We are, he says, very early.

Doing search at that scale cannot mean rebuilding Google's infrastructure thousands of times over. Parallel's answer is efficiency: distill ranking intelligence into smaller and smaller models, and run search indexes on GPUs rather than the CPU-bound designs search has historically used. His rule of thumb is that for every ten GPUs the world spends on model inference for agents, it will spend one to two on web search, and Parallel rents GPU capacity to grow alongside inference. The scramble is real. He describes repeated fire drills in the last three months, scouting availability from every hyperscaler and neocloud, sometimes needing GPUs within five milliseconds of where the web index sits, with availability often first floated over iMessage before the finance teams do the paperwork. So far, he says, they have never had to turn a customer away.

Growth, for Parallel, is benchmarked against inference itself: the goal is to grow alongside it or faster, which today means thinking in terms of roughly tenfold growth a year. The company began with demanding early customers such as Harvey and Notion, whose agents do work with a high quality bar. The frontier has since moved to voice agents, where a natural conversation cannot wait on a slow lookup. A human web search took six or seven hundred milliseconds end to end; a voice agent needs the answer in one hundred fifty to two hundred. Quality, cost, and latency all have to be crushed at once, because expectations ratchet: he suggests turning off web search in a chatbot today and watching ninety percent of questions disappoint, like using the product as a caveman.

The conversation ranges over the new consumer agents, including Muse and Instinct, and the earlier OpenClaw wave. Agrawal thinks OpenClaw was right in most ways but asked too much setup and upkeep of ordinary users. The new products onboard naturally, he argues, because the interface is simply texting something that can do anything. Retention then becomes one question: can the product deliver on that simple promise often enough to become part of a life? He expects it can, and he expects the next phase to be proactive agents that know their users and act before being asked, a phase he admits today's always-on products only hint at.

Asked what the base layer of this new economy looks like, he lists the ingredients of an agent: a model for inference, perhaps a sandbox, web search of the kind Parallel provides, an action space built from a browser and API hooks, and connectors into personal and company data, with observability, feedback loops, and security around all of it. A cloud for agents, in other words. Then comes the harder problem. If agents become the primary consumers of the web, the web gets re-architected, and its business models break: subscriptions and affiliate models relied on humans wandering pages, and advertising relied on monetizing human attention. When an agent does the wandering, only the buying action still monetizes. Everything else needs a new answer.

Parallel's answer is to pay for reading. The company has built a system that runs a model to compute how much each content owner should be paid every time an agent reads their content, with differential pricing that rewards high quality. Agrawal compares the ambition to AdSense: if content owners earn from agent traffic, they have a reason to keep the web open instead of blocking agents outright. He extends the same alignment problem to identity and action. Good agents should declare themselves, he thinks, and ideally inherit the access their owners hold. Today, declaring often gets an agent blocked, precisely because the business model alignment is not done yet, so the products carry rough edges. On security, he expects agents to arm attackers and defenders alike, a consequential cyber event every few weeks, and defense built layer by layer, because web content flowing into an agent's context is an injection surface. His Twitter years taught him the same lesson about abuse: you never take it to zero, you contain it, and the real danger arrives when you can no longer tell a human from an agent.

The idea he says he most wants to be asked about is the proactive web: a web that moves from pull to push. Instead of people searching, agents that understand a user's world would maintain high precision alerts over everything changing on the entire web and act when something matters. The obstacle is allocation. The web is enormous, most of it is junk, it changes faster every day as agents themselves publish more content, and there is never enough compute to process all of it. Prioritizing what deserves compute is the problem he is currently obsessed with. Meanwhile the company is scaling into the demand: about one hundred twenty people now, up from near thirty at the start of the year, with a sales team built in recent months because, as he puts it, almost every business that buys inference ends up needing to buy web search. Customers arrive when a bundled search proves not good enough, when they want the freedom to switch among models, or when scale makes token efficiency worth paying for, since a higher signal search means fewer searches and fewer tokens end to end.

The closing stretch is personal. Asked who sets his standard, he names his wife, a physician and investor, whose ambition pushed his own higher, and he traces his drive to a peer group of high expectations from childhood onward. Asked how he does not break under pressure, he answers, "What pressure?" Pressure, he argues, is largely a made up thing tied to meeting other people's expectations. What he cultivates instead is insecurity: he wants jobs he would not hire himself for, because feeling slightly out of his depth keeps him humble, curious, and working hard. His own expectations, he says, have always been higher than the world's.

Open questions

The forward-deployed engineer role

What Is a Forward-Deployed Engineer?

On McKinsey & Company, the video "What Is a Forward-Deployed Engineer?" gathers several of the firm's engineers and consultants to define a job title that clients keep asking about. Their shared answer starts with the engineering. A forward-deployed engineer, or FDE, does data science, data engineering, and software engineering. The forward-deployed part is the placement: instead of working in a lab, the engineer is embedded at the client, working on the client's real systems and real problems.

What sets the role apart, they argue, is the goal. A standard engineer makes technology run. An FDE is responsible for the outcome: the technology has to reach the right people, change how they work, and produce a measurable business result. It is not enough to fit software into a company's existing process. The stronger version of the job is to rethink the process itself, working hand in hand with the client to create a new way of working.

The sharpest contrast the speakers draw is with consulting. A traditional consultant is rewarded for a recommendation and leaves behind a plan on paper. An FDE leaves behind working software. The role also differs from ordinary software development, because a developer usually builds a product that has already been defined, while an FDE first has to figure out, with the client, what the problem actually is and what should be built. Several speakers add that the two roles are merging anyway: a consultant who delivers insights and walks away no longer gets far, and a really good consultant, one says, makes a really good forward-deployed engineer.

One veteran of twenty years concedes that the FDE label itself is maybe a bit of a buzzword, then argues the concept matters more than ever. Modern language models are not sufficient on their own, he says; they have to be tailored to the problem at hand. Otherwise a company implements AI for the sake of AI: token spending climbs and the bottom line never moves. Demand is apparently not a problem. Another speaker says demand for the firm's QuantumBlack engineers is the highest it has ever been, with the longest waiting list he has seen.

The speakers tell a consistent story about why. Three years ago, companies installed enterprise-wide AI platforms and then discovered that nobody used them and little value showed up. The current lesson is that value comes from rewiring one full domain end to end and reimagining the process around the tool, because that is what drives adoption. AI also does not arrive packaged. A working deployment needs a model, a data layer with the right data, a harness that keeps the system behaving as expected, and people willing to work differently.

AI is changing the job itself in two ways. First, the coding is increasingly done with agentic tools, so the engineer thinks harder about specifications and what to ask the AI to do. Second, the product being built is now itself an AI system. One speaker describes the resulting ladder shift inside teams: analysts who did analysis two years ago now work almost like managers of AI, and the managers above them now work almost like directors, overseeing people who each run their own small operations.

The working method starts with immersion. In the first week, an FDE typically sits next to the people doing a task, say a supply chain planning team deciding how to reroute ships and trucks, and learns who makes which decisions, which data actually matters, and what is written down nowhere. Then comes problem definition, finding and extracting the data from the client's core systems, and building the application layer in pieces, demoing each piece to the client and iterating. Judgment matters throughout: the right method might be generative AI, an agentic system, machine learning, or a simple regression, and the FDE is expected to pick what is fit for purpose rather than what is fashionable.

Two client stories carry the argument. An industrials client facing a demand ramp of more than six times anything it had delivered before needed visibility into its specialized supply chain: the right parts, at the right time, with actions sequenced and owned. The team was challenged to build the capability and had a working tool in about a day and a half, work that, the speaker says, would once have taken hundreds of people years. At a utility, the team rebuilt a call center around agentic AI so that agents always had the proper answer without hallucination, ending with a real phone number customers could call, and feedback from customers and staff that was much better than before.

The hardest part, the speakers agree, is rarely the technology. It is the people. One describes a client whose most experienced software developer went through training in agentic coding, tried it, hit a failure, and went back to fixing code by hand in his editor. The risk is that he never tries the agent again, and the speakers note that the most expert people can be the hardest to move. The counter-story is a client who openly distrusted agentic engineering. After ninety minutes of being taught the fundamentals and applying them to a problem he had to solve within a day, he came back the next week saying he had used all his tokens. A week later he was the client's public example of AI adoption, asking the team to train his entire staff.

So who is good at this? The profile the speakers converge on is part engineer, part teacher. One calls it the extrovert nerd: someone who likes the machine room and likes explaining the work to people, collecting feedback, and going back to build again. Another reaches for the multi-sport athlete and the bilingual speaker as metaphors: technical enough to build, but also able to read how decisions really get made, ask the right questions, and empathize. Content knowledge and people skills both count, but the quality named above all is learning speed, plus the creativity to think out of the box about something that has never been created before, not least because part of the job is inspiring the client.

Open questions

  1. If the FDE label is partly a buzzword and the concept is decades old, what actually distinguishes a forward-deployed engineer from a strong consultant on a given engagement, and who decides?
  2. The video's evidence for impact is anecdotal, a tool in a day and a half, a converted skeptic, a happier call center. How should a client verify, before scaling, that an FDE engagement produced the outcome rather than a good demo?
  3. If agentic tools keep absorbing the coding itself, does the FDE role grow toward specification, change management, and teaching, and what happens to the engineering depth the speakers say the job still requires?

Black October and AI key theft

Black October Isn't Just A PHP Bug. Attackers Want Your AI Keys.

On AI News & Strategy Daily, Nate B. Jones argues that the wildest week in cybersecurity he can remember adds up to a turning point he calls Black October. His core claim is that AI tokens now behave like cash. Anyone who has pasted an AI key into an app is exposed: a stolen key lets an attacker run models on the victim's account, or sell that access on, while the victim gets the bill. The defense, he says, is not to hold back. It is to put the strongest available intelligence on the defending side.

The week's centerpiece is PHP, the software underneath a large share of the web, including much of WordPress. On October 21, PHP released fixes for two critical vulnerabilities. Under Apache's mod_php setup, an attacker could get code running ahead of a website's own application code, effectively hijacking the platform before the site's defenses start. Security agencies in the UK and Europe responded by telling organizations to isolate or take down affected systems they could not patch quickly, a step Jones describes as close to a nuclear option: governments telling businesses to interrupt themselves because the layer underneath is unsafe.

A second incident shows how fragile the internet's trust chain is. The certificate issuer eMudhra, whose certificates help browsers decide whether a site is genuine, had eight private keys that were trivially weak. Security researcher Scott Helme recovered them in less than a second, and one supposedly secret key, which should run to hundreds of digits, was the number four. Because eMudhra sat in the chain of automatically trusted root issuers, one company's mistake could cascade across the internet. Jones notes that Helme used Claude to recover the keys with a single prompt. Mozilla, Cloudflare, and Vercel moved within hours to shield the sites behind their platforms, acting as a front line for site owners who had not yet patched.

The offensive side is industrializing too. Microsoft has documented a China-aligned group using an AI agent framework to target hundreds of organizations, mostly failing, but in some cases reaching privileged cloud access within a couple of hours. One vector is the proxy attack, where credentials customers previously trusted are turned against other surfaces. Jones's explanation for the surge is structural. An agent can read unfamiliar code, notice something suspicious, write a test, run it, find its first explanation wrong, and keep going, and it never gets tired. Offense and defense are that same loop, which is why he warns that safeguards designed to restrain offensive models are tripping up defenders during emergencies, and why he urges companies to secure access to the strongest defensive models now rather than save money on weaker ones.

Two forces make the old timetable impossible. First, attacks have become cheap: a target company should assume it faces not a large state operation but one person running a fleet of agents. Second, maintenance lags. PHP's maintainers had seen signs of one flaw back in 2024, but the fix carried compatibility risks, demanded scarce expert time, and stalled in trade-off discussions. AI removes the time those two-year deliberations used to assume. The scale of what is surfacing is large: Jones cites Anthropic reporting more than 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities from six months of scanning important software projects, with about 6,000 reviewed so far, and raises the possibility of millions of undiscovered weaknesses across everyday software.

The key theft risk is concrete, not theoretical. LiteLLM, a popular tool that connects applications to AI models, disclosed in October that a release contained credential-stealing malware, after an earlier compromise in March. Jones walks through an illustrative investigation: a gateway built on LiteLLM, a source repository that reads clean, a gateway someone has already upgraded. The trap is that the official source tag was clean while the distributed release carried malicious files, so reading the code does not establish what actually ran. The real question is which code executed, and in his scenario a build job that ran hours earlier held an identity able to retrieve production provider keys, so the exposure survives after the malicious process is gone. His test for choosing a defensive model follows from that: give candidate models the same evidence and tools inside a reproduced incident whose answer is known, and see which one digs past the clean repository to the build record and the keys it could reach.

The same week showed the pattern at small-company scale. On October 21, the founder of a small AI startup took his own service offline for several hours after warnings that the company's Anthropic and Stripe keys had been compromised, as part of a breach at the hosting provider Railway. Jones praises the move: stopping the service let the founder regain control and document what happened for customers who had incomplete information. He pairs that praise with a demand for transparency. Companies hit by a breach owe customers a clear account of what was exposed, whether access has stopped, and what to do next, not a reassurance that everything is fine.

Jones also warns that the defensive tools can wound their owners. LiteLLM reports cases where Claude Code placed a user's GitHub credential into a response that could end up in logs, and he describes websites that ask visiting agents to declare themselves, a request some agents answer by altering the URL they post in ways that can leak personal information such as an email address. His answer is defense in depth and a measure of paranoia, including asking your own AI to audit your setup.

The longer-term fix, he argues, is shrinking what attackers can reach before the next bug arrives. He cites Anthropic work across 25 projects that found almost 500 opportunities to reduce exposed code and unsafe configuration interactions, including an OpenSSH case where complicated signature-handling code could be reached before authentication even when the algorithm was disabled, code then removed in OpenSSH 10.6. Patching faster is necessary but not sufficient; systems also need their chained weaknesses understood and cut down in advance. The spending has already started: Jones notes Bank of America took a charge of a couple of hundred million dollars in the last quarter to hire cyber defenders.

For individuals, the episode closes with five actions. Update browsers, phones, operating systems, and apps, and finish the restart so updates take effect. Audit the connected-app settings on important accounts, starting with email and files, and remove or narrow anything unused. Review browser extensions and restrict their site access where the browser allows it. Use a password manager with a unique password per account, turn on multifactor authentication, and agree on a family voice passphrase against voice fakes. When a breach notice arrives, act on it: change exposed passwords, revoke affected app connections, check with credit bureaus if identity data may be involved, and, for developers, rotate API secrets. A companion Substack post carries the full LiteLLM investigation as a test bed, two copyable audit prompts, and talk tracks for the board, the CFO, engineering, and vendors.

  1. If a single individual with a fleet of agents can now mount attacks that once took a state-sized team, what level of defensive spending and model access becomes the baseline expectation for an ordinary company, and who bears that cost for small firms?
  2. Safeguards meant to restrain offensive AI keep tripping up defenders mid-incident, by this account. Where should the line sit so that the strongest models are available for defense without making the same capability freely available for attack?
  3. Jones argues the real fix is reducing exposed code and chained configuration weaknesses before bugs are found. Can that proactive shrinking keep pace with agents surfacing candidate vulnerabilities by the tens of thousands, or does the backlog grow faster than it can be cut?

AI inference economics and energy constraints

Inference Will Be $10TRN Market: What No One Sees | Software Investors are F***ed | Naveen Rao

On 20VC with Harry Stebbings, the episode "Inference Will Be $10TRN Market: What No One Sees | Software Investors are F***ed | Naveen Rao" is a wide-ranging conversation with Naveen Rao, the founder of Unconventional AI. Rao previously sold MosaicML to Databricks for 1.3 billion dollars, and Stebbings's introduction puts his new company's valuation near 5 billion dollars. The throughline of the interview is that energy, not models, is the real constraint on AI, and that hardware is where the value will sit.

Rao starts with the cost of power itself. Building one gigawatt of energy capacity runs to roughly 50 billion dollars, more than the cost of a leading semiconductor fab. The American grid produces around 900 gigawatts on average and can peak near 1.3 terawatts, and AI growth so far has mostly soaked up spare capacity by running existing infrastructure harder. That spare capacity, he argues, is nearly gone: within the next few years the industry will converge to the actual pace of construction, perhaps 10 gigawatts a year rather than the 40 some claim. China passed the United States in total capacity in 2023 after building six times faster for twenty years.

Asked whether the current market wobble is a bubble bursting, Rao calls it a blip. Intelligence, he says, carries almost no adoption risk, the way a cure for cancer would carry none. A two-tier market for electricity is already forming, with behind-the-meter deals in which data centers pay far more per kilowatt-hour than ordinary grid customers. They can tolerate the premium, he says, because compute sells at roughly five times the price of the electricity behind it, and builders like Bloom Energy are racing to add new supply.

On compute, Rao says the physical layer has stopped delivering easy gains: shrinking transistors no longer buys much efficiency. Recent progress came from the architecture layer, above all from smaller number formats, moving from 32-bit down to 4-bit representations. The largest remaining prize, he argues, sits at the model level, in co-designing the model, the architecture, and the physical hardware together, the way the brain co-evolved its intelligence and its wiring. His company designs energy first rather than speed first, a break with a philosophy he traces back to ENIAC in 1945, which was built, like every machine since, simply to be faster than the alternative.

That leads him to expect deep specialization in chips, built to exploit ideas such as sparsity that general purpose hardware can only use within limits. He does not expect chips to commoditize. Demand for intelligence is effectively unlimited in an adversarial world, hardware is the machinery that converts energy into intelligence, and whoever converts most efficiently captures the value. Nvidia, he says, recognized the shift early and executed superbly, and today it is Nvidia, not the model companies, that takes the largest share of the margin across the stack.

His market arithmetic is sweeping. AI models alone will be a trillion dollar market within a few years, he says, and inference will be a 10 trillion dollar market within ten years, because each large gain in efficiency expands the set of affordable uses rather than shrinking the total. Prices per token will fall and squeeze companies that merely serve other people's models, while the hardware layer keeps its advantage. Hardware, in his phrase, is the place to be over the next ten years.

The bluntest claim is aimed at software investors. Pure software investors, Rao says, are in serious trouble, because the long SaaS era of spinning up cheap applications and fragmenting markets is ending. What replaces it is companies that deliver outcomes through AI combined with humans, with Harvey in legal services as the early example: effectively a better version of a services firm. Most companies will never build their own intelligence, he argues, because that takes real scale; everyone else will buy ready-made services, and the big players who do customize will do it because rivals force them to.

Inside companies, Rao says AI has moved the bottleneck from producing work to verifying it. He can now get an answer in about an hour to a chip design question that once took two weeks of his research team's time, which lets him explore far more ideas, but someone with judgment still has to check every artifact. Agent decisions should not yet be trusted the way trained human judgment is, he says, because judgment is built over years of cases, and long chains of machine decisions can drift far off course while looking robust. Expertise will not go out of style; the value of a future machine may lie in how it was taught, much as a surgeon's worth reflects who trained them.

On how models improve, Rao credits curated data and training curricula more than architecture: what a model can do comes mostly from the data it was taught on and the sequence of training stages, from pre-training through reinforcement learning. Reinforcement learning matters because the world cannot simply be gathered as data; it offers rewards that can be verified. The big labs, racing month to month, cannot afford deep architectural gambles, so startups take those risks instead, much as in drug development. He also expects a post-transformer world eventually, since transformers reason inefficiently, by generating and comparing long traces rather than from first principles the way brains appear to.

His forward views stack up quickly. Within three years he expects about 80 percent of token traffic to flow through open models, while spending splits roughly evenly, since frontier models keep a small set of the hardest and most valuable problems. Robotics he expects to dwarf coding many times over once intelligence enters the physical world: working prototypes in two to three years, a real market in five to seven, with value in the technology stack rather than the application layer. Asked to pick one company other than his own, he named Physical Intelligence, for its work on converting a task into physical action.

He closes with an investor's version of the same argument. For deep technical problems he has stopped treating market validation as the key metric: some markets need no validation, so measurable progress on the problem is what counts. Given unlimited resources, he would build his own fabs and manufacturing, optimizing everything from the raw silicon upward. And he is openly impatient with doomerism, which he calls the stupidest worldview he has heard: AI, in his telling, lets humanity attack scientific questions, from deeper physics onward, that there were never enough people to approach.

Open questions

  1. If the pace of energy construction, not chips or models, sets how fast AI can grow, which of Rao's timelines slips first: the 10 trillion dollar inference market within ten years, or a real robotics market within five to seven?
  2. Rao argues value will concentrate in efficient hardware and in the data curricula that teach machines expertise. What happens to that thesis if a post-transformer architecture arrives that today's specialized chips were not built for?
  3. If verification is the new bottleneck and human judgment takes years to build, do companies actually bank the productivity gains AI promises, or does the cost of checking its output quietly eat the savings?

AI-era cybersecurity and coming system shutdowns

System Shutdowns Are Coming in the Next Year | Joel de la Garza

On MTS, the episode "System Shutdowns Are Coming in the Next Year | Joel de la Garza" is a conversation between host Theo Jaffee and Joel de la Garza, a partner at Andreessen Horowitz who focuses on cybersecurity. It was a quiet week in security, and de la Garza uses the lull to explain why the recent wave of serious vulnerability disclosures, including flaws in PHP, BIND, and the Linux kernel, is not a blip but the start of a long stretch of patching.

His starting point is what he calls the original sin of the security profession. The chief information security officer role was created at Citigroup in the late 1990s, after Russian hackers stole a great deal of money, and the first CISOs were focused on fixing technical problems. Over time, though, the job drifted into risk acceptance: companies learned to live with medium and low severity flaws, on the theory that exploiting them took hundreds of hours of scarce expert time. He compares it to rating a physical safe by how many hours it takes to crack.

AI has now broken that bargain, because the cost of that expert knowledge work has fallen to nearly zero. Agents and bots are scanning code that has sat unexamined for years, and flaws that were always there are suddenly cheap to find. De la Garza expects the disclosures to keep coming for months: beyond the public ones, he says, four or five more are currently under embargo. Attackers are moving too, possibly helped by open weight models from China that he says have been very good at this work for six to eight months, and embargoes leak, which pulls disclosure timelines forward.

His overall read is that this is, so far, relatively healthy. Since Heartbleed, the industry has built a real patching cadence, and modern systems ship as images that can be updated quickly. But he warns his portfolio founders that the pace has to be sustained for months, not treated as one bad week. Security teams will need more resources and more engineering time, and companies will need a zero tolerance policy for vulnerabilities in what they ship. The era of the MVP that ships with known security bugs, he says, has to end.

Hardware is the harder class of problem. De la Garza sits on the board of Eclypsium, which works on firmware and hardware security, and he notes that nation state attackers shifted much of their effort to the hardware layer about fifteen years ago. Critical infrastructure is mostly prepared, if not for the coming volume. The real exposure is the long tail of cheap connected devices, seven to twelve dollar gadgets like heart rate monitors and wearables, which simply cannot be patched. The open question is how much damage such devices can actually do.

Jaffee presses him on the darkest scenario, a cyber event that knocks out a power grid or the internet for days. De la Garza resists the framing. He lived through the 2003 Northeast blackout, which affected 55 million people, and he argues that leaping to doom scenarios makes people check out and feel helpless, which undermines the case for doing the basic work. A catastrophic grid failure is possible, he says, but it sits in roughly the same band of probability as an asteroid strike. The large tech companies, Google and Microsoft in particular, defend themselves at a nation state level, and he guesses they spend closer to twenty percent of their technology budgets on security against an industry norm of ten. The risk concentrates in regional, low margin, underfunded systems instead.

For ordinary people, his advice stays familiar: unique passwords, a password manager, and an authenticator app rather than texted codes. The threat aimed at individuals is still mostly social engineering. He cites the Verizon breach data showing that eighty to ninety percent of security incidents begin with social engineering or spear phishing, and AI driven deepfakes are making that worse. People, he says, remain weaker than the infrastructure.

His closing prediction gives the episode its title. Asked the odds of real disruption to daily life from agent enabled attacks over the next year, he calls disruption almost certain, but not because attacks succeed. Governments and operators are already choosing to take systems offline until they can patch and bring them back safely, a step he says the NSA advised just this week and that he has also seen in the United Kingdom. The likely shape, he predicts, is a DMV closed for a day or two while it upgrades, not a grid in flames. The big banks, he adds, should be fine: they received early access to a frontier model months ago to test their own systems, have done little but patch since, and one of Bank of America or JPMorgan is hiring roughly a thousand additional people just to patch.

  3. If defensive shutdowns become a routine tool, who decides when a public system goes offline to patch, and how much disruption will the public accept before it pushes back?
