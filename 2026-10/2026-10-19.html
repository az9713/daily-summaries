Sources · OpenAI Dots · The future of ChatGPT

OpenAI’s Dots lead on the future of ChatGPT

On Sources, host Alex Heath sits down with Alexander Embiricos, OpenAI's product lead for Dots, for "OpenAI’s Dots lead on the future of ChatGPT," recorded in front of a live audience at OpenAI's 2026 DevDay conference. Dots are OpenAI's personal AI agents, persistent assistants that work from their own cloud computers and look for ways to help without waiting to be prompted. Embiricos joined OpenAI a little over two years ago, when his startup was acquired, and he says shipping Dots has been his mission ever since he arrived.

He frames Dots as a third chapter. First came chat, then coding agents that could actually do things. The remaining problem, he argues, is that even a superb agent only acts when told, like a brilliant principal engineer who refuses to check Slack or Linear. Dots are meant to close that gap. The model is trained to be persistent, to take open-ended assignments like babysitting a pull request or staying on top of a feedback channel, and to propose how it can help instead of waiting for the perfect prompt. He adds that the timing is not only about model capability. A proactive agent is useless without context, so connectors and tool access had to mature, and people and teams have to learn new habits around an agent that works on its own initiative.

The product's shape came out of internal experiments. A Codex feature called /go, which lets a model pursue a problem for a long time, and internal prototypes of long-running agents spread so fast inside OpenAI that a feedback channel for one prototype was constantly active. The team debated shipping a local version that ran on the user's own computer as a smarter coding thread, or waiting for something harder to build: an agent with its own cloud computer and its own identity. They chose the cloud computer and the identity. Embiricos says embodiment matters because people who can see an agent show up in Slack start handing it jobs that take days, and because a named entity makes it intuitive that one agent holds one context across every surface, whether he is talking to his Dot by voice on his commute, tagging it in Slack threads, or using the app.

A naming lesson came free. When everyone at OpenAI could create many agents with whimsical names, nobody in Slack could tell whose agent was whose, so agents are now prefixed with their owner's name: his own agent, which he calls Dot, appears as AE-Dot. His broader take is that most people will have exactly one agent, which should feel like an extension of themselves, while power users may create more. Separately, organizations will run specialist Dots, centrally managed agents with their own identities that own a function or workflow for a whole team, a direction he is excited about but describes as starting slowly with a handful of customers.

The interface is messaging, deliberately as simple as texting a person, with a single thread per agent. Long-form work goes somewhere else: Space, a workspace built for humans and agents as equal collaborators. A page in Space can carry standing instructions for an agent, much like a rules file for a codebase, and because pages are built in HTML they can hold live charts and clickable prototypes that an agent keeps up to date. Embiricos describes tagging his Dot inside a document to flesh out a section, pull in data, or check whether a teammate finished something, instead of bouncing between a document and Slack.

Asked what sets Dots apart from rival personal agents, he gives two answers: the most capable agent, and the safest and most trustworthy one. Dots run on GPT-6 Astra, which he calls OpenAI's most capable and most aligned model, inside a modified version of the Codex harness that was re-evaluated for persistent behavior, including prompt injection tests over email that he says produced no successful injections. The default posture is conservative. A Dot does proactive research across the tools it is connected to, but it confirms before acting: it will flag a calendar conflict rather than move the meeting, and draft an answer rather than send it. Owners can add their own rules, a second model called auto review watches the first against those rules, and everything the agent and its sub-agents do can be audited afterward. In Slack, a Dot responds only when tagged unless its owner tells it to listen more widely. He also stresses that the rogue-agent incidents OpenAI has disclosed involved internal models that were never intended to ship, running without the product's sandboxing and safeguards.

The business model is unusual. Dot usage does not count toward plan limits, and Embiricos says that is permanent. Rather than metering, OpenAI plans a flat monthly price, which he compares to buying internet or keeping a responsive contractor on call: the agent is available around the clock and always answers quickly, while the amount of work it does varies with what you pay. As a user approaches a limit, the agent might switch to a cheaper model or ask whether a job can run overnight instead. The rollout starts with Pro, Business Premium, and enterprise customers, whom he calls the most AI literate audience, with texting from phones coming soon. On the open web, he promises a partnership-first approach rather than a pirate one. OpenAI's agent openly declares that it is a bot, using WebBot Auth built with Cloudflare. On advertising, he concedes that nobody in the industry has an answer yet, only that services which lose human ad views to agents will need either the ads preserved or some other way of being paid for what they provide.

ChatGPT itself, he insists, is here to stay. Heath puts ChatGPT at 1.2 billion weekly users, and Embiricos says the agent products Codex and Work have about 35 million users between them, and that Work should simply merge into Chat. Dots start as a premium standalone product, repeating what he calls the Codex playbook: ship the frontier capability carefully to a narrow audience, learn, then broaden. The plan is to move from Pro to Plus and eventually bake the capabilities into Chat itself, reaching free users without asking a billion people to change how they use the product. Asked about the next year, he mostly deflects, telling the story of Greg Brockman informing him that two years is an incredibly long time at OpenAI and that he should have asked about success in two months.

His closing story is the one he says blew his mind. An engineer who had asked his Dot to stay on top of a feedback channel woke up to a stream of congratulations in Slack: overnight, the Dot had spotted a problem in the feedback, written a fix, and opened a pull request. Following the engineer's own habits, it had not left the request in draft mode and had set it to auto-merge, a colleague approved it, and the bug was fixed before it ever reached production. Embiricos tells the story with some reticence, since the fix happened without the engineer knowing, and says that is exactly why the rollout starts small and careful.

Open questions

  1. Dots ask before acting by default, and the plan is for trust to widen over time. What evidence tells OpenAI, or an owner, that an agent has earned the right to act without asking, and who gets to decide when the defaults loosen?
  2. The flat monthly price promises an agent that is always available and never runs out, while the amount of work quietly flexes with the tier. Can that model survive heavy users, or do the economics eventually force metering back in?
  3. If agents increasingly browse, shop, and read on behalf of people who never see the ads, what replaces advertising as the way the open web gets paid, and who negotiates that settlement?

Sourcery · Apollo · The Yahoo playbook

Apollo’s Yahoo Playbook, 5 Years After the $5B Deal

On Sourcery, host Molly O'Shea sits down with Jim Lanzone, Yahoo's chief executive, for "Apollo’s Yahoo Playbook, 5 Years After the $5B Deal." Apollo Global Management bought Yahoo and AOL from Verizon in 2021 for about $4.8 billion, in a deal structured as roughly $4.25 billion in cash and $750 million in preferred interests, with Verizon keeping a 10 percent stake. Five years on, Lanzone says Yahoo has already returned Apollo's capital, making it one of the fastest returning deals in the firm's history, and that the company is now, in his words, playing with house money.

Lanzone was not an Apollo insider. He was called in while the firm was deciding whether to buy, and he took the job in September 2021, in the middle of the COVID Delta wave. It was his third turnaround: he had run Ask Jeeves through the web 1.0 crash under Barry Diller's IAC, then led CBS Interactive, and he had spent a short stretch as Tinder's chief executive in 2020. He says Verizon had paid roughly twice what Apollo paid for the combined assets, and that under Verizon the brands had been bent toward someone else's mission, helping sell 5G, rather than their own.

He pushes back on the private equity stereotype directly. Apollo, he says, was not stripping the company for parts. The deal partner, Reed Rayman, wore a "Make Yahoo Great Again" hat in purple, still talks with Lanzone almost daily five years later, and now heads Apollo's AI sector work while chairing Yahoo's board. Lanzone calls himself a growth person who only does turnarounds to reach the fun part, investing in new products for a huge inherited audience, and says he would not have taken the job under a cut-and-flip plan.

The first structural move was to treat Yahoo as what it is: a conglomerate of brands, not one product. Yahoo Finance competes with Bloomberg and CNBC, Sports with ESPN, Mail with Gmail, and each needs its own leadership. Verizon had run one head of product, one head of technology, and one head of content across everything. Lanzone reorganized the company into general-manager-led brands, each with its own product, technology, content, and usually sales leads, staffed through 2022 with a mix of internal promotions and outside experts. Every general manager, he notes, has been a founder at least once.

Underneath the structure, he reinstated the founding mission: being the trusted guide to the internet, helping people get things done in their personal lives, the job Jerry Yang and David Filo set in 1995. He is blunt about the original sin that broke it. In June 2000 Yahoo outsourced search, first to Inktomi and eventually to Google, because search looked like a cost center with no business model, even though most internet sessions started there. Generations of strategy wandered after that, through a Hollywood phase, a parade of chief executives, and finally the Verizon years.

Focus meant exits. EdgeCast, a content delivery network, went first. The bigger surgery was ad technology: AOL and Yahoo had each bought more than 30 ad tech companies over the years, and the inherited stack of supply-side platform, demand-side platform, and native ads lost hundreds of millions of dollars a year while also forcing Yahoo to take ads only from itself. In February 2023 Yahoo shut the supply-side platform down rather than sell it to anyone who would expect preferential treatment, a move that came with more than 2,000 layoffs. The native business was sold to Taboola for a 25 percent stake in that company. One piece was a keeper: the demand-side platform, which Lanzone calls a crown jewel and says wins nine of ten head-to-head tests on the strength of Yahoo's first-party data. TechCrunch was divested, and AOL was sold to Bending Spoons in October of last year, in a deal reported at $1.4 billion to $1.5 billion.

What remained is still enormous. Yahoo reaches about 700 million users a month and sits in the top five internet properties in the United States. Sports and Finance each draw over 100 million users a month, about a third of Americans hold a Yahoo Mail account, and News is a top three aggregator after a deliberate return to aggregation over original reporting, with content meant to give context, like fantasy and market analysis. About 75 percent of users arrive directly rather than through search, and roughly 75 percent are logged in on any given day, which Lanzone says makes Yahoo unusually effective for advertisers.

The product philosophy is a video game rule: you cannot skip levels. Some Yahoo products had not been meaningfully improved in a decade, so the first level was fixing them. Only then came new builds. A college fantasy football launch this summer drew 40 percent of its users from people new to Yahoo, in a year when the platform also posted its three biggest NFL fantasy days ever. The flagship is Yahoo Scout, the company's first in-house search engine in 17 years, launched earlier this year. Scout grounds Yahoo's own assets, 30 years of search history from what is still the third-ranked search engine in the United States, publisher relationships, a knowledge graph, and user data, then routes that grounded context through a lightweight model, Anthropic's Haiku at launch. It also ends 17 years of outsourcing search to Microsoft, a dependence Lanzone calls a vulnerable position for a core product and revenue stream.

Trust, he argues, is the asset the AI era rewards. Yahoo is not building a frontier model and carries none of that backlash. More than half its audience is Gen Z and millennial, 75 percent of Gen Z uses a Yahoo product, and its campaigns have produced a 31-point lift in brand love among Gen Z. Mail is the strategic center: it is the vault of a person's life, which is why every new agent product asks for email and calendar access first, and Yahoo already holds that permission. He says the mission filter also keeps Yahoo out of the AI gold rush: no enterprise pivot, no chasing AI coding or cloud businesses, just the personal-life job it has done for 30 years.

On the endgame, he frames Yahoo as a later-stage pre-IPO company whose investors, venture or private equity, eventually expect an outcome, an IPO or a sale, though he offers no date. He expects the surprise, when financials surface, to be how healthy the growth is for a 30-year-old brand. He is openly amused that startups with less than one-tenth of one percent of Yahoo's users carry higher valuations, and that search startups were at one point valued at six times Yahoo. Asked for the biggest mistake companies make at scale, he says falling asleep at the wheel under leaders who are not product-driven. Having lived through the dot-com crash, the financial crisis, COVID, the 2022 selloff, and tariffs, he calls Yahoo a resilient company that will be fine if this cycle pops too. His closing claim is simple: the best days of Yahoo can still be ahead of it.

Open questions

  1. Apollo has its capital back and Lanzone concedes that investors eventually expect an outcome. When Yahoo does go public or sell, will the comeback story survive public-market scrutiny of a 30-year-old brand, and what valuation would a direct, logged-in audience of this size actually command?
  2. Scout grounds Yahoo's proprietary data in lightweight third-party models rather than competing at the frontier. As the big AI answer engines improve, does that architecture stay distinctive, and can Yahoo convert the trust around Mail into a real position when agent products come asking for email and calendar access?
  3. Yahoo's growth is coming from younger users while its most loyal users resist any change to familiar products. How long can it modernize aggressively enough to keep winning Gen Z without breaking the habits that produce its unusually direct, logged-in traffic?

Theories of Everything · AI and physics · String theory

Could AI Find Physics We Can’t Understand?

On Theories of Everything with Curt Jaimungal, host Curt Jaimungal interviews Hirosi Ooguri, chair of Caltech's Division of Physics, Mathematics and Astronomy, in "Could AI Find Physics We Can’t Understand?," billed as Ooguri's first English-language podcast. The conversation ranges over his whole career in string theory and quantum gravity, but it opens and closes on a worry. AI may one day hand us the answer to a fundamental question in theoretical physics, he says, and we may be unable to understand it, the way his late dog Rosie knew food lived in the refrigerator without any hope of grasping why it stays cold.

His path into physics began in fifth grade. From a revolving restaurant, using the similarity of triangles, the height of a building estimated from the monsters in films he loved, and the distance to his father's hometown on the horizon, he worked out the size of the Earth to the right order of magnitude. The lesson he took from it became his creed: there is truth in the world, and we can know about it. He chose physics over mathematics because he wanted this world rather than the Platonic world of all possible worlds. Mathematics, in his account, is the language physics thinks in, a part of the process rather than the destination.

That distinction runs through everything else he says. Mathematical truth, given axioms and logic, is exact and timeless. Physical truth is approximate, built on observation, and so far it has held across an enormous span of scales, from far below the atom to the edge of the observable universe. He is a pragmatist about the gap between the two: whatever lies beyond what can be described and known makes no practical difference to a person with a finite lifespan.

There is one place where the description itself is expected to give out. Following an argument associated with Nima Arkani-Hamed, Ooguri expects space and time to stop making sense below the Planck length, so the centuries-long progression toward ever more microscopic, ever more fundamental laws ends at the unification of gravity and quantum mechanics. He calls quantum gravity the final boss of physics, the last in a line of unification battles that runs from Newton and Maxwell through Einstein, and the one after which the search for deeper layers in the microscopic direction simply stops.

The sharpest part of the conversation is his explanation of why AI has raced ahead in mathematics while lagging in physics. Language models thrive where statements are precise and can be checked by machine: a proof can be certified by a system like Lean, a program can simply be run, and that checkability feeds reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Physics statements are only approximately true, many are not mathematically rigorous, and there is no automatic process that certifies a new theory, so the loop that powers progress in math does not exist for theoretical physics. Quantum field theory, the basic language of particle physics, still has no firm mathematical foundation at all.

That missing foundation is the Yang-Mills mass gap problem, which Ooguri describes as the hardest of the Millennium problems. He recounts how mathematicians at the July International Congress voted on which problem would fall first: Yang-Mills was judged least likely, Navier-Stokes among the most likely, and Navier-Stokes was indeed solved soon after, in the episode's telling by OpenAI fielding tens of thousands of agents once it was clear a solution was within reach of existing methods. Yang-Mills, he stresses, is a mathematics problem rather than a physics one: physicists have no doubt the theory exists, since its predictions are among the most precise ever tested, and what is missing is a rigorous formulation. His hope is less for the solution itself than for the new mathematical tools the attempt will create, and he says he would be even more excited if the conjecture were disproved, because that would mean something deep about the particle world has been misunderstood all along.

He keeps his timescale expectations calibrated by history. Quantum mechanics and special relativity were unified within a couple of years in the late 1920s, yet it took fifty years to build the Standard Model on that foundation and forty more to verify it completely, ending with the Higgs boson. Unifying quantum mechanics with gravity took fifty years just to produce string theory as a consistent candidate. Scaling by those ratios, he estimates a realistic string model of our universe could take 1,250 years to arrive and another 1,000 years to test, roughly 2,250 years of human work, comparable to the span between the Greek atomic hypothesis and its experimental confirmation. AI may compress that enormously. Whether understanding would survive the compression is the open fear behind the episode's title. Asked what he would ask an oracle, he does not hesitate: the fundamental law that unifies gravity and quantum mechanics in this universe.

Much of his own research concerns what gravity forbids. The swampland program, launched by his collaborator Cumrun Vafa in 2005, holds that not every low-energy theory containing gravity can be completed into a consistent theory at the Planck scale, which breaks the older assumption, inherited from Kenneth Wilson, that any effective theory is permissible. His distance conjecture with Vafa says every parameter in a low-energy theory must be the expectation value of some field, a condition with consequences for inflation and dark matter. Because the AdS/CFT correspondence defines quantum gravity in terms of a better understood conformal field theory, some of these conjectures can actually be proved: with Daniel Harlow he proved quantum gravity allows no global symmetries, and he has since proved parts of the distance conjecture, whose remaining cases could be tested by cosmic microwave background polarization experiments in the coming decade.

He also tells the story of his favorite unsolved puzzle. As a graduate student he computed an infinite sequence of integers from the geometry of K3 surfaces for his PhD thesis, and twenty years later, chatting with colleagues during a snowstorm in Aspen, he found the same numbers sitting in the character table of the Mathieu group M24. The phenomenon, named Mathieu moonshine, ties his sequence to the mock modular forms Srinivasa Ramanujan described in his final letter, and he is still working on it, this month with a visiting mathematician. It is a small example of the test he borrowed from Henri Poincaré: a discovery matters in proportion to how much of science it touches, and string theory's methods have repeatedly spilled over into mathematics.

For his own daily work, AI is already indispensable, but on his terms. In theoretical physics judgment, choosing concepts and approaches, he still rates current models at the level of a beginning graduate student, and he notes in the episode that he said so in a Tokyo lecture in March 2026, weeks before the Navier-Stokes news showed how far beyond that level they already were in mathematics. His method is to carve a physics question down to a sharply posed mathematical subtask and hand the machine that, a division of labor behind his recent classification of entanglement inequalities for holographic systems, done with reinforcement learning once the physics had been turned into combinatorics. The rest of his routine guards the slow human part: mornings reserved for thinking, administrative work confined to afternoons in his role as division chair, and acceptance that on most days a fundamental question yields nothing but a blank sheet of paper. His commitment, he insists, is not to string theory but to the truth. If a better route to the unified law appeared, he would take it.

Open questions

  1. If an AI produces a unification of gravity and quantum mechanics that no human can follow, does that answer count as physical knowledge, and what would verification even mean?
  2. Can anything like the verifiable-reward loop that powers AI progress in mathematics be built for theoretical physics, or does the approximate, unrigorous character of physical statements rule it out for good?
  3. The distance conjecture constrains how inflation could have unfolded in the early universe. Will the cosmic microwave background polarization measurements of the next decade bear those constraints out?

WSJ Leadership Institute · Jamie Dimon · Global risks

Jamie Dimon on the Biggest Risks Facing the Global Economy | WSJ Leadership Institute

On WSJ Podcasts, in the WSJ Leadership Institute series, host Alan Murray, the institute's president, sits down with Jamie Dimon, chairman and chief executive of JPMorganChase, for "Jamie Dimon on the Biggest Risks Facing the Global Economy." The conversation was recorded on October 7, 2026, at the bank's new global headquarters in New York, in front of members of the WSJ CEO Council. Dimon is plainly proud of the building, whose trading floors move two to three trillion dollars a day and whose Patriots bar displays flags from the USS Constitution, the D-Day landings, and the Apollo missions. He says he salutes the flag in the lobby every morning, because it reminds him of the values, free speech, free religion, free enterprise, that built the country.

Much of the conversation is a one-year progress report on the bank's Security and Resiliency Initiative, its plan to finance American and allied supply chains in areas like grids and power, drones, space, missiles, advanced manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, and healthcare. Dimon says the initiative will blow well past its 1.5 trillion dollar scale, with 10 billion dollars of that as direct investment, and that it now spans 29 industries. Some of the deals are earlier-stage and riskier than the bank's norm, deliberately so: defense suppliers often cannot win a contract until they can prove they can build the plant, and cannot build the plant without the money, and the bank is trying to bridge that gap. The effort has an advisory circle that includes Jeff Bezos, Michael Dell, Condoleezza Rice, and Bob Gates, with General Paul Nakasone involved on the cyber side, and it has been extended to allies including Germany, Italy, France, Britain, Poland, Australia, Japan, and South Korea. Alongside the money, Dimon stresses a less glamorous half of the work: research into ecosystems like shipbuilding, chips, and pharmaceuticals, and skills and training for the thousands of small vendors those industries depend on.

His case for doing this now is partly a confession. America outsourced advanced manufacturing to save, by his estimate, almost nothing, around 500 million dollars, five cents on a magnet that might add ten dollars to a car, and in doing so it lost not just production but the intellectual property and the ability itself, which rivals kept improving for a decade. The country now depends on China for rare earths and on single sources for semiconductors and active pharmaceutical ingredients. China, he says, was strategic for years while America behaved like, in his words, a bunch of gangly teenagers who wanted a beer. The world has also become more dangerous: two wars that will not end, a superpower rivalry, and the postwar institutions deteriorating. The invasion of Ukraine, he argues, did not change the world; it reminded everyone that peace was never guaranteed and that bad people do bad things, and America was not prepared.

Asked for the biggest risk he sees, Dimon names two. The first is that the current wars develop in unexpected ways and disrupt populations, alliances, and the world economy. The second is cyber. He had written in his chairman's letter that cyber was the bank's biggest risk, a couple of weeks before Anthropic's Mythos model was released, and he says that now it is the biggest risk by far. He points to the undersea fiber cables that carry enormous volumes of company data: Russia has already cut some in the Baltic Sea, a severed cable can cut off a whole country, and satellites cannot replace that traffic. Threats like that, he notes, are asymmetric in ways drone warfare has also exposed.

His defensive prescription is concrete. Banks are comparatively well protected, he says, but whole critical industries are not, including water, parts of transport, some technology companies, and smaller banks, and everyone shares vendors, so one weak supplier can endanger a prime contractor. He is temporarily chairing the Alliance for Critical Infrastructure, a group of about 50 large companies founded by Tom Fanning, which is setting up clearinghouses to vet open-source code and share what it learns across industries, including banks' own dependencies on telecom, water, SWIFT, chips, and Fedwire. His homework for every company, starting today: segment your networks, lock down identity and access management, use the most powerful AI models you have to hunt for vulnerabilities, patch quickly when they surface, and make cyber a standing item in management meetings. Smarter Models Killed the Harness. Here's What's Left — Jediah Katz, SpaceXAI

On AI Engineer, Jediah Katz gives a conference talk called "Smarter Models Killed the Harness. Here's What's Left," built around one question: is the agent harness dead? Katz joined Cursor early in 2025 as the longest tenured engineer working on its agent harness, and he recently moved to SpaceX's AI team through the acquisition of Cursor. Few people are better placed to ask whether the software layer he spent years building still matters.

A harness, in his telling, is the eyes and hands of a model. It wraps around the model, steers it, and connects it to computers and to third party services such as MCP servers. He divides it into two layers. The runtime is the foundation: it decides where the core agent loop runs and how state is stored so work can resume. The context and orchestration layers are the wilder part, deciding which model gets called when and what it sees, from prompts and tools to memory compaction and a set of quiet tricks that keep the agent behaving as intended. The job itself has been renamed twice in three years, from prompt engineer in early 2023, to context engineer in mid 2025, to harness engineering at the start of this year.

The talk opens at what he calls peak harness. Around the new year, Cursor announced it had built a browser entirely from scratch using a new long running harness called Grind Swarm, a multimodal system that orchestrated large numbers of agents and machines. It was very expensive, and it took Cursor's own service down once or twice along the way. Within a few months, though, the mood online flipped to declaring the harness dead. Tariq at Anthropic described stripping much of the system prompt out of Claude Code. Garry Tan of Y Combinator predicted the harness would fade away and only skills would matter. Katz and his colleague Stefan wrote about removing guardrails from Cursor that had been patching gaps in the models' intelligence, among them a small helper model whose only job was applying code edits, because frontier models were bad at that, and detailed instructions on how to explore a codebase and check your own work.

He then argues the obituaries are half right, and he brings receipts. A UC Berkeley study ran seven frontier models through several harnesses, including Pi, Codex, and Claude Code, and found that on the newer, smarter models the choice of harness made no significant difference to scores on SWE-bench Light. A second study, from Apple and EPFL, found much the same for a dedicated machine learning engineering harness on MLE-bench. His verdict: a fancy harness no longer adds coding intelligence. The skills the harness used to teach, exploring a codebase, sticking with a task for a long time, running tests to check the work, have been absorbed into the models themselves.

The reason, he says, is a data flywheel, and the harness is what spins it. A better harness brings harder coding tasks within reach, so more people attempt those tasks with agents. That produces more coding data flowing to the labs, and the models eventually learn the patched skills natively. At that point the harness can delete the scaffolding it once needed. The harness, in other words, keeps making parts of itself obsolete.

But dead is the wrong word, for two reasons. The first is that the harness is also what opens the next frontier. His example is Grokbot, SpaceXAI's autonomous colleague for knowledge work well beyond coding. Its harness is built from long lived coordinator bots that orchestrate fleets of worker agents, a structure he compares to a senior lawyer who owns the strategy and farms the work out to paralegals. Notably, the Grokbot team has been putting prompting and guardrails back in, the opposite of the Cursor trend, because its tasks are ones models have not mastered: website flows such as making slide decks and booking travel, keeping a user informed without overwhelming them, knowing when to act and when to ask before doing something risky like emailing a colleague, and learning how someone works through memory. The same pattern shows up inside software engineering, where the more senior the work, the less of it is pure coding, and in the idea of the software factory: a bug reported in a chat channel gets triaged automatically, a cloud agent fixes it and proves the fix with before and after videos, and another agent watches the production rollout. He calls that a meta harness, one harness coordinating many agents.

The second reason is that some parts of the harness sit outside the context layer, where the model cannot absorb them. Infrastructure is one: agent tasks that once took minutes now run for hours or days, and a laptop that goes to sleep cannot host a truly autonomous colleague, so the runtime has to live in the cloud. Trust and security are another: a toy harness that hands the model a shell and a file full of API keys can pass coding evals, but companies need permissions scoped to specific agents and people, and everyone needs protection against a catastrophic action or a 10,000 dollar API bill. The third is efficiency. Both studies he cited found large differences in cost per task between harnesses even where scores were level, and he describes spending the past month tuning Grokbot's harness, improving cache hit rates, token efficiency, and the mix of models, to cut its cost and response time substantially with no loss of quality.

His forecast is that the flywheel is speeding up, and that products are once again moving faster than the models, so harnesses will get messier before they get tidier. He expects teams to design them for easy shipping and unshipping of features, to expose less of the machinery to users, since anything exposed becomes something users depend on, and to build composable primitives instead: his example is subscriptions, a single framework that lets an agent wake up in response to an event, whether that is a Slack message, a failing check on its pull request, or a time of day. Stopgap fixes, which his team calls shims, are tracked centrally and logged every time they fire, and a bot watches the dashboard and has an agent open a cleanup pull request when a shim stops firing. Harnesses are also rising a level of abstraction: where the last generation was assembled from raw model calls, the new one uses agents as the building blocks. His headline prediction is dated: within six months, 90 percent of agent work will run off the laptop.

So his answer to his own question is split. The parts of the harness that made agents smarter at coding are gone, and the flywheel explains why. But the harness remains how agents reach each new frontier of work, and it owns the infrastructure, trust, and efficiency that models cannot absorb. Yesterday's harness may be dead, he concludes, but long live the agent harness of tomorrow.

Open questions

  1. If the choice of harness no longer moves coding benchmark scores, how should a team actually choose one: on cost per task, on the security model, on how quickly the vendor ships and removes features? And can buyers measure those things as easily as they measure an eval score?
  2. Katz predicts that within six months, 90 percent of agent work will run off the laptop. What happens to local first tools, and to organizations whose security rules will not allow unattended agents running in a vendor's cloud with scoped access to internal systems?
  3. The flywheel argument says models eventually absorb whatever the harness patches. Should that apply to judgment calls like acting versus asking, and to the trust and safety layer, or is there a line where behavior should stay in deterministic code no matter how capable models become?

Why Cheap Hardware Changes Robotics | Joel Jang

On MTS, host Sophia Dew interviews Joel Jang, co-founder and CEO of Dream Labs, on the day his company came out of stealth with a $90 million seed round and launched Dream 1, a robot foundation model that anyone can call through a public API. The episode's title is also Jang's thesis: hardware used to be expensive because robot software was weak, and now that the models are capable, cheap hardware suddenly becomes useful.

Dream Labs started at Nvidia, where Jang and his three co-founders worked together for about three years. They became convinced that the dominant recipe for robot foundation models was wrong. Those models were built on vision language models, which carry a lot of knowledge about language but little spatial intelligence. Jang's team bet instead on backbones pre-trained on video, which they call world action models. Because video pre-training can learn from all video, including footage of humans doing useful things, the models pick up physical priors that robot-only data cannot supply.

Dream 1, the flagship model shipped today, is the first public API of its kind, and Jang says it generalizes zero shot to robots it has never seen, tasks it was never trained on, and environments it has never been in. The company's promise to users is everyday capabilities for everyday robots. Before this, he explains, a robot developer had to buy a robot, pick a task, collect data, choose an open source foundation model, rent GPUs to train it, and rent more GPUs to host it, a three to six month slog for a single task. Dream Labs aims to remove those layers so a developer can simply start using the model.

The deeper problem, Jang argues, is how few robot users exist. The industry leaders sold roughly ten thousand robots each last year, so perhaps thirty thousand users in total, and no business succeeds with thirty thousand users. His favorite counterexample is Hugging Face's Microduck, a $400 robot that sold ten thousand units in four days. It is cute, it rattles around, but it also has a beak and can pick things up, and Dream Labs has already run Dream 1 on it to do useful things. A capable two arm robot now costs around $2,000, less than an expensive laptop, and can already fold clothes. Capable models, in his telling, are what turn that affordable hardware from a novelty into a platform.

His strongest take is that the robotics takeoff will happen with consumers, not in enterprise. Teams chasing highly specialized models with very high reliability are, in his view, building work that generalized models will wash away when they arrive. Rather than guessing the killer application first, Dream Labs shipped the API and intends to learn from what people actually ask robots to do, the same way prompting data taught the LLM labs what to build. Within a year or two, he expects affordable robots moving around on wheels, doing useful work in everyday places.

The launch numbers are early but real: within five hours, users from 22 countries had signed up and made 70,000 inference calls, mostly pick and place experiments. Jang is candid that Dream 1 is not perfect and does not yet post the highest success rate on every task. What comes next is cheaper inference, which he puts at 16 to 40 times cheaper than controlling robots with big lab models, then mobile manipulation as robots start moving around, and dexterous hands once that hardware gets cheaper.

Open questions

  1. Dream 1's zero shot claim spans unseen robots, unseen tasks, and unseen environments, yet Jang concedes its success rates are not yet the highest. What reliability bar does a $400 to $2,000 consumer robot actually have to clear before it is a product rather than a toy, and who measures that?
  2. If the takeoff is consumer led and usage data is the real moat, who captures the value: the model API, the cheap hardware makers, or whoever aggregates the users first?
  3. Jang bets that generalized models wash away specialized, high reliability efforts. What happens in settings where that last stretch of reliability is the whole point, such as robots working around children, elderly people, or heavy machinery?

Terence Tao & Yu Deng: Why We Build Open Math Model

On SAIR, the SAIR Foundation's interview channel, Fields Medalist Terence Tao of UCLA and Yu Deng of the University of Chicago discuss what happens to mathematics when a commercial AI company releases more than 700 machine generated proofs at once. The conversation moves from that proof drop to the deeper questions it raised about credit, understanding, and openness, and it ends with the pair explaining the Open Math OS, the open alternative their foundation is building.

The immediate problem is quality. After the release, three of the proofs had to be retracted because of basic sign errors, and readers noticed a wide gap between the explanatory text and the formal code. Tao and Deng worry that dumping half checked proofs into the wild pollutes the literature and turns working mathematicians into unpaid debuggers for a company's marketing.

Tao resists framing any of this as being for or against AI. Asking whether someone is pro AI, he says, is like asking whether they are pro car or anti car: every technology has gains and costs, and the real task is balance. AI can automate tasks that used to be extremely laborious, but it also displaces slower ways of working that produce understanding, community, and trained students, and speed is not the only thing worth optimizing.

That leads him to incentives. The mathematical community has always prized solving hard problems, but a solved problem was really a stand in for a bundle of goods: the solver understood the result, could explain it, could pose new questions, and could teach it. Companies now solve such problems almost incidentally, as benchmarks for general purpose models, and Tao describes the resulting proofs as a kind of toxic byproduct that someone still has to deal with. His proposal is to reward explanation and insight explicitly, to develop best practices for these releases, and for companies to bring mathematicians in before publication rather than asking them to clean up afterward.

Deng frames the shift with two boundaries. One boundary separates questions that have proofs from questions that do not. A second separates proofs that exist from proofs that people actually understand. Before AI, the second boundary barely mattered, because whoever produced a proof understood it. Now a proof can sit on the far side of that line, known only to a machine, and he argues the community's underlying value, understanding the world, has not changed even though the situation has.

Credit is where the two guests sound most concerned. Science has treated its literature as a commons for centuries, free to build on as long as sources are cited, so later readers can trace where ideas came from. An AI system absorbs that whole literature and returns answers opaquely, and the current tools are poor at showing which papers an idea actually came from. Tao notes that companies have little incentive to fix this, since it suits them to imply the answers came from nothing. Deng adds that researchers have already seen ideas they typed into a chatbot reappear inside generated proofs, with no way to follow the trail.

The Open Math OS is their answer. Tao chairs the SAIR Foundation, and he says its founding vision was tools built by the community and aligned with the community's values, independent of the big tech companies whose proprietary models he and his colleagues still use daily. The plan was long term, but the recent proof releases, and the surge of interest in alternatives that followed, pushed the foundation to accelerate. What exists today is the framework: an operating layer around existing open source models, with collaboration tools, that a mathematician can use without hosting a model or buying a GPU. The foundation is assembling teams to contribute ideas, data, training, and compute, and Tao hopes its own models, built on the open ones, will arrive within a few months.

Deng joined for a simple reason: science has always been open, so AI for science should be open too, rather than resting on closed tools. He describes first discussing the project with SAIR around the International Congress of Mathematicians, and finding friends at USC making nearly identical plans. In his telling, groups around the world are now starting similar efforts, and keeping mathematics open has become a shared academic project.

Both men close on what stays human. Tao's word is empowerment: the right tools leave their users smarter, the way a car used mindfully does not have to cost you the ability to walk. Deng points to the skills that become more valuable, not less: asking the right next question, holding a global plan for a proof, and introducing genuinely new ideas. He gives a fresh example in which a collaborator who already understood a hard PDE problem and had a proof plan handed the execution to a frontier AI model, which completed it in a day or two. The machine did the labor; the understanding, and the plan, were the human's.

Open questions

  1. If machine proofs now arrive far faster than anyone can read them, who does the slow work of understanding and explaining them, and what replaces problem solving as the reward the field organizes around?
  2. Can the chain of credit ever be restored for AI generated proofs, given that today's models cannot reliably show which human work their ideas came from?
  3. The Open Math OS starts as a framework around other people's open models, with SAIR's own models still months away. Can a community project keep pace with the frontier labs, and what would persuade working mathematicians to switch?
