Invest Like The Best · Cliff Sosin · Free intelligence and what stays scarce

Why Free Intelligence Won’t Change Everything

On Invest Like The Best, the episode "Why Free Intelligence Won’t Change Everything" is a conversation between host Patrick O'Shaughnessy and investor Cliff Sosin. Sosin begins by holding two stories at once. Token use could explode for years and make everyone in the buildout a fortune, or the cost per token could collapse so quickly that we discover we have wildly overbuilt. He can tell both stories with equal certainty, and the rest of the conversation is his framework for deciding what survives either way.

His central claim is that intelligence itself is widely misunderstood. People picture it as an unbounded quantity, rising like the height of a tree from amoeba to monkey to Einstein and then zooming off toward God. Sosin says it is better pictured as a ratio, like the roundness of a ball. Intelligence is a gadget for making predictions: we hold a thin scattering of facts about a complex universe and interpolate between them. In tidy domains such as mathematics, that interpolation can approach perfection. Most of the interesting questions, though, live in complex dynamic systems, and there the trick fails.

The founding story of chaos theory makes the point. A researcher reran an early weather model and got a completely different forecast within days, and eventually traced the divergence to initial conditions that a technician had rounded at one fewer decimal place. Tiny errors compound through every step of such a system, which is why no amount of intelligence can simply think its way to the future of the weather, of groups of people interacting, or of cream mixing into coffee. Past a certain point, better answers require new information, and new information means experiments, and experiments mean touching reality, which takes time. One corollary is that intelligence gets turned toward reducing its own cost, so very good intelligence becomes nearly free, and good-enough intelligence turns out to cover most of the questions people actually need answered.

Sosin's investing rule follows directly: assume everyone has superb intelligence, and ask what is left. What is left are the complements to intelligence: proprietary data, attention, emotional attachment, physical proximity, scale, and, a little cynically, regulatory capture. The mirror image also matters. Many software products are crystallized intelligence, hardened projections of thinking that a general-purpose model can simply reproduce. And many businesses quietly profit from customer lethargy: the depositor who never chases a better rate because thinking about it costs more than it pays. Agents do not get lethargic. They optimize continuously, and that prospect alone reprices a large part of the economy.

Meta is his model owner of complements. Its core business, he says, is now personalized TV. In a deliberately reductionist view, the company takes in about $250 billion a year and spends roughly the same, and nearly all of that spending buys intelligence: data centers, inference, and very smart people writing algorithms and code. If the price of intelligence falls steeply over the next five years, that cost base deflates, while what Meta actually owns, enormous data and enormous attention, is untouched by cheap intelligence. Creators make better content, recommendations improve, ads target and convert better. Grindr makes the same point at small scale: a company with data and attention whose ambitions have been capped by a small team, the sort of business that could become far more if it suddenly had ten thousand of Google's best engineers for five years.

On the frontier labs, Sosin describes models climbing a stack of tasks, with each layer they saturate commoditizing behind them, since nobody sensible pays frontier prices to run a customer service chatbot. Demand for intelligence looks diamond-shaped to him: it widens through very good intelligence and then narrows, because truly elite intelligence is needed only rarely, and some of the grandest prizes, like curing cancer, require experiments rather than more IQ points. Even a lab that becomes the low-cost producer of good-enough intelligence has won a deflating commodity market, which may not be a large prize.

He is equally skeptical of the Jevons paradox argument that cheaper intelligence must mean endlessly more spending on it. Jevons, he says, describes one stretch of a U-shaped curve. Moving information used to be expensive; as the cost collapsed, traffic and the market grew for a long time, and then the market shrank even as costs kept falling. Cogent Communications routes a large share of all internet traffic and is worth only a few billion dollars. Nor can models manufacture unlimited new questions on their own. They will first harvest the insights already latent in the scientific literature, connections across papers that no human had cross-read, and that harvest will be wonderful. After it, the bottleneck is designing and running experiments. Automated laboratories and robotics close that loop, and he calls the idea super great, but the loop has speed limits: an experiment that requires breeding mice still takes as long as mice take.

He has deliberately stayed ignorant of the supply-side buildout, because forecasting inference demand and inference cost within orders of magnitude looks impossible, and value accrues only at bottlenecks in the chain. The flood of brilliant people into the buildout proves less than it seems, since no huge infrastructure buildout of an important technology ever happens without strong price signals and great talent; the causality may run backwards. Capital cycles like this one end in overbuilding, with long lead times and a winner's curse for whoever stops last. He would rather own the equilibrium that follows than bet on which railroad tycoon wins: the consumer brands that come after the railroads, not the railroads. He also stresses that consumer demand for AI is genuinely price-sensitive. He generates custom video explainers for his daughter's math homework because they are nearly free, and would not pay five dollars apiece for them.

America's ability to redeploy roughly 3 percent of GDP into a speculative venture almost overnight strikes him as deep innovation in itself, and strikingly clean next to the fraud of the railroad era. His pet theory for American dominance starts with self-selection: emigrating was dangerous, risk tolerance is partly heritable, and the culture and institutions that grew from that population, sensible bankruptcy law, venture capital, tolerance of failure, keep reinforcing one another. On interest rates, part of the story is simple arithmetic: a society building for the future has to pay people to consume less today, and the plumber building a data center is not producing today's consumption. And he doubts that agents will replace price itself, which he calls a miracle, a signal wrapped in an incentive.

Agents will, however, change how prices behave. Decisions sit on a spectrum. At one end is an FDIC-insured deposit rate, which he would delegate to an agent entirely; at the other is choosing a favorite sweater, where judgment cannot be delegated. Waste collection sits near the delegable end: agents can negotiate switching street by street, the low-cost producer gains density and gets cheaper still, and prices that once crept up once a year start moving daily. Aggregation economics also weakens when an agent reads every search result instead of the first one, and cheap coordination may permit full-stack businesses, pet care running from food to hospitals to insurance, that today's complexity forbids. Whether aggregate margins rise or fall under all this, he can argue both ways.

For investors themselves, he expects a freestyle chess era: humans working with models beat either one alone, because people still hold knowledge of the world that never made it into the training data. AI will encroach from short time horizons upward, and one day adding human judgment to a model's forecast may look like second-guessing the seven-day weather forecast. O'Shaughnessy describes a friend who picks the stocks himself but hands portfolio construction to an AI harness, and is drastically outperforming his own sizing. Sosin, whose portfolio holds roughly five positions with about 80 percent in Carvana and a small stake in Meta, jokes that he must be underweight: it should perhaps be 90. His defense of concentration is that diversification research is written for people seeking average results, and that owners of truly differentiated businesses, the early Walmarts and Home Depots, were never diversified, though he concedes the history books suffer survivorship bias.

His operating advice to the companies he owns starts with vibe coding for everyone, the board and the chief executive included; Carvana calls its hackathons pizza parties, and the point is to reset what people believe can be done. Next the chief executive demands read-only agent access to every system in the company, anything a person could look up becoming available in a prompt. Write access and change processes follow, permission by permission, percolating downward. Each step sounds like a bullet point and is years of impossibly hard work, so the real question for any incumbent is whether its business is good enough to buy that time before a clean-sheet competitor meets it halfway. It is the best time ever to start a company, he says, until tomorrow, though enduring economics still require a wedge, and the great outcomes tend to be the ones that look as if they make no sense.

On inequality he has two hands again: cheap intelligence smokes the economy's small rent-takers, the niche software charging every bowling alley, and compresses the rents of the professional class, which flattens outcomes; or it multiplies the reach of the smart and ambitious, which steepens them. Education is where he lands most firmly. Personal intelligence is not made useless by infinite machine intelligence, because directing a model takes crystallized knowledge of your own: you cannot have a deep conversation about a stochastic process you have never heard of. So children should learn the foundations away from the models, multiplication before matrix multiplication, not because they will compute by hand but because they must understand what the model is doing well enough to direct it. And schools should get far more ambitious at the same time, setting work a hundred times harder, done with models at full stretch, and making students defend the result and own it.

Open questions

  1. If demand for intelligence is really diamond-shaped, vast for the good-enough kind and thin for the elite kind, what return can the frontier labs earn on the largest capital buildout in history, and who absorbs the overbuild if the cost per token collapses first?
  2. Sosin argues that agents erase the customer lethargy many businesses quietly monetize. How much of today's corporate margin is rent on inattention, and what happens to prices and margins when agents renegotiate everything, every day?
  3. The freestyle chess era in investing rests on humans holding knowledge the models lack. How would anyone know that era has ended, and what is the investor's job the day after it does?

Starlink on every plane and the business of running United

The airline CEO putting Starlink on every plane

On Cheeky Pint, the episode "The airline CEO putting Starlink on every plane" is a conversation between host John Collison, joined by Ben Thompson, and Scott Kirby, the chief executive of United Airlines. Kirby took over United in May 2020, at the height of the pandemic, and the conversation ranges across what he did next: the largest aircraft order in history placed while rivals shrank, a fleetwide Starlink rollout he treats as a once-in-a-generation advantage, air traffic control as the industry's true bottleneck, route discipline, and the weekend coding habit that changed how he thinks about software.

The founding bet came during COVID. Every other global long-haul airline permanently retired part of its wide-body fleet; United ordered 200 wide-bodies, then several hundred narrow-bodies, for airplanes that list at around $400 million each. Kirby says the logic was asymmetric. Because everyone else was shrinking, United did not have to be right, only not totally wrong, and as the only buyer in the world it also won pricing nobody else could get.

Kirby distrusts the machinery most companies use to see the future. Experts, he argues, force everything inside two standard deviations, while value is created in the tails, and he calls Value at Risk the dumbest model in history for assuming the world is normally distributed. Surveys he treats as useful for how people felt about something already done and useless for what they will do next: during COVID, terrified survey respondents said they would never travel internationally again.

During the same period, United spent several hundred million dollars moving off mainframes onto modern cloud databases, a project with no calculable return on investment. Kirby argues the payoff is speed. United owns its own reservation system, which he says no other airline does, and it is now taking the seatback entertainment software in-house from Panasonic, so screens can know who the passenger is, open on the map that passenger prefers, and mark moments such as a traveler crossing two million miles.

His AI epiphany, eight months before the conversation, was about the power of programming rather than summaries. After three one-hour tutoring sessions, Kirby spent a Saturday morning in his home gym writing, between sets, a program that pulled odds from four sportsbooks and placed a bet with whichever offered the best price. If a chief executive can build that himself in two and a half hours, he concluded, the company should be far more ambitious about software, and United went all in.

That ambition is wrapped in a culture instruction. Kirby tells his officers their job is one word: wow. Each surprise a loyal customer banks becomes something United owes them again. He rejects expectation management as an excuse: the job is to communicate well and to reduce the problem itself. When Chicago passengers were told of weather delays under a sunny sky, United sent half of them a radar picture showing the line of thunderstorms closing the routes. Those passengers rated the experience plus 60 on a net promoter basis against negative 60 for the standard message, a 120 point swing from a picture. AI simulations of Denver's pop-up thunderstorms, he says, could cut summer weather delays there by 50 to 75 percent.

Starlink is the centerpiece bet. United is installing it at about three airplanes a day, starting with the regional jets that were certified first. Roughly 48 percent of flights have it as they speak, crossing 50 percent within days, with Los Angeles set to become the first fully covered hub in the first half of next year and the 787 fleet waiting on Boeing certification work. Kirby is blunt about the pitch: free was never the value proposition, fast is, and the free legacy systems at rival airlines are so starved of bandwidth that he compares them to dial-up internet. He calls Delta's failure to move first a mistake, and expects a two to three year window in which travelers try United for the Wi-Fi and stay because the rest of the technology is better.

The industry's biggest limiter, in his account, is not airplanes or airports but air traffic control, which he calls 90 percent of the issue. Some flights take longer today than they did 50 years ago despite faster planes. He blames an annual appropriations process that cannot run five to ten year capital projects, facilities scattered across congressional districts for political reasons, and a funding fight in which the airlines pay the system's fees while private jets fly nearly free and lobby against change. The caps have already arrived: the FAA limits flights in New York, and limits reached Chicago this summer.

Network math sits behind his fight with American Airlines at Chicago. A hub burns hundreds of scarce flight slots on short catchment routes, such as the eight United flights a day from Chicago to Milwaukee, whose passengers are mostly connecting onward. Two airlines sharing a hub duplicate that waste; single-hub Atlanta and Dallas, smaller cities, sustain more international service. American, focused on the Sun Belt, failed to rebuild its Chicago schedule and lost six gates to United under the airport's reallocation rule. Kirby says American will lose about a billion and a half dollars in Chicago this year, while United has moved from trailing to roughly double American's revenue share there.

For Kirby, discipline in the airline business means routes. United flies into its hubs rather than point to point, because a route without connecting feed competes for only a quarter of its market. His advice to new airline chief executives is to start with the route network, and to cancel any money-losing route that people defend as strategic. The creativity runs the other way as well: 53 transatlantic destinations by next summer, up from 23 a decade ago, including Nuuk in Greenland and a pair of Spanish and Portuguese pilgrimage cities added together after his team spotted broken itineraries in the data. Only two additions in ten years failed, and both were cut.

Low-cost carriers, he argues, broke their own model. Spirit's average fare was $58 while the New York airports alone charged $52 per passenger, before fuel, crews, or airplanes, and airport costs across the big US airports are up roughly 70 percent since before COVID. Ryanair, the one durable success, flies to cheap secondary airports and refuses distractions; its American counterparts abandoned that discipline for expensive big-city airports just as United's basic economy fares and larger planes erased their price advantage. Six of ten US airlines will lose money this year, Kirby says, and the two winners, United and Delta, win on brand loyalty rather than price alone.

He is impatient with the hardware itself. The Boeing and Airbus duopoly, he claims, has quietly settled into not investing in a genuinely new airframe, and he has spent 20 years begging both for a 757 replacement. He points instead to blended wing bodies, through United's investment in JetZero, and to products such as Relax Row, three coach seats that convert into a bed. His larger ambition is a United that Americans think of the way Dubai thinks of Emirates: a flag carrier that can meet state-subsidized rivals head to head, in a market where two-thirds of long-haul seats into the United States are flown by foreign airlines.

The personal details match the argument. Kirby eats most meals with a serving spoon because it is faster and keeps the best bits from falling off a salad, limits meetings to two or three hours a day, avoids social media and television, and sleeps eight and a half hours a night. He once counted cards seriously enough to be barred from blackjack at the Bellagio, and gave up short-changing his sleep at the tables when fatigue started costing him the count.

Open questions

  1. United's Starlink lead depends on rivals staying a generation behind on connectivity. When every large airline offers fast satellite Wi-Fi, does the loyalty Kirby expects to convert during the window persist, or does the contest reset to schedule and service?
  2. Kirby says air traffic control is 90 percent of the industry's constraint and doubts government can modernize it at commercial speed. If flight caps keep spreading beyond New York and Chicago, which parts of United's growth plan survive a permanently capped system?
  3. His route doctrine says to cancel any money-losing route defended as strategic, yet his technology strategy was a several-hundred-million-dollar investment with, by his own account, no provable return. Where exactly does he draw the line between a bet and an excuse?

The AI vulnerability tsunami and software supply chain defense

AI Vulnerability Tsunami | First Pass Ep. 13 with Dan Lorenc (Chainguard)

On Altimeter Capital, the First Pass episode "AI Vulnerability Tsunami | First Pass Ep. 13 with Dan Lorenc (Chainguard)" is a conversation between hosts Jamin Ball and Palak Goel and Dan Lorenc, cofounder and chief executive of Chainguard. It was recorded at the end of what Lorenc calls one of the worst weeks ever for software and internet security, a week when five major security events landed at once and some people were calling for the internet to be turned off.

Chainguard's job, as Lorenc describes it, is to build a safe source for open source software. Open source makes up somewhere between 90 and 95 percent of the code running everywhere, on phones, in ATMs and on cruise ships. It is better than any alternative by his account, but because the whole world is built on the same foundation, a bug in it creates concentration risk: a flaw in one vendor's product hurts that vendor, while a flaw in the shared foundation hurts every vendor at once.

The week's headline event was a set of critical vulnerabilities in PHP, the programming language behind Facebook and, by Lorenc's estimate, roughly 70 percent of the internet. The flaws sat in the code for about ten years. Several research groups found them independently around the same time in August, then worked quietly with the maintainers on fixes ahead of a scheduled disclosure. The bugs allow remote code execution on an internet-facing server running PHP, close to the worst outcome a vulnerability can have, though the exploit itself is complex and has preconditions.

What has changed, Lorenc argues, is the race that follows a disclosure. Defenders used to have days or weeks to roll out a patch; now they have minutes, sometimes less. The mean time to exploit has gone negative this year, meaning attacks begin before a fix is public. Amazon found attacks against this flaw in its logs starting up to four days before the patches were available, and Cloudflare saw them a day or two before. Compared with famous earlier flaws such as Heartbleed and Log4Shell, this one is harder to exploit, but frontier models have made sophisticated attacks accessible, which rewrites the old calculus.

That shift is the episode's core. Vulnerabilities are scored from zero to ten, and turning a middling six into a critical ten used to take scarce expert labor, so nobody knew how many sixes could become tens. Now anyone with a capable model can try. Lorenc gives his own example: the night before he was briefed on the embargoed PHP flaw, he pointed a frontier model at PHP and asked it to find a vulnerability, and by the next morning it had produced a working proof of concept for the same bug. His team, and teams everywhere, are finding hundreds more like it.

His advice to enterprises comes in two parts. First, patching has to become fast and boring, because flaws like this will arrive daily and nobody can panic every time. Defense in depth buys the time to patch, as when Cloudflare wrote a new firewall rule that week to block the attack. Second, companies have to get off the patching treadmill entirely. Lorenc frames the moment as thirty years of tech debt coming due at once, the highest stakes and fastest modernization effort anyone has ever had to do, and points to formal verification, which Anthropic has written about, as a way to eliminate whole classes of vulnerabilities at scale instead of chasing each one.

Open questions

  1. If attacks now begin before patches exist, what replaces the patch cycle as the main line of defense, and can approaches like formal verification be rolled out fast enough to matter?
  2. Nobody knows how many mid-severity flaws already sitting in widely used open source code could be turned critical by a capable model. Who should be counting them, and how?
  3. The window between disclosure and exploitation is now measured in minutes. What are smaller companies without dedicated security teams supposed to do when even large ones cannot move that fast?
