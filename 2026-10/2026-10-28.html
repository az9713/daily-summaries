Matthew Berman · DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, tested hands-on

Deepseek crushed everyone

On Matthew Berman, the episode "Deepseek crushed everyone" is a hands-on tour of DeepSeek's new V4.1 Flash model, which Berman says flew under the radar and is, in his judgment, the best open-source model on the planet. Rather than reading benchmark tables first, he spends most of the video making the model build things: a 3D watch, two games and simulations, a full spreadsheet, and a complete desktop app. His verdict at the end is that this is potentially the best overall model available right now, not because it tops every quality chart, but because its quality sits close to the frontier while its cost and speed are in a different league.

The first test combines 3D generation, spatial awareness, and knowledge stored in the model's weights: build a hyperrealistic 3D mechanical watch diagram in a single web page, with hover text naming each part and explaining what it does. The result, which the model calls Mechanica, impressed him with its working, correctly fitted parts, its lighting and shadows, and its labels. Hovering over the hairspring identifies it and describes how it sets the watch's rhythm, clicking zooms in for a closer look, and an explode button pulls the whole movement apart layer by layer while the labels keep working. His one disappointment is an omission: the back of the watch is missing the little weight that winds the movement as the wearer's wrist moves.

Next he asks for a clone of the multiplayer game Fall Guys, a test he has run on many models. The result, Bean Blitz, is built in plain HTML, and he finds the 3D generation genuinely good, with working hammers that knock his character off the course and a dive move on the space bar. Some objects clip through each other and a few obstacles that look animated do not actually move, and he rates the clone close to, but not quite as good as, what Opus 5.5 produced, at a fraction of the price and much faster.

The third test asks the model to research a product on the web and recreate it in 3D: the folding iPhone Duo. Berman calls the result flawless, with a perfect Apple logo, light reflecting off the screen, a matte finish on the inner display, and the buttons and hinge in the right places. He puts the same test from Moonshot's new Kimi model on screen next to it, and the comparison is not close. The only flaw he finds is in the folding animation, which briefly sends the device off screen and drops the screen image for a moment mid-fold.

A sponsor segment for CoreWeave's Arena program follows, showing load testing of an AI app before production launch, ramping from one request to ten and using an assistant to diagnose why response times rose.

The model is also multimodal, so he tests its visual understanding on a photograph of a pastry counter and asks it to count every item. Working at maximum reasoning, it took four minutes and twenty-five seconds and tallied 137 individual pastries, naming each kind as it went: butter croissants, cream-filled horn croissants, and row after row of macarons. Its count of 24 orange macarons in a pyramid was one short of the 25 Berman could see, close enough that he calls it arguable. More striking to him, the model volunteered, unprompted, that the macarons, croissants, and brownies were stacked, so some items were partly or fully hidden, and it put a defensible range for the whole counter at roughly 130 to 145.

When he then asked it to estimate the hidden items too, the follow-up took only twenty-three seconds, because the spatial reasoning was already done. The model treated the macaron pyramid as a cone-shaped heap, five to six wide at the base and five to six layers tall, and concluded that 45 macarons in total was far more likely than the 24 visible on the front face. It reasoned the same way through the croissant piles, judging them two to three layers deep. Berman is openly delighted: the model understood what was not shown in the image nearly as well as what was.

Speed is the next exhibit. With reasoning effort turned off, he has the model write a thousand-word story directly in the chat window, and it streams at roughly 125 to 150 tokens per second even in that interface. On the Artificial Analysis figures he shows, third-party providers serve the model far faster, up to 1,286 tokens per second at the top end, with Baseten at 845 tokens per second.

He then tests the model as an agent: browse YouTube, understand his interview with Sam Altman, and extract the five most clippable moments with exact timestamps and a reason for each pick. It worked for six minutes and twenty-four seconds, and because the model is open weights, he could inspect its chain of thought and every tool call along the way. Its first pick was Altman saying he has not looked at a line of code in about a year, with the joke that doing so again would feel like a Revolutionary War reenactment, and its reasoning, that the CEO of OpenAI admitting this is newsworthy and the image travels well, matched Berman's own judgment. The other picks, including "coders have never been busier" and the exchange about the AI consciousness debate, were all clips he says he would have chosen himself. The obvious next step, having the model download the video and cut the clips itself, he leaves for another video.

Two builds run entirely on his own hardware. He loaded the model locally on a DGX station and asked it to build an Excel clone for the browser; after fifty minutes of work it produced GridLine, an offline-first spreadsheet where formulas calculate, fill down across dragged cells, and generate charts, short of the full Excel feature set but very good. A deliberately simple prompt for a 3D Rubik's cube that can scramble and solve itself also passed cleanly: the colors stayed on their correct sides through the scramble, the move list was shown, and the solve rotations looked physically right.

The final build is personal. Berman argues that everyone should now be building their own software for the small tools they need, and shows a PDF reader he wanted for Windows because he finds Adobe Acrobat bloated and spammy. Opus 5.5 wrote a detailed prompt, specifying an Electron, React, and TypeScript app called Folio, and DeepSeek, on its high thinking setting, built it: page navigation, highlighting, drawing in multiple colors, text notes, signatures you can draw, upload, or capture by photographing one on paper, and a dark mode that renders any PDF in dark colors. Run locally it cost him nothing; through a paid provider, he estimates, an app like this costs less than a couple of dollars to create and is yours for life.

The benchmarks he closes with support the demos. On a quality-versus-cost frontier chart, the model sits alone in the top-left corner, better and cheaper than Opus 5, Fable 5, GPT 6.1 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and GLM 5.3 Max. It scores 42 on Humanity's Last Exam, level with Kimi K3 and GLM 5.3, and 42 on Terminal Bench. His favorite metric combines price with how many tokens a task takes: cost per completed intelligence task. The priciest models on his chart run to hundreds of dollars per task; this model completes one for twenty-six cents. Provider pricing matches that story, at sixty cents per million output tokens and fifteen cents per million input tokens from the cheapest listing he shows, with cache hits free, and Baseten, which he calls the best overall offering, at a dollar twenty per million output tokens. He acknowledges that Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro scored higher overall in the benchmarks, and then says he personally thinks DeepSeek is much better, and invites viewers to watch his Kimi video and make the comparison themselves.

Open questions

  1. Berman's verdict rests on his own test set, which leans heavily on 3D generation, browser-built apps, and short, well-specified tasks. Does V4.1 Flash hold up as well on the long, messy, ambiguous work where the frontier models earn their much higher prices?
  2. At twenty-six cents per completed task and inference above a thousand tokens per second, the economics of the closed labs look exposed. If open models this capable keep arriving every few months, what exactly are the frontier labs charging for?
  3. The pastry test showed the model reasoning confidently about objects hidden from view, and being roughly right. Where is the line between that kind of spatial inference and simply inventing detail that is not there, and how would a user tell the difference?

Stark and European defense sovereignty

European Defense Sovereignty Starts in the Factory: Stark’s Uwe Horstmann and Johannes Schaback

On Sequoia Capital, the episode "European Defense Sovereignty Starts in the Factory: Stark’s Uwe Horstmann and Johannes Schaback" is a conversation between Sequoia partners Pat Grady and Luciana Lixandru and the two co-founders of Stark, a German defense company that builds autonomous strike drones and other unmanned systems. Uwe Horstmann came to the company from venture capital and defense investing, with a military background of his own; Johannes Schaback leads its engineering. Two years after founding, Stark has about 700 employees and, by the hosts' account, billions of dollars of contracts landed within its first eighteen months. The argument running through the hour is the one in the title: sovereignty is not only a matter of buying weapons, it is a matter of being able to manufacture them at scale.

Horstmann opens with the shift that made the company possible. Europe has absorbed the message, repeated for years from Washington, that it must take more responsibility for its own defense, and it has understood that peace is kept by deterrence rather than assumed as a default. At the same time, the war in Ukraine showed that the battlefield itself changed: the new systems are software driven and AI driven, relatively inexpensive, and built at small scale. Governments responded by buying to fill capability gaps and by reforming how they buy, because, as Horstmann puts it, you do not procure a drone the way you procure a battleship.

Stark's playbook has four parts. First, innovate close to the battlefield: the company keeps a large team in Ukraine, embedded with the units that actually fly its systems. Second, be venture first, funding product development itself instead of waiting on government research money. Third, design products to be genuinely usable by NATO forces, carrying them all the way through certification, validation, and safety rather than stopping at a clever prototype. Fourth, design for industrial scaling from the first sketch, so the products are not a few exquisite systems standing around somewhere, because production itself is deterrence.

The sharpest version of the first principle is a distinction between the buyer and the customer. The buyer is a senior official; the customer is often a staff sergeant on a tactical level who has to operate the system, and Stark treats that soldier's time and feedback as the most valuable input it gets. Schaback tells the story of arriving with a beautiful, lightweight plastic transport box and being asked why it was not made of wood, since wood can at least be burned to keep soldiers warm in winter. Nobody in a lab thinks of that. The same user-first instinct drives the open questions the company is still working through: how a soldier supervises a drone swarm on a small screen, whether the controls work with gloves on, and how much information a person can process at once before the mental load becomes the limiting factor.

The company runs ten to fifteen programs at any time, organized almost like a venture portfolio, with autonomous teams per program and a shared platform layer of capability teams underneath, covering things like navigation where satellite signals are jammed or denied, target recognition, communication links, and flight control. The flagship product is a strike drone, in effect a software driven munition, and the capability the founders are proudest of is accuracy: sub-meter precision against targets moving at sixty kilometers an hour, which Schaback says took an unusually tight integration of hardware and software, with compute and memory budgets tuned exactly to the airframe.

The episode's centerpiece story is a failure. About a year ago Stark brought a major hardware and software upgrade of one of its systems to an important German army experiment before it had been fully tested, partly because an upgrade had shifted things like the center of gravity and a camera angle, and the test went badly in public. The response became what Schaback calls a forging moment. The whole engineering leadership decamped to a spa hotel near the shooting range, worked three shifts around the clock in November cold, flew, collected data, retrained and retuned, even rebuilt systems from scrapped debris to keep shooting, and used AI-trained flight dynamics models to sharpen their simulation in parallel. They set one exit condition: nobody leaves until the system lands three consecutive spot-on strikes. Within two weeks it did, and, Schaback says, it has hit ever since.

On procurement reform, Horstmann argues the changes will last precisely because they were not driven by a charismatic leader with a narrative, which he says is not how Europe works. They came from reality kicking in, from structural change, and from a lesson he states plainly: the real learning from the war is not any particular drone but that an innovation ecosystem is the actual wonder weapon, and the West has to set itself up to keep innovating, across soldier feedback, talent, and capital.

That ecosystem argument extends to how Stark builds. Engineering shifts left on manufacturability, choosing components that are very good and, just as importantly, procurable in large numbers rather than the single best camera on the market. Operational experts touring their factory recently remarked that a system looked about as complex as a washing machine, which Horstmann counts as praise: scaling a washing machine line from a thousand a month to ten thousand is hard but doable, and the established primes, for all their skill at exquisite and expensive systems, are not built for that game. The company balances vertical integration against a deliberate openness to local suppliers, because customers in other countries want production rooted at home, and against a supply chain problem nobody in the episode pretends is solved: parts of the Western defense industry still depend on China for raw chemical inputs, and fixing that will take coordination beyond any one company.

Autonomy gets its own guardrails discussion, prompted by the recent Hugging Face agent incident. Stark says it has followed NATO's principles for responsible use of AI from the beginning: the operator sets the intent, modeled as mission elements like no-fly zones and a kill zone, and hard-coded constraints keep the system inside that intent at every stage, for example refusing to arm over an inhabited area, with safeguard layers borrowed in part from functional safety practice in autonomous driving. Whether a strike then completes fully autonomously or waits for a human confirmation is the customer's choice. AI also shows up before takeoff, in a ground control system the founders call Minerva, which decides how many systems to send against an incoming target and plans the most cost-effective mission.

The scale figures come late and they are striking. Stark intends to invest on the order of 600 million euros over the next one to three years in research, development, and production capacity, which Horstmann notes is not far off what the big European primes spend on core research once government-funded work is subtracted. The headcount went from zero to about 700 in two years, most of them engineers. And the problem Horstmann says he most wants to solve next is not a product at all but a contract: a design, closer to a cloud contract with surge built in, that pre-arranges the stockpiles, the stored components, the weekly software updates for systems sitting in depots, and the manufacturing capacity that spins up if a hot conflict comes. Nobody in the West has figured that out yet, he says, and getting it done is where real deterrence would come from. The mission he states for the company is to become one of the pillars of the European and Western defense architecture and, just as much, an orchestrator of the ecosystem around it, in the hope that a stronger Europe becomes a better partner to the United States in more peaceful times.

Open questions

  1. Stark's feedback loop runs on a live war and on procurement reform born of emergency. If the shooting stops and budgets cool, do the embedded teams, the fast certification, and the years-to-months buying cycles survive peacetime, or were they wartime exceptions?
  2. The company's edge is startup speed and designs a washing machine could be proud of, aimed squarely at the inexpensive end of the market. As it grows past 700 people and spends at prime-like scale, can it keep the culture that is supposed to be its advantage, and will the primes simply copy the playbook with more capital?
  3. The surge contract idea asks governments to pay in peacetime for idle capacity, rotating stockpiles, and software that must be refreshed weekly. Who carries that cost and risk, and can such a contract be designed before a crisis, rather than improvised during one?

Ackman on permanent capital

Bill Ackman: Is Permanent Capital an Investor’s Biggest Edge? | Podcast | In Good Company

On In Good Company, the podcast of Norges Bank Investment Management, host Nicolai Tangen, who runs the Norwegian sovereign wealth fund, sits down in New York with Bill Ackman, the founder of Pershing Square. The conversation circles the question in the episode's title: whether permanent capital, money that cannot be pulled away in a panic, is the biggest edge an investor can have. Ackman's answer, built up over nearly an hour, is that it comes close, because almost everything else in his method, the concentration, the patience, the indifference to quarterly noise, depends on it.

He starts with what a great company is: a business you could buy today and still know, with enormous certainty, would be enormously valuable in ten or twenty years even if the markets shut in the meantime. The tell is structure rather than sector. He loves businesses that work like royalty collectors, annuity-like streams of cash that grow at a high rate for a very long time and do not need their cash reinvested to survive. He guesses there are only a couple of hundred to three hundred really great big businesses in the world, and they seldom become available at prices that make them great investments, which is why Pershing Square owns so few names and writes such large checks when one does.

That discipline is written down. Pershing Square's core principles exist as a checklist, which the team had engraved on what was meant to be a stone tablet and came back as heavy plastic. The heart of it is predictability: the value of a financial asset is the present value of the cash it will generate over its life, so if you cannot model a business ten, fifteen, or twenty years out, you cannot value it and should not own it. Around that sit durability, the proverbial moat, and a refusal to take risks the firm cannot control: no commodity-sensitive, interest-rate-sensitive, or regulatory-sensitive businesses.

The firm itself is built to match. Pershing Square has 48 people and nine investment professionals, one portfolio of roughly twelve to fourteen investments, and no individual profit-and-loss accounts, so nobody is paid for getting their own ideas funded. Ryan Israel has been CIO for four years. Ideas surface from anyone on the team, a two-person crew goes deep with oversight from Ackman or Israel, and the whole team, including people who have done no work on the name and have nothing invested in it, forms the view. Objections trigger more diligence rather than a vote, and Ackman says he cannot think of an investment in the last decade the team did not collectively believe in. The Brookfield position began with the relationship he built with Bruce Flatt during the General Growth bankruptcy and still took months of diligence once Brookfield had become a more asset-light, royalty-like business.

His information diet is part of the method. He reads the Wall Street Journal, the Financial Times, and The Economist, but calls X probably his most important source, because he can follow the strongest voices on both sides of the same question and, he says, feel a week or two ahead of conventional media. Some of his best ideas came from personal passions rather than research assignments: an interest in health had him watching COVID emerge in January 2020 before it was an investment question at all.

Why, then, do so few investors copy a style that Buffett, Chris Hohn, and Ackman all describe openly? His answer is structural. Many investors want to get rich quick, and most of the industry's capital can be redeemed at short notice, which makes genuinely long-term thinking almost impossible for the person managing it. Leveraged, short-horizon firms like Citadel and Millennium cannot absorb the volatility a concentrated portfolio produces. Permanent capital is scarce precisely because it has to be cultivated, and he calls the ability to be a long-term investor in a short-term world, time arbitrage, his firm's real advantage. It let Pershing Square deploy aggressively during COVID without worrying about end-of-month redemptions.

The Buffett template is explicit. Buffett ran a hedge fund in 1969 with the right to take 25 percent of profits above a 6 percent return, gave that up, handed capital back, and took control of a struggling textile business for a $100,000 salary and no incentive fee, because redeemable capital would impair his ability to compound. Ackman's own version of the textile mill is Howard Hughes, a good real estate business to which the market assigns a high cost of capital. The plan copies Berkshire's sequence: harvest cash from the real estate operation, make it asset-light, and rebuild the company around an insurance operation whose assets Pershing Square will manage. In a decade, he says, Howard Hughes should be more than 80 percent insurance company and maybe 20 percent real estate. The insurance team is deliberately oversized for a company with not quite $2 billion of capital: Mark Grandisson, who ran Arch Capital Group, his protege David Gansberg, and Lucy Fato, a former vice chairman and general counsel of AIG. When the insurance deal closed in June, they sold the intermediate and longer-term bonds, put the float in short-term Treasuries, and invested the surplus in a portfolio of common stocks. He is at pains to separate this from Apollo, Brookfield, and KKR: those firms, in his telling, use insurers as low-cost financing for levered credit spreads, while his is a specialty property and casualty insurer that takes real insurance risk at much lower leverage, the way Buffett's insurers did on the way to returns on equity above 20 percent.

On artificial intelligence he has the contrarian's version of the story. AI is making market participants shorter term, since a filing can now be read instantly and huge pools of capital hunt for momentary edges, and that short-termism is a gift to a patient buyer. Pershing Square bought Amazon after the liberation tariffs were announced, and Meta and Microsoft after each disappointed the market by committing to enormous capital spending to meet demand; those names are now about a third of the portfolio. Intelligence itself, he argues, is becoming a low-cost commodity for almost everyone, and what remains distinctly human is creativity, the kind of leap no amount of reading the past would have produced from Einstein's predecessors. He expects an explosion of entrepreneurship and growth to follow.

Activism, the strategy that made his name, has changed character around him. The last campaign he launched was in 2016, and where a stake once earned him silence, he now gets a thank-you note from the CEO. Indexation has shrunk floats and leveraged short-term capital has grown, so a high-quality company that disappoints can fall 30 or 40 percent instead of three or four, which fills his portfolio with well-run businesses available at discounts without any fight. The fights he does remember are vivid. In 1998 he sold Carl Icahn a stake in Hallwood Realty Partners with what Icahn called schmuck insurance, a promise of half of Icahn's profit above a threshold if he sold within three years. Icahn sold, owed about $4.5 million, refused to pay, and stretched the litigation through appeals for six or seven years while interest ran at 9 percent. Ackman won, told Icahn so in strong words, and Icahn later squeezed Pershing Square's short position in Herbalife, a fight that cost Ackman about a billion dollars. On the decline of shareholder rights he lands in a nuanced place: founder control can be a genuine asset when the founder is a Buffett, a Walton, or a Musk, but he prefers one share, one vote, generally avoids controlled companies, and worries about control passing to a founder's children.

The personal passages carry the same find-the-other-side philosophy. His test for conviction, in investing and everything else, is to find the best short seller of something he owns and rebut each of their arguments one by one. That instinct drove his public fight over Harvard after October 7, which he tells as a free-speech and meritocracy story, and it shapes how he uses X, where he follows about a thousand accounts and looks for the most articulate person on each side of an issue. He also speaks about the hardest year of his life: his 26-year-old daughter suffered a brain hemorrhage from an arteriovenous malformation and lay undiscovered for almost nineteen hours. She is relearning to speak and walk, though pressure on the brain cost her most of her vision. The experience turned him into an optimist about brain-computer interfaces and AI-driven rehabilitation, and his firm has assembled a 3.4-acre New York site with a 400,000-square-foot biotech building for a brain-recovery institute he is now staffing.

He closes with the book that shaped him, John Rawls's A Theory of Justice, and its veil of ignorance: design the world before you know whether you will be born in the Bronx or on the Upper East Side. His advice to young people follows from the whole conversation. Choose your circle carefully, because you become like the people closest to you. Read voraciously, the way a model is trained, and follow your genuine interests. Guard your health, sleep, lift weights, go easy on alcohol, and treat sugar as poison. And take character as a decision rather than a gift: showing up on time, answering people, and doing the right thing when nobody is watching are available to anyone tomorrow, which is more than can be said for being Michael Jordan.

Open questions

  1. Ackman argues permanent capital is scarce because most money can leave at short notice. If structures offering it multiply, from listed permanent vehicles to insurer tie-ups like Howard Hughes, does the edge survive its own popularity, or is scarcity the edge?
  2. The Howard Hughes plan copies Buffett's insurance playbook starting from not quite $2 billion of insurance capital and a recruited team sized for a far bigger company. Can that team compound the way Berkshire's insurers did without Berkshire's scale, deal flow, and sixty-year head start?
  3. He says AI is making markets shorter term, and that this is exactly what creates his buying opportunities when great companies fall 30 or 40 percent on a disappointment. If AI also makes every other investor faster at spotting the same overreactions, how long does the window he depends on stay open?

Lütke on agents and Shopify

Tobi Lütke on the AI agent revolution and future of Shopify

On Sources Podcast, the episode "Tobi Lütke on the AI agent revolution and future of Shopify" is a conversation between host Alex Heath and Tobi Lütke, the co-founder and chief executive of Shopify, recorded at Shopify's Toronto office. It opens with a news contrast: Amazon recently blocked Anthropic's Muse internally while Shopify publicly embraced it. Lütke says he posted about embracing Muse before learning of Amazon's move, and he treats the split not as a surprise but as the latest expression of a twenty-year difference in philosophy between the two companies.

In his telling, Amazon is the digitization of a department store, an aggregator whose strength is depth of inventory rather than distinction or storytelling, and one with a large advertising business to protect. Shopify was built, deliberately, to sit on the same side of the table as its merchants up and down the stack. That is why it has never built a marketplace: a marketplace is three-sided, and when buyer and seller interests collide, the operator ends up optimizing for buyers. Applied to agents, the stance is simple. Shopify wants its checkout to work well, and it does not care whether the buyer at the end of it is a person or an agent acting for one, so it is building the APIs and partnering broadly across the agent ecosystem.

Asked how agents are already changing shopping, Lütke says the volume is growing fast from a low base, and that the interesting shift is in the kind of purchase. Agent orders skew toward projects: help me set up a home gym, a request that may begin with a photo of a room or a floor plan and ends in a considered, high-value order. His favorite example is a weekend project with his kids, where an agent figures out everything a small electronics build needs, magnets, filament, parts, and places the orders with local stores so it all arrives in time. Agents connected to a home network can already reorder printer filament when it runs out. His read of the phase we are in: agents are mostly helping people do what they already do, only better, and the phase after that, discovering things that were not possible before, is where it gets fun.

The conversation turns to science fiction, a lifelong input for him. He recently reread Neal Stephenson's Cryptonomicon with his son and was struck that nearly everything in it, cryptocurrencies and fractional ownership included, has come to pass. But his larger point is that science fiction tells you more about the time it was written in than the time it describes: an atomic spider for the nuclear age. He blames a long run of dystopian fiction, commercially dominant for a couple of decades, for some of the technology industry's mopiness, and he hears optimism returning now for a concrete reason. For the first time, he says, we have software that we train rather than write, and then have to interview for its capabilities.

Optimism, in his account, is what a frontier does to people. Humans work best when there is opportunity around them; without a perceived frontier, the rational strategy becomes competing for what already exists, and zero-sum thinking takes over. Pessimism is the default because it sounds sophisticated, while optimism sounds naive, which is why it tends to be the preserve of the very high status and the slightly crazy. His economics run the other way. Value sits locked inside things until knowledge releases it: the world had floating-point math and matrix multiplication for a very long time before anyone extracted trillions of dollars of value from doing them much faster, in a particular way, and uranium was just a rock before fission. He sees the same creation daily in Shopify businesses that began as a lunchtime decision by someone dissatisfied with a job.

On the growing negative sentiment toward AI, Lütke thinks entrepreneurs may be the job category that benefits most, because starting a company requires understanding every job well enough to hire for it, and because the founders who historically succeeded were disproportionately those who had someone in their life who would answer an entrepreneurial question within a day. AI democratizes that advisor. He invokes the Promethean myth as a deep cultural reflex, the assumption that every good must carry harm of equal proportion, and rejects the arithmetic: AI is a lever, it will land unevenly, but his personal probability of doom is zero, because at the end of the day it is math. His definition of dystopia is forty years ago; teleport anyone back thirty years and the missing comforts would horrify them. Sentiment, he finds, differs by place. The United States leads in adoption and business formation, with a younger generation he calls extremely entrepreneurial. Europe is more cynical and often reasons from stale first impressions, and some of the newest tools are not even available there. Canada sits in between, America for Europeans as he calls it, and takes justified pride in Toronto's role in AI's foundations, from AlexNet to Geoffrey Hinton's lab keeping machine learning alive through its winter.

He has no wish to move Shopify to California, despite visiting monthly. Being an outsider who observes Silicon Valley from a distance and edits what he takes in is, he thinks, one of his core skills. Silicon Valley raw is a fast-swinging pendulum, and its company cultures are strikingly convergent: try finding a large company there that has escaped the OKR system. That gravity makes reinvention hard, and he prizes diversity among companies, distinct places built for distinct kinds of people.

His board seat at Meta gives him a second vantage point. He calls Mark Zuckerberg clearly one of the best entrepreneurs who has ever lived, and he is grateful for the decision to open source the Llama models, which set a norm other labs followed and without which his optimism would be slightly lower. The Llama 4 release was a setback, a public null result of the kind every lab has, and what impressed him was the speed of the response, reassembling a team that is now training remarkable models. The seat also reminds him of his own limits: he comes home thankful that Shopify is not a 70,000-person company, a scale he doubts he could lead.

Founder control, he argues, is an asset a hired manager cannot replicate, and his reflexive AI use memo is his example. He could simply require that everyone try AI first, and ask anyone requesting something of him how their AI attempt had failed, without a committee sanding the memo down first. It drew headlines outside the company and almost none inside, which he took as evidence the transition had already happened. A year and a half on, the habits are organic: a peer review question about showing up with an AI prototype, headcount requests that must first answer what AI cannot do. His own management runs without an annual plan. Instead he tries to teach everyone the same function, a way of weighing all the inputs and picking the next best step, loosely coupled, which he credits for the company's creativity. These days, he says, he sees the craziest thing of his life in a review about once a week.

On how the agent economy pays for itself, he is relaxed and a little fatalistic. The agent startups Heath describes all assume a take rate on transactions, and Lütke warns them to expect basis points rather than two percent, noting that most of Shopify's roughly three percent goes straight to the card networks. But agents are so valuable that monetization will find a shape, whether metering, transactions, or something else. Only about two percent of American households pay for AI today, while startup capital pours into compute, or, as Heath puts it, it is all going to Jensen. For Shopify itself he would rather take a lower percentage of far more activity: he optimizes for gross profit dollar growth, not margin, because the mission is diffusing entrepreneurship until it is a plan A career rather than a plan B. It remains his favorite statistic that the most common timestamp for a new Shopify signup is the local lunch break. Cheaper tools get used more, the Jevons paradox he sees merchants discovering, and the cost of starting has fallen below what most people spend on coffee in their first month.

The acute risk, as he frames it, is that someone builds a better Shopify. Every year he spends its opening trying to design exactly that, then spends the rest of the year closing the gap between the design and the real thing, because the sunk cost fallacy kills everyone. The standing tension is range: the same software must work perfectly for a lunch-break signup and for billion-dollar brands everyone has heard of, and an installed base can slow evolution. His answer is to arrive first at every new way of creating value for merchants, before two people in a garage with a data center of agents get there.

Advertising, the thing agents seem to threaten most, he treats as infrastructure worth saving. There still needs to be a discovery and growth engine, a market that connects a new product to its future thousand true fans, and he calls Meta's advertising engine the greatest boon to small businesses of all time, partly because paying to advertise is a filter: a business that can put money behind its product has usually made some. The trust problem is real, though. An assistant quietly paid to steer your choices would be a breach of trust, so the forms agent-era discovery takes, possibly built on now-mature blockchain primitives, are both a design problem and an enormous business opportunity.

The interview closes at home. Lütke has replaced his wine cellar with a server rack, a rack cellar as Heath jokes, holding a Dell machine built around Nvidia's GB300 that trains models locally, so his agent work never has to leave the house. He describes himself as an atoms minimalist but a digital archiving maximalist: he has logged most of his computing for about fifteen years, and when he set up his local agent it identified him from a Unix username and a Toronto geolocation before he introduced himself. The agent writes briefing notes he may or may not read, keeps his calendar and location, and digests his health data; cross-referencing heart-rate records, it told him which of his meetings are the most stressful, a finding he shared with the people involved, who found it less funny than he did. He could live without the thing, he says, but losing it would feel like one of the most significant regressions imaginable.

Open questions

  1. Lütke argues Shopify's merchant-side alignment turns the agentic web into a tailwind. If a few agent platforms consolidate and begin charging merchants for access to their own customers, does Shopify's open stance remain an advantage, or does it become the exposure?
  2. He expects agent monetization to settle in basis points while the money finds its own shape. Until more than a small share of households pay for AI directly, who actually captures the agent layer's margin: the agent startups, the labs selling compute, or the platforms that own checkout?
  3. His personal agent performs as well as it does partly because he has fifteen years of his own digital life archived to hand it. How much of that experience is available to people without such an archive, and what does the gap mean for who benefits from personal agents first?

The CEO agenda for the agentic age

The CEO’s Agenda for Thriving in the Agentic Age

On McKinsey & Company, the episode "The CEO’s Agenda for Thriving in the Agentic Age" is the 300th edition of the firm's Inside the Strategy Room podcast. Host Sean Brown is joined by four McKinsey experts: Tanguy Catlin, a senior partner and a director of the McKinsey Global Institute; Sandra Durth, a partner who leads the firm's thinking on the agentic organization; Lari Hämäläinen, a senior partner who leads the generative AI and agents work at QuantumBlack, McKinsey's AI practice; and Antoine Montard, who leads the strategy practice's research and insight center. Their shared argument is that generative and agentic AI amount to a true general purpose technology, and that capturing its value requires CEOs to work through three lenses at the same time: strategy, technology, and organization.

The conversation starts from a frustration the panel hears constantly. CEOs can see powerful signals, from semiconductor manufacturing moving onshore to breakthroughs in drug discovery, but they are surrounded by noise arriving very fast, and they struggle to make sense of it. At the micro level their questions are immediate: how will profit pools in their industry evolve, where will competitive advantage sit, how much should they invest and how fast, should they build the technology or partner, and how do they drive adoption while upskilling the workforce at scale? Two barriers keep the three lenses apart. Few executives yet understand the technology deeply enough to integrate them, and the leaders who own the pieces, the CEO, the CIO, the head of strategy, the head of HR, rarely spend quality time debating these questions together. The capability horizon of the tools is also moving roughly monthly, so what was true a year ago may no longer hold, which makes change management inside organizations of tens of thousands of people genuinely hard.

The technology lens opens with the sheer scale of investment. Catlin puts United States capital spending on data center infrastructure, hardware, and energy at about seven trillion dollars between 2026 and the end of 2030, five times the combined investment of the rest of the G6 and China together. Hämäläinen adds the energy consequence: data centers took about 2 percent of US energy last year, and projections put that at 10 to 13 percent by 2030, in an economy whose generation capacity has barely grown in thirty years. Managing the generation, transmission, and distribution buildout that implies is, in his words, a society-wide transformation.

On returns, Hämäläinen argues the investment has paid off so far, but not yet inside the enterprise. The first step function was quiet: the backbone of the consumer internet moved onto generative AI, so the recommendation and search systems people use daily now run on it, and that unseen demand is what utilizes the new capacity. The second step function is programming, which now accounts for about half of the generative AI workloads running in data centers and emerged in roughly the last six months. Whether enterprise work, in finance, HR, operations, and beyond, becomes the third step function is the open question. Meanwhile the inputs keep compounding: the compute poured into training frontier models rises four to five times a year, algorithmic efficiency improves about three times a year, fewer and larger models get trained, and AI systems have already surpassed most of the benchmarks built to compare them with humans.

Asked whether one model provider will take all, Hämäläinen doubts it. The performance gap between the leading models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google has halved in the last year, he says, and as raw capability converges, the real differences become behavioral, closer to the tonality of an employee you are hiring than to a specification sheet. His sharper warning is about dependence. A company with 100,000 employees today might, within about three years, employ 50,000 people alongside some 500,000 agents doing work, and the cost base of those agents could be twice the employee cost base. If all of that runs through one provider, someone else effectively controls your operating costs, which he calls an unacceptable position for any enterprise. Diversifying across models is a strategic decision, not a procurement detail.

Montard then brings the strategy lens and the long view of earlier general purpose technologies. Steam power took decades to move from pumping mines to reorganizing factories and supply chains, electricity paid off fully only once factories were redesigned around the electric motor and complementary inventions like the light bulb and refrigeration arrived, and Robert Solow could still quip in 1987 that the computer age was visible everywhere except the productivity statistics. Closer to home, e-commerce needed about twenty years to reach a tenth of retail sales, and autonomous vehicles remain costly and edge-case prone after a decade. The pattern repeats: the technology becomes available quickly, but the full economic impact takes a long time and a great deal of further innovation.

From that history he draws three waves of value creation. The first is productivity: automating tasks and workflows. Those gains mostly accrue to the tool makers and to consumers, rarely to the adopting company, because competitors get them too; productivity is table stakes you cannot skip, but not a source of durable advantage. The second wave is innovation, new products, services, and business models, from AI-discovered drugs to AI tutoring to real-time tailored offers and robotics, and this is where growth and value creation will be most acute, though much of it remains to be invented. The third is transaction costs. AI cuts the cost of searching, evaluating, contracting, and switching suppliers, which puts business models built on aggregation, brokering, and intermediation under pressure and moves the control points in value chains. Catlin underlines the point: because your competitors have access to the same models and will chase the same efficiencies, productivity is where you lose the battle, not where you win it.

The clearest evidence in the episode comes from software development. Hämäläinen describes client development teams that were benchmarked twice: the first measurement found generative AI had cut developer productivity by about 20 percent, and when the same group was measured again the following February, productivity was up about 20 percent. By then the developers were refusing to take part in further benchmarking because they did not want to code without AI. His explanation is a capability threshold. In 2023 the models could perform about twenty seconds of a developer's work; in 2024, one to two minutes; in 2025, twenty to thirty minutes; and this year, around ten hours, with a threshold near five hours of task length crossed last fall, after which the models became valuable as near co-programmers. By the end of this year, he expects more than 20 percent of all code to be AI written. The question he leaves hanging is whether the same threshold effect is about to arrive in accounting, corporate law, recruiting, and other knowledge work now being taught to the models.

Catlin adds a second driver that he thinks matters as much: the system around the tools has to change. Simply deploying the technology gets an organization roughly a 20 percent productivity gain. Organizations that fundamentally redesign their end-to-end workflows do far better; he cites development cycles compressed from two-week sprints to a single day, with agents coding overnight and humans handling requirements and testing during the day. The catch is that most workflows have never been codified. They live in employees' heads, so redesigning them is slow, effortful work, and it is where most of the value sits.

The organization lens takes up what this means for people. The panel argues about whether AI destroys or creates work. Montard recalls Keynes predicting in 1920 that productivity growth would bring a fifteen-hour week by 2000; instead, cheaper output expanded demand, living standards rose, and jobs followed. Durth counters that such answers depend entirely on assumptions about adoption speed and about how easily people can be redeployed, and she calls the short to medium term genuinely difficult at the individual level: a person who has been a call center agent for thirty years does not automatically become the person driving innovation. Her one no-regret conclusion holds in every scenario the panel considers. The work humans do will be fundamentally different within three years, so investing in human capability, AI fluency alongside expertise, judgment, and systems thinking, is the move no CEO will wish they had skipped.

Durth's research also reframes the scale of displacement. About 30 percent of the work done today could more viably be done by technology, once regulation and social constraints are counted, but that 30 percent is spread thinly across almost every job rather than concentrated in 30 percent of jobs. One figure she cites says about 60 percent of managers already spend half or more of their time on tasks that could be automated today. Catlin makes the arithmetic explicit for a figure he puts at 75 percent of the workforce: to capture productivity across that much of a company, you have to reconfigure most people's jobs rather than remove most people. New roles are already appearing, such as overseeing agentic systems in a development lifecycle, with skill sets nobody has training pipelines for yet, and he warns against the temptation to stop hiring high-potential people, which would starve the future leadership bench.

On speed, Durth is an optimist. The pandemic showed how fast redeployment can happen when it has to, and she cites technology organizations that redeployed 70 percent of their workforce within nine months. Her operating principle is that you do not teach AI, you do AI: the leadership question is what mechanisms guarantee every employee actually works with the tools. Catlin notes the redesign extends well beyond employees, to contractors, global capability centers, and outsourced vendors, where cutting external work can be one way to protect internal employment while the gains are harvested.

How organizations should be structured for this is the panel's most speculative stretch. Durth argues that companies are still designed for the industrial era, around command, control, and predictability, while AI's removal of friction and transaction costs points toward fluid organizations that deploy people and agents against problems rather than holding them in rigid roles, designed for adaptability instead of stability. Hämäläinen points to the power user phenomenon: roughly 1 percent of users in an organization consume and use AI far beyond everyone else, and they are the ones inventing new ways of working. His suggestion is to skunkworks the transition: choose one to three areas, staff them with about twenty power users, and have them build what an AI-native process looks like from a blank sheet, rather than only retrofitting the existing organization. The result, the panel agrees, will be flatter organizations where shared outcomes replace knowledge as the dominant axis, and where governance and trust have to be designed in from the start rather than added as an afterthought.

Two cautions run underneath. When the mundane tasks that gave people mental breaks disappear, what remains is continuous judgment work, and Sean Brown presses Durth on whether that means a more stressful day and a burnout risk. Her answer is that leaders must create real space for learning by doing, and invest in resilience and brain health, because an organization that simply automates its existing processes will become, in her phrase, wonderfully efficient at doing the wrong things. On cognition, Catlin cites the University of Toronto's Ajay Agrawal: machines now predict very cheaply, which raises the value of the complementary goods, proprietary data and human judgment. He expects the jobs of the future to lean on what stays uniquely human, empathy, relationships, and vision among them, and the panel closes expecting an eventual premium on human skills and strong cultures once the transition's uncertainty passes.

The closing advice is compact. Durth: do not wait on your no-regret and learning moves. Montard: be ambidextrous, exploiting the present while exploring the future, with all three lenses in play at once because there is no choice about considering them together. Hämäläinen returns to his cost arithmetic: if an organization's future cost base might be one third people and two thirds the agents they use, then designing the technology architecture is among the most strategic decisions a CEO makes, and it is the CEO's topic, not the CIO's; enable everyone, invest in their transition, and run bold, almost breakaway experiments alongside. Catlin gets the last word: bring the team together to learn jointly, because the roles of strategist and technologist are already hard to separate, and tomorrow's CHRO and head of technology will be just as hard to separate once the workforce is hybrid.

Open questions

  1. The panel concedes that AI investment has so far paid off outside the enterprise, in the consumer internet's backbone and in programming. What observable evidence over the next year or two would settle whether enterprise workflows really are the third step function, rather than a slower, shallower adoption curve?
  2. If productivity gains are table stakes whose value flows mainly to model providers and consumers, while the durable money sits in innovation and in shifting transaction costs, how should a CEO actually split investment and attention among the three waves when only the first is easy to measure?
  3. Hämäläinen's warning that an agent cost base twice the payroll hands control of operating costs to a single provider assumes switching models stays hard. As agents embed into proprietary data and workflows, does multi-model diversification remain practical, or does depth of integration quietly recreate the lock-in he warns against?
