Matthew Berman · DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, tested hands-on

Deepseek crushed everyone

On Matthew Berman, the episode "Deepseek crushed everyone" is a hands-on tour of DeepSeek's new V4.1 Flash model, which Berman says flew under the radar and is, in his judgment, the best open-source model on the planet. Rather than reading benchmark tables first, he spends most of the video making the model build things: a 3D watch, two games and simulations, a full spreadsheet, and a complete desktop app. His verdict at the end is that this is potentially the best overall model available right now, not because it tops every quality chart, but because its quality sits close to the frontier while its cost and speed are in a different league.

The first test combines 3D generation, spatial awareness, and knowledge stored in the model's weights: build a hyperrealistic 3D mechanical watch diagram in a single web page, with hover text naming each part and explaining what it does. The result, which the model calls Mechanica, impressed him with its working, correctly fitted parts, its lighting and shadows, and its labels. Hovering over the hairspring identifies it and describes how it sets the watch's rhythm, clicking zooms in for a closer look, and an explode button pulls the whole movement apart layer by layer while the labels keep working. His one disappointment is an omission: the back of the watch is missing the little weight that winds the movement as the wearer's wrist moves.

Next he asks for a clone of the multiplayer game Fall Guys, a test he has run on many models. The result, Bean Blitz, is built in plain HTML, and he finds the 3D generation genuinely good, with working hammers that knock his character off the course and a dive move on the space bar. Some objects clip through each other and a few obstacles that look animated do not actually move, and he rates the clone close to, but not quite as good as, what Opus 5.5 produced, at a fraction of the price and much faster.

The third test asks the model to research a product on the web and recreate it in 3D: the folding iPhone Duo. Berman calls the result flawless, with a perfect Apple logo, light reflecting off the screen, a matte finish on the inner display, and the buttons and hinge in the right places. He puts the same test from Moonshot's new Kimi model on screen next to it, and the comparison is not close. The only flaw he finds is in the folding animation, which briefly sends the device off screen and drops the screen image for a moment mid-fold.

A sponsor segment for CoreWeave's Arena program follows, showing load testing of an AI app before production launch, ramping from one request to ten and using an assistant to diagnose why response times rose.

The model is also multimodal, so he tests its visual understanding on a photograph of a pastry counter and asks it to count every item. Working at maximum reasoning, it took four minutes and twenty-five seconds and tallied 137 individual pastries, naming each kind as it went: butter croissants, cream-filled horn croissants, and row after row of macarons. Its count of 24 orange macarons in a pyramid was one short of the 25 Berman could see, close enough that he calls it arguable. More striking to him, the model volunteered, unprompted, that the macarons, croissants, and brownies were stacked, so some items were partly or fully hidden, and it put a defensible range for the whole counter at roughly 130 to 145.

When he then asked it to estimate the hidden items too, the follow-up took only twenty-three seconds, because the spatial reasoning was already done. The model treated the macaron pyramid as a cone-shaped heap, five to six wide at the base and five to six layers tall, and concluded that 45 macarons in total was far more likely than the 24 visible on the front face. It reasoned the same way through the croissant piles, judging them two to three layers deep. Berman is openly delighted: the model understood what was not shown in the image nearly as well as what was.

Speed is the next exhibit. With reasoning effort turned off, he has the model write a thousand-word story directly in the chat window, and it streams at roughly 125 to 150 tokens per second even in that interface. On the Artificial Analysis figures he shows, third-party providers serve the model far faster, up to 1,286 tokens per second at the top end, with Baseten at 845 tokens per second.

He then tests the model as an agent: browse YouTube, understand his interview with Sam Altman, and extract the five most clippable moments with exact timestamps and a reason for each pick. It worked for six minutes and twenty-four seconds, and because the model is open weights, he could inspect its chain of thought and every tool call along the way. Its first pick was Altman saying he has not looked at a line of code in about a year, with the joke that doing so again would feel like a Revolutionary War reenactment, and its reasoning, that the CEO of OpenAI admitting this is newsworthy and the image travels well, matched Berman's own judgment. The other picks, including "coders have never been busier" and the exchange about the AI consciousness debate, were all clips he says he would have chosen himself. The obvious next step, having the model download the video and cut the clips itself, he leaves for another video.

Two builds run entirely on his own hardware. He loaded the model locally on a DGX station and asked it to build an Excel clone for the browser; after fifty minutes of work it produced GridLine, an offline-first spreadsheet where formulas calculate, fill down across dragged cells, and generate charts, short of the full Excel feature set but very good. A deliberately simple prompt for a 3D Rubik's cube that can scramble and solve itself also passed cleanly: the colors stayed on their correct sides through the scramble, the move list was shown, and the solve rotations looked physically right.

The final build is personal. Berman argues that everyone should now be building their own software for the small tools they need, and shows a PDF reader he wanted for Windows because he finds Adobe Acrobat bloated and spammy. Opus 5.5 wrote a detailed prompt, specifying an Electron, React, and TypeScript app called Folio, and DeepSeek, on its high thinking setting, built it: page navigation, highlighting, drawing in multiple colors, text notes, signatures you can draw, upload, or capture by photographing one on paper, and a dark mode that renders any PDF in dark colors. Run locally it cost him nothing; through a paid provider, he estimates, an app like this costs less than a couple of dollars to create and is yours for life.

The benchmarks he closes with support the demos. On a quality-versus-cost frontier chart, the model sits alone in the top-left corner, better and cheaper than Opus 5, Fable 5, GPT 6.1 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and GLM 5.3 Max. It scores 42 on Humanity's Last Exam, level with Kimi K3 and GLM 5.3, and 42 on Terminal Bench. His favorite metric combines price with how many tokens a task takes: cost per completed intelligence task. The priciest models on his chart run to hundreds of dollars per task; this model completes one for twenty-six cents. Provider pricing matches that story, at sixty cents per million output tokens and fifteen cents per million input tokens from the cheapest listing he shows, with cache hits free, and Baseten, which he calls the best overall offering, at a dollar twenty per million output tokens. He acknowledges that Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro scored higher overall in the benchmarks, and then says he personally thinks DeepSeek is much better, and invites viewers to watch his Kimi video and make the comparison themselves.

Open questions

  1. Berman's verdict rests on his own test set, which leans heavily on 3D generation, browser-built apps, and short, well-specified tasks. Does V4.1 Flash hold up as well on the long, messy, ambiguous work where the frontier models earn their much higher prices?
  2. At twenty-six cents per completed task and inference above a thousand tokens per second, the economics of the closed labs look exposed. If open models this capable keep arriving every few months, what exactly are the frontier labs charging for?
  3. The pastry test showed the model reasoning confidently about objects hidden from view, and being roughly right. Where is the line between that kind of spatial inference and simply inventing detail that is not there, and how would a user tell the difference?
