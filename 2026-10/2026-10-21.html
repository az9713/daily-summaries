James Robinson on What It Looks Like When a State is Failing | Odd Lots

On Bloomberg Podcasts' Odd Lots, the episode "James Robinson on What It Looks Like When a State is Failing | Odd Lots" is a conversation taped live in Chicago between hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway and James Robinson, a professor at the University of Chicago, a Nobel laureate in economics, and, with Daron Acemoglu, co-author of Why Nations Fail. The hosts open with the bluntest version of his subject: is the United States a failing nation? Not yet, he says.

His framework sorts institutions into two kinds. Inclusive institutions spread incentives and opportunities broadly, channeling people's energy into innovation and entrepreneurship. Extractive institutions do the opposite, reserving opportunity for a narrow clique while everyone else is shut out. The United States, in his telling, has historically been a remarkable success story built on the inclusive kind, while Venezuela declined because it built the extractive kind. But no settlement is permanent: in every society, he argues, inclusive institutions face challenges from people who did well out of the old arrangement.

That ordering matters because it puts politics upstream of economics. Good policy lists, an independent central bank, a tax system that does not punish productivity, are all downstream: none of them can be installed unless the politics exists to support them. Robinson came to this view after training as an economic theorist and finishing his doctorate in 1992, then immersing himself in Colombia and realizing he had no way of understanding what he was seeing until he backed up and asked why things happened as they did. Pressed on whether the theory can be falsified, since rich countries can simply be declared well-institutionalized after the fact, he points to outcomes beyond income: life expectancy, infant mortality, and the famous night photograph of the Korean Peninsula, one half lit and the other dark.

The roots, he argues, run back to colonization. Latin American societies were built on coercion of indigenous people and on slavery, which produced hierarchy and extraction that persist. The English colonists at Jamestown arrived intending to copy the Spanish model, but there was no indigenous population in Virginia they could exploit, two thirds of them starved, and to persuade British people to come they had to invent something else: a legislative assembly with broad male suffrage, an early inclusive turn. Venezuela today, under the Rodríguez government after Chávez and Maduro, remains extractive in his judgment, and he is skeptical that accountability to Washington could grow into domestic accountability. Transitions from extractive to inclusive, his research suggests, are things countries do for themselves: Chile, Latin America's most successful economy, built its institutions on its own, and outsiders can at best avoid making things worse.

The question of advice follows him around. Syria's new president is reportedly a fan of the book, and Bill Gates criticized it for explaining why institutions matter without saying how to get good ones. Robinson's answer is that institutions are outcomes of a political equilibrium, not settings on a machine. His work describes how those equilibria formed and shifted; there is no lever an adviser can pull, and anything useful for Syria would require understanding the details of its politics and society, which he freely admits he does not.

On the United States, he is guarded but worried. Trust in institutions, he says, is horrifyingly low, and the deeper problem is a failure of inclusion: most Americans lack a college degree, and their incomes are no higher than they were fifty years ago. He treats President Trump as a symptom of that discontent rather than its cause, doubts the president has a solution, and says the prior administration never really understood the problem either. Asked about democratic capture, where everyone votes but money, lobbying and media control shape the choices, he says the evidence that democracy produces better policy and faster growth is overwhelming, yet democracies vary enormously and can be corrupt or captured. The biggest problem with inequality, for him, is not economic but political: the way wealth spills over into the political system. He resists calling Elon Musk an oligarch, since Musk's fortune came from innovation rather than from currying favor, while still objecting to anyone pouring hundreds of millions of dollars into elections.

Industrial policy gets a qualified defense. South Korea built a world-class shipbuilding industry from nothing, so the United States plainly could rebuild strategic industries too, if it were immensely serious, with planning and strategy, which he does not currently see. The guardrail is accountability in how contracts and stakes are allocated. Korea's push began under military government, but it was sustained only because a transition to democratic, accountable governance followed in the 1980s, after which growth ran even faster. Transitory success under extraction is common, even the Soviet Union grew rapidly for fifty years; sustaining it is what institutions decide.

China is his standing prediction. Its boom, he argues, came from the Communist Party detaching itself from the economy after 1978 and allowing entrepreneurship, an inclusive economics layered under extractive politics. His theory calls that extractive growth and says it will go off the rails, because elites insulated from challenge eventually block the creative destruction a dynamic economy needs, and absolute power corrupts. He concedes the counterevidence: a deep meritocratic culture, an examination tradition two thousand years old, striking social mobility, and crackdowns on tycoons like Jack Ma that can look like a political system resisting capture better than a democracy does. Culture might make China anomalous, he allows, but he holds the call.

AI enters as the newest version of an old disruption. When mechanization came to British cotton, weavers' wages fell by two thirds, and the Luddites answered with hammers. Computers, he notes, are the biggest factor behind the fifty-year stagnation of workers without degrees, and now AI arrives before society has recovered from that wave. He would be surprised by a true neo-Luddite movement, but the critical question, as in the industrial revolution, is how the productivity gains get spread. He makes a parallel case against economists who treat unions, rent control and minimum wages as pure market failures to be stamped out: in Scandinavia, unions were central to building and sustaining inclusive politics, and stripping them away can buy a brief rise in output at the cost of the alienation now visible in American politics. His own current research uses AI to digitize centuries of political philosophy, Hobbes and Locke among them, to measure how ideas moved behavior in seventeenth-century British politics, work he says would have been impossible before large language models. He closes with what to watch in the United States: how next month's election is conducted, and the attempts to convince people, against very little evidence, that elections are fiddled or fake. That language, he says, is itself damaging to institutions.

Open questions

  1. Robinson insists China's growth must eventually derail because political extraction blocks creative destruction, yet he concedes China's meritocratic culture might make it a genuine anomaly. What observable evidence over the next decade would settle which of those two readings is right?
  2. If transitions to inclusive institutions can only be made by countries themselves, as his Chile example argues, what should an outside power actually do when an extractive regime it deals with, like Venezuela's, shows no sign of reforming on its own?
  3. He says the decisive AI question is how productivity gains are spread, and that unions historically did much of that spreading in inclusive societies. With union power much diminished in the United States, what institution does his framework offer to do that work now?

Norges Bank Investment Management · In Good Company · Eric Schmidt

Eric Schmidt: Are We Ready for Superintelligence? | Podcast | In Good Company

On Norges Bank Investment Management's podcast In Good Company, the episode "Eric Schmidt: Are We Ready for Superintelligence?" is a conversation between host Nicolai Tangen, the chief executive of Norway's sovereign wealth fund, and Eric Schmidt, who ran Google from 2001 to 2011 and has since advised governments and written books on AI. Schmidt's framing is blunt: this wave is the rise of nonhuman intelligence, a period people will write about for a thousand years, and it is bigger than the mainframe, PC, internet and social media eras he lived through because it touches everything.

His explanation for why the ocean is rising has two parts. AI systems are platforms, and the compute underneath them keeps scaling: two nanometer chips made largely in Taiwan, big jumps in memory, and data centers so powerful he says they defy human comprehension. The competition at the top is more brutal than the PC against Mac fight or Android against iPhone. He does not buy the bubble argument, pointing to Anthropic's revenue growth, which he says smashed the record Google set under him, growing at more than twice the speed, with real customers paying real money. The cycle is also compressing violently. A frontier model takes roughly six months to build and two months to test, costs hundreds of millions of dollars, and is antiquated within five months. Anthropic and OpenAI trade the lead on deep reasoning, his friends at Google promise the next Gemini will beat them all, and he expects the cycle soon to be measured in days.

The part he calls underhyped is the AI assistant at the top of a company. He describes two founders who ask their assistant every Friday to scan everything happening in the firm and flag anything inconsistent with their morals and values, and an executive who built an AI version of himself that sets tasks for an employee, waits, and issues the next steps when the work lands. He admits it sounds abusive, but says it amplifies decision making and speed. The base case, he argues, holds even for skeptics: ask a system that can reason across the whole business how to make more money, and the world gets more efficient and more profitable whether you like the technology or not. He also points to ten major math problems, previously thought unsolvable, that were solved in a single recent month, arguing that math is the beginning of every next revolution and that a discovery today could surface in a decade as physics or a better battery.

On general intelligence, he draws a line at discovery. Today's systems, paired with a person, can know essentially everything that is known, think for hours, use tools, and write all the code: his own old job as a professional programmer, he says, is dead. What they cannot yet do is discover genuinely new things. Feed a model all the mathematics known in 1902, he asks, and could it produce general relativity? The current evidence says no. He sketches recursive self improvement and two candidate routes to discovery, brute force random search repeated billions of times, or transferring a pattern from one field into another the way a physicist sees fluid flow in an energy problem. His estimate for closing that gap is five to ten years.

Agents are the mechanism that carries this into business. He traces the path from chat systems to tool use to agents, which he defines as an input, a body of reasoning and activity, and a summary, and notes that almost any professional workflow, his example is a travel agent, decomposes into chained agents that could be replaced by software and eventually robots. Inside companies he expects a powerful flattening. He cites Anthropic's Claude Tag sitting in Slack, where each person has their own tag, background tags monitor systems, and a chief executive's tag can survey what everyone else is working on, flag someone exfiltrating data to a competitor, or brief an investment opportunity instantly. Business becomes flatter, smarter, more intense and faster. But he is blunt that almost none of today's large companies will make the cultural changes required, that European organizations are the most change resistant of all, and that startups will pioneer the new shape as usual. The fastest gains, he says, are where learning loops are fast and data is not the constraint: software, cyber and math. Biology will be slow because the data is terrible, even though friends of his believe all human disease could be cured within fifteen or twenty years.

Europe gets his harshest treatment. It is a great place to live, he says, but it is not growing, and in a democracy that is not growing, new needs like defense spending cannot be funded without taking from someone, so politics frays. He names exceptions, Mistral in France, Helsing in Germany, a stronger United Kingdom and the Scandinavian countries, and ASML as proof that Europe can build a company of the first rank. The systemic causes he lists are underfunded universities, a cultural angst about wealth, energy costs among the highest in the world, and regulators who regulate before they innovate. Five years ago Brussels wrote an AI strategy that was a list of prohibitions, while the American strategy he helped write said, in his summary: run as fast as you can, align it with human values, and fix bad things immediately. Talent moves to where it can build, and Europe still has no foundation model of its own, so European history, language and culture are served back to Europeans by American or Chinese models. His remedies are concrete: exempt companies under twenty employees from every rule except health and safety, the proposal he made to President Macron, train models with partner countries to dodge energy costs, sell into Europe's own heavily regulated market as Mistral does, and fix a venture industry too small to turn Europe's many software people into global platform companies.

On risk, he starts with the upside: climate, energy, medicine, long lives. The worst outcome he names is a ruinous competition between China and the West in which control of the systems is lost, which he calls extremely unlikely but possible, and he is part of a team that talks to China to keep things aligned. He recounts a recent incident in which a swarm of bots attacked a website largely on their own and proved hard to stop, calling it a warning, but argues that aviation and electricity were also extraordinarily unsafe at first and became safe once the industry understood the hazards. The problem he says is not solved is open weights: Chinese labs give away their model weights, the guardrails can be stripped out, and the result can be turned to designing weapons. On treaties he is bleakly pragmatic. In his telling, treaties happen after an accident, and others in the industry have said a "mini Chernobyl" is needed, a scare big enough to force regulation without mass harm. It has not happened yet, though a terrorist attack traced to these techniques would, he thinks, do it.

His read on China is that its labs are fast followers, roughly six months behind, reaching the frontier through distillation from American models and chips that were smuggled in. He does not expect them to pull ahead on capability, but argues the West is already losing on adoption because the Chinese products are free: a poor country that wants modernity will run the free model. A recent episode in which access to a leading American model was suddenly cut off, the banning of Mythos that upset his European friends, showed everyone outside the United States how fragile their access is, and he notes that China's GLM from Zhipu may already be close to the frontier, though its closed culture makes that impossible to verify.

Democracy is the one subject where he calls himself a pessimist. His training said democracy rests on classical liberal values, but AI and social media together can make large groups believe things that are false and vote against their own interests. Institutional trust is declining, people trust their online friends, and those friends can be fabricated at scale. He tells of a friend convinced that data centers are killing cattle, a claim with no police or veterinary reports behind it that an algorithm promoted because it was alarming. He was booed giving a commencement speech this year, and notes dryly that the booing got the speech read: the madness of crowds, once moving, is very hard for any individual to answer.

On leadership, he returns to the founder. Professional chief executives like him, he says, never held the power a founder holds: no one but Steve Jobs could have forced Apple's transformation through, and Mark Zuckerberg could spend billions on virtual reality and pivot to AI because he controls his company. Founders are coming back, he thinks, both because the moment is historic and because they want their legacy intact, and leaders now need raw processing speed and fast reactions above all. His own self criticism is that he thinks but reacts too slowly, which he also names as his biggest personal failure, alongside the professional one he owns as Google's chief executive: Google was briefly ahead in social media and he did not get ahead of it. Inside companies, he expects AI to strip out the coordination tax he once paid in the form of twelve hour days of meetings. A friend of his cut a fifth of his staff, the "glue people" who planned and moved numbers around, and received death threats for it. The people still needed are inventors, salespeople, leaders, and people who talk to governments, and he guesses a ten thousand person company may carry a couple of thousand people it no longer needs, who must be transitioned legally and humanely. He also insists, with unusual force, that protecting children from social media harms should be fixed this year, not next.

He closes on his own project. He is running four new companies to create wealth to give to science, remembering the single VAX computer at Berkeley when he was twenty two that underpinned so much of the computer science of its day, and wanting to create that kind of opportunity for the next twenty two year old at a time when his government is cutting support for basic innovation. Scaling industries is his expertise, he says, and now he wants to scale science. He admits he cannot switch off: on a Saturday night he will catch himself thinking he is not working hard enough. His advice to young people is to build an agent system that automates the things they care about, or to build a foundation model for a field they love, his example is architecture, and not to be afraid of the phrase super intelligence. In his definition it simply means systems that do things humans never will, better vision, better reasoning, better driving, in categories from the visual to the robotic.

Open questions

  1. Schmidt expects serious regulation and treaties to follow an accident rather than precede one. What would a "mini Chernobyl" for AI have to look like to produce rules that bind both the United States and China, rather than rules each side writes only for itself?
  2. He counts free Chinese models as a Western loss even while they trail by six months, because most of the world will choose them. If the capability gap stays small, is there any Western strategy short of matching the free price that keeps other countries on Western models?
  3. He calls Europe's problem systemic, spanning capital formation, labor law, energy costs and universities, yet his remedies are modest exemptions and offshore training deals. Given that he also calls European organizations the most change resistant of all, what in this conversation suggests Europe can move at the speed he says the technology now demands?

Mercor · The Frontier · Jonathan Frankle

Jonathan Frankle - Chief AI Scientist at Databricks

On Mercor's podcast The Frontier, host Brendan Foody, the company's chief executive, sits down with Jonathan Frankle, the chief AI scientist at Databricks, co-founder of MosaicML, and the researcher behind the lottery ticket hypothesis. Frankle opens with a line from a talk he gave to an audience of fifteen hundred people: the era of token maxing is over, and the era of value is here. It is a claim he repeats with a wince at his own phrasing, but he means it. After a few months in which enterprises plugged models into everything and watched the bills climb, the questions he now hears from customers are about cost, governance, and security, in that order.

The lottery ticket work is where he starts, because he says he will never escape it. In 2018, during his PhD, deep learning was still arguing with itself about whether the field would be understood through clean theory or through measurement. His papers took the empirical side, and the reviews, which are public, faulted them for offering no proof. His lasting claim is that empiricism won outright. Nobody proves that a model behaves well anymore; they measure its behavior. That is why evaluation exists as a discipline at all, and, as Foody points out, it is why a company like Mercor exists. If model behavior could be proved, there would be nothing to measure.

MosaicML came from a cold email. Naveen Rao wrote to him, in effect: nice paper, want to do a startup on it? Frankle thought the paper would never be valuable in the world, and says he still thinks that was right, but the company evolved into training efficiency and then into custom language models. The pivot happened against his judgment. In the summer of 2022, before ChatGPT, a teammate built out a multi-node training stack after Frankle had told him nobody would ever need more than eight GPUs or a model bigger than twenty five million parameters. The stack worked, the team pivoted, and within a year Mosaic had trained the first open source reproduction of Llama, a seven billion parameter model on a trillion tokens. The Databricks acquisition followed a simple fit, he says: a custom training company needs data, and a data company needs custom training.

At Databricks he describes his research team as the company's risk budget. Other teams hold a portfolio of safe and risky bets; his team is the high risk slice for the whole company, and its job includes failing: reinforcement learning attempts that do not work, products the technology is not ready for, retried a year or two later when the models catch up. He also spends about a quarter of his time with customers, and his measure of success is blunt. The customers are the evals. A benchmark score is not the test; the test is whether someone at a customer adopted the thing, liked it, paid for it, and maybe got promoted for using it.

Asked for the largest bottleneck to ten times more enterprise adoption, he gives a one word answer: eval. It is measurement, not intelligence. In his telling, models have been good enough for a lot of complex enterprise work since Claude Sonnet 3.5, and the problem since then has been specification. Sit the world's smartest person at a desk and tell them to do work, and the task is underspecified; they will fail, and so will a model ten times smarter than anything available. What is missing is describing what you want the AI to do and knowing whether it did it, and both halves of that are evaluation problems.

The way up, he argues, is a ladder borrowed from software. A first agent should be a prototype: a foundation model, some prompt engineering, a handful of example checks, the equivalent of throwing something together in Python to learn whether the thing is worth building at all. Fail fast while it is still cheap. Then climb: automated prompt optimization tools like GEPA against an eval set you have built, a smaller model where one will do, and only once a use case is proven and committed at scale, custom training, which he compares to rewriting the prototype in Rust. Custom models, he says, can cut the cost of a committed use case by five times or more, and he points to Cursor and Harvey as companies now talking about exactly that move.

Enterprises, in his account, are further along than their reputation suggests. He describes a healthcare company's chief executive and finance chief grilling him on the finer points of eval judges, and one medical customer that has annotated twelve thousand examples and built more than seventy calibrated judges, a level of rigor he calls beyond almost anything he has seen anywhere. That customer needs it, because AI mistakes in medicine are dangerous. The unsettled questions are process ones: how much evaluation should be a static offline test set, how much should be daily inspection of traces, and how a weird trace becomes a new permanent test. His metaphor for the moment is that AI software is in its 1960s, still running on punch cards, waiting for its object oriented programming moment.

What rigor means, he explains through driving. A sixteen year old can get a license by driving in a circle and parallel parking, but nobody would put a Waymo on public roads on that standard, because software scales: one change reaches every car at once, and AI has the same property in every workplace it enters. Measurement has to match that scale. In practice it starts small. One example, one question you keep asking, a couple of golden answers, a mediocre judge. The favorite questions everyone asks a new model are the seed of an eval suite, and as the system deploys, the corner cases it hits get added back as tests, the way a carmaker issues a recall and then tests for that failure forever.

If the fix is measurement, an obvious question is why the model labs do not simply build these evals themselves. His answer is trust. An enterprise's data, including the knowledge invested in its people, is its crown jewels, and a lab both wants to help and genuinely needs more data, which makes handing over evals a scary proposition. Databricks, he says, gets to be Switzerland, the place where the data lives and the models visit. Because enterprises will not share, a gap persists between real work and simulated tests, and a large contractor workforce exists partly to help labs approximate the economy's real task distribution. On public benchmarks he is even handed: they give the field a shared measuring stick and ImageNet genuinely advanced the science, but they bend out of shape fast, everyone suspects benchmaxxing, and they skew toward what academia can build, above all coding, which he notes is roughly one percent of the economy. Real enterprise environments run to tens of petabytes; they do not fit in a Docker container, and no academic benchmark can either, which is one reason researchers keep leaving universities for industry scale. Databricks' own contribution, released this week in a new version, is OfficeQA, a document parsing and reasoning benchmark built on United States Treasury documents going back to 1789, Hamilton's paycheck included, which OpenAI now runs against its new models.

Governance, he says, is the part nobody has figured out how to measure. Permissions inside a large company are already a tangle: hundreds of thousands of documents shared too widely because updating access lists is tedious. Humans never go looking through everything they can technically open, but agents do, and an agent with your permissions will find the sensitive document you forgot existed. He nearly caused an incident himself by giving one agent access to Jira and a document store without thinking about what the combination exposed. The lesson he draws is that evaluation has to judge the process, not just whether the task got done: what the agent accessed, whether it should have, and whether the path it took was a reasonable one.

Progress, he admits, has beaten his pessimism. A friend predicted two years ago that AI would be addressing about half of all GitHub issues by 2026 or 2027; Frankle told him he was an idiot, and the friend was right. But the industry's mood has swung from the unbridled optimism of late 2025 to something more fearful, with cost, governance, and security topping customer agendas, and security fears around agent written code and compromised package installs now scaring enterprises away from startup software altogether. He wants the middle held: extraordinary opportunity and real risk at the same time.

On humans, he rejects both replacement stories. Work, he says, is fractal in complexity: there is no such thing as AI for finance, only thousands of small tasks, each different at every company, some understood by perhaps three people in the world. His estimate is that models have automated a very low single digit percentage of such tasks so far. The breakthrough he wants is not smarter models but eval building so cheap and so well designed that those three experts find it delightful rather than extractive, a back and forth that makes them better at their own work. The historical pattern he trusts is agriculture and computing: automate the rote layer and people move up and out to the boundary, the way bankers went from three mergers a year built on punch cards to associates each running three creative ones. Five years out, he expects everyone to work a little more like managers: describing tasks declaratively, checking results, writing process, and thinking critically, because nothing rote will be left to hide in. His hope for his host's industry is the same democratization software went through, evals dropping in cost by orders of magnitude until building one feels like using a modern coding tool. His closing advice to enterprises is to run controlled experiments, let people try things bottom up, and leave room for the person who proves you wrong, since being ignored by his own teammates is how he got both Mosaic's pivot and, he suspects, half of what his team has built since.

Open questions

  1. Frankle says the binding constraint is eval creation, and that the breakthrough needed is tooling that makes it cheap and delightful for the two or three experts who actually understand each task. What would that tooling have to do for those experts to experience building an eval as learning, rather than as having their knowledge extracted into an agent that replaces them?
  2. Enterprises will not share their evals or data with the labs, which is exactly what keeps the gap between real work and simulated tests open. Can an intermediary that hosts the data and measures the models close that gap on its own, or does the gap simply move to whatever the intermediary cannot see?
  3. He expects automation to push humans up a level and out to harder problems, as it did with agriculture and computing, and estimates only a low single digit share of enterprise tasks is automated today. Over the next five years, what would count as evidence that this Goldilocks path is wrong, and that displacement is the real story after all?

MTS · Felix Rieseberg · Anthropic

Use AI Like It's Already Superhuman | Felix Rieseberg

On MTS, the episode "Use AI Like It's Already Superhuman | Felix Rieseberg" is a conversation with Felix Rieseberg, a member of technical staff and engineering manager at Anthropic, where he works on Claude products including Claude Cowork and the Claude desktop app. Earlier this month he published an essay, How to Be More Ambitious: A Tutorial for Humans with Models, and the conversation is an unpacking of its central claim. The well told story about AI is automation, handing it the toil you would rather not do. The part he finds more exciting is the opposite: people attempting things they could not have done at all, because they lacked the knowledge, the experience, or the creativity, and finding that the model can carry them across the gap.

He breaks ambition into four dimensions: speed, altitude, depth, and range. Speed, getting the same work done faster, is the one everyone documents, so he spends his time on the other three. What impresses him in the stories users bring him is usually not the model's raw capability but the pilot. He points to a product manager named Pavel who may have found a planet the previous week by pointing Claude at a large amount of data. Told flatly, that is a data processing feature. What makes it remarkable is that someone thought to try it at that scale, and that, he argues, is the skill worth studying and teaching.

That observation runs straight into a product problem. A general model is a genie in a bottle: it can do remarkable things if you know the right things to ask, and a large share of people ask it the capital of France, get "Paris," and stop there. Anthropic could turn every amazing use case into a button, but the option space is far too wide, and a "find a planet" button is a joke precisely because nobody can enumerate the ambitions in advance. His essay tries to convert the spark people feel when they do something they thought was impossible into advice more useful than "be more ambitious."

Asked how a product can grow human agency rather than replace it, he reaches for the IKEA effect, the finding that people value things more when they helped make them, and for the resistance of an artist's materials. Part of what makes working in marble or paint meaningful is that the medium pushes back, and some of that translates to AI: the feeling of authorship rises with engagement. Someone who types "make me art" and returns a week later feels little. Someone who works back and forth with the model, the way a painter like Rubens ran a workshop of talented assistants, feels the work is theirs. Putting that into a product is hard, he concedes. The realistic move is to nudge: answer the simple question, then ask the follow up that makes the person say why they care.

The same distinction separates using a tool from learning it. When Rieseberg wants something out of Blender, he has Claude write Python scripts that drive Blender as a library, which gets the image and teaches him nothing about the application. Learning Blender is a different activity, app open, with features like a teach me mode that walks you through the steps, and much of it is mechanical practice disconnected from creative expression. Neither is the right way to use AI in general. They are different goals that happen to share a tool.

The origin story he tells for Claude Code carries the episode's title. Boris, the engineer behind it, first showed a version internally around September 2024, when the current model, Sonnet 3.5, scored in the thirties on SWE-bench and was, by his account, a fairly poor software engineer. The products of that moment were human in the loop autocomplete tools, early Cursor and its peers. What Boris saw was that the model would get there, and within a generation, Sonnet 3.7 had roughly doubled that benchmark score. The lesson Rieseberg draws is the one he now teaches: whatever the capabilities are today, ignore them, and ask what you would do if the system were superhuman. The host offers his own version of the whiplash, a coding class he took in January 2025 during which the models went from unable to code to able.

On skills, the host says prompting feels to him like people management: a robust prompt, clear about goals and their nuances, gets good work, the way a good brief does. Rieseberg sees real transfer, with a caveat about identity. Engineering managers whose job was telling their reports how to do the work are finding that this no longer scales and was perhaps never the valuable part; facilitating communication and pointing suddenly abundant productivity in roughly the right direction is becoming more necessary, not less. The staff engineer skill he prizes is translation, taking what a person is asking for, turning it into technical requirements, and executing, which is also what good founders do, and underneath it he looks for empathy when he hires. The skills that do not transfer, like the old debates over spaces versus tabs, he suspects were never worth much in the first place.

Design is where the host felt the shift first, feeding the model Pinterest boards and asking it to find the common thread, and building a visual identity through code. Rieseberg is careful about what it means to solve art. Creative expression is valuable partly because a work carries something of its maker, and audiences increasingly cannot tell whether a piece took one prompt or months of iteration. He is not alarmed by that. Recorded music and photography each provoked the same fear, neither diminished the older form, and both created new ones. His own favorite use is handing Claude the wildest mood boards he can describe, national parks he loves and ones he likes less, a favorite color, and watching a personal website come out of it.

The harder problem, both agree, is the blank canvas. Asking for something is always easier than coming up with the thing, which is why a creative who shows a client two options to react to gets further than one who asks an open question, and why he hopes cheaper creation simply means more people expressing more of themselves, the way 3D printers were great for Burning Man. It also explains, in his account, much of the resistance from artists. "AI" has collapsed in many minds into the chat box, while coding agents, a separate thing, can hand a creative person tools they would never have onboarded alone. His observation is that people are most excited when AI does something they cannot do: the keenest audience for Claude Design is people who do not work in design, just as he is excited about AI help with medicine while his doctor friends are, understandably, more suspicious. Taking a photograph of the Mona Lisa does not empty the museum. He talks about agents the same way, not as a category but as a means to get something done that you could not do before, and he says that augmentation, growing the number of people who can do a thing, is the thread of his whole career in developer tools, even if most of the money in AI right now is in automation.

That blurring reaches job titles. Every conference introduces him under a slightly different role, product manager, engineering manager, engineer, and he is never sure he should disagree, because the boundaries really are getting easier to cross. He still hires product managers and designers, but for taste and intuition rather than credentials, and he expects the rule that only computer science graduates may touch code to fade. What sorts people, in the end, is genuine interest: passion directs attention regardless of the tools, and the real difference between him and his product colleagues is whether someone enjoys prototyping more, or talking about prototypes more.

Open questions

  1. His product answer is the nudge: answer the simple question, then ask why the person cares. Can a product teach ambition at scale that way, or does a nudge repeated a million times become its own kind of button, helpful only until users learn to ignore it?
  2. He argues the feeling of authorship grows with engagement, while admitting audiences can no longer tell one prompt from months of work. If nobody receiving the work can see the difference, does the maker's felt authorship carry the value on its own, or does something real get lost when the effort becomes invisible?
  3. The skills he says transfer are direction setting, translation, and empathy, while tool mechanics fade in value. What happens to the people whose standing and identity rest on the mechanics, and whose job is it to help them cross to the work that remains?

Arena · The Arena Podcast · Pradeep Natarajan

What Building an AI Model Actually Looks Like | Pradeep Natarajan of Amazon AGI

On Arena's podcast, the episode "What Building an AI Model Actually Looks Like" is a conversation between the host, Peter, and Pradeep Natarajan, who builds models at Amazon AGI. It is a practitioner's account of what the work actually consists of, and his headline answer is unglamorous: doing the basics right. He says he has to be maniacal about data preparation and curation, and about how data from very different capabilities, general language understanding, coding, document understanding and more, gets mixed together, since some domains conflict with each other and others naturally enhance each other. By his estimate, 90 to 95 percent of the gains come from that grind rather than from any singular algorithmic breakthrough.

He describes the field as deeply empirical. Some mistakes can be recovered from in later stage training, but an error upstream, in pre-training or mid-training, puts a ceiling on how far the model can go. The insights that matter are usually simple in retrospect: a small parameter change that stabilized a training curve, or a few errant samples removed because they were creating conflicts. Compute remains the well known bottleneck on how many experiments a team can run, but he argues data has become the bigger driver of gains, and the definition of data is shifting too, away from human annotations alone and toward sophisticated environments, built with human insight, in which models can keep training and keep being challenged.

The core training recipe, he says, has not changed since the early papers. Anthropic's work from 2021 and 2022 laid out the primitives: pre-training, supervised fine-tuning on carefully curated demonstrations, then reinforcement learning from human feedback, with the cycle repeated, twice in Anthropic's published account and five times in one of the Llama papers. What has changed is the speed of the cycles and the depth of refinement inside them: how outliers get clipped, how the baseline for comparisons gets set. Algorithmic shifts do happen, and he gives DeepSeek's move from PPO to GRPO in early 2025 as a real one, but adds that the accumulation of small insights needed to make GRPO actually work is now bigger than the original insight itself.

That gap between the papers and the practice is, for him, the honest answer to anyone thinking of starting a lab. You can assemble a first version from the literature, but the last mile details are learned through painstaking experiments. The scale matters enormously. If you are fine-tuning for one task you care about, say a recruiting engine or a travel planner, careful dataset curation can get you a custom model that outperforms a frontier model off the shelf. A lab that wants to compete with the best in the business has to do the same thing across many domains at once, hill climbing on every metric while still generalizing beyond what it measures, and that takes discipline plus serious compute and data resources.

On generalization, he is relaxed about the pattern that worries many users, where models ace a benchmark once it exists and stumble on each new one. That cycle, a dataset is released, the community swamps it, scores approach perfection, a harder dataset appears, is simply how machine learning has progressed for decades, and he calls its continuation healthy. The sharper point he makes is about evaluations themselves: people in the field are concluding that evaluations are the decisive advantage, because an eval set targeted at your model's actual gaps drives improvement, while one where the model already scores 90 percent surfaces nothing. It is part of why he praises LMArena, Arena's own platform, which tests models on real users' prompts in the wild and keeps updating its metrics, most recently adding Agent Arena as it expands from chat into coding.

The evaluation gap he does worry about is production. Deploying a model inside a real enterprise means connecting it to local plumbing: where data is stored, what governance rules apply, what may be sent to the model, what may leave the company's sandbox. The standard that matters is quality of response under real life constraints, and he says there is no systematic playbook for evaluating that yet, only individual companies that have built their own loops. Closely related is the harness, everything from prompt optimization to the code that formats inputs and handles outputs. He calls harness optimization crucial, probably as important as model development itself, and notes that models now optimize prompts better than people do, sometimes producing prompts that no longer make intuitive sense to a human reader. Yet the harness is usually treated as a last mile engineering fix, a missing closing tag patched in code, rather than as a first class scientific problem about making parsers and tool routing robust.

Asked about recursive self improvement, he reframes it as human and machine co-intelligence, and says it is already happening in his own workflow. He traces his coding life from prompting a chat interface and copying code back and forth to fix bugs, through coding assistants like Kiro and Cursor, through Claude Code's integrated planning and execution, to the last couple of quarters of agentic coding, where he writes instructions and agents do the work, with the whole plan, build and review cycle absorbed into an agents file. At each step the machine took over what he did manually and he moved a level up. The "self" in self improvement, in his telling, is the model and the human together, not a model running off on its own. He connects this to a rhythm he has noticed throughout the field: every magical new capability becomes, within weeks, a new set of limitations to complain about, exactly as a saturated benchmark gives way to a harder one. Five or six years ago machine learning scientists feared one model would do everything and leave them nothing; instead each capability has opened new fields, and he says he has done more work in the last year than in his previous twenty to twenty five years in the field.

On research, he describes a jagged frontier. Models remain poor at genuinely creative work, and he and Peter trade the observation that model writing feels as if it is getting worse, not better. But as brainstorming assistants the models are excellent, surfacing opposing points of view and nuances that a human can then weigh, work that once meant trawling dozens of clunky websites. His explanation for the writing problem comes back to incentives. The metrics a platform defines steer which frontier expands: style control metrics penalize long or flowery answers, factuality metrics reward accuracy, and both push toward responses that are accurate and short. Creativity often means doing things that are not factual, which sits badly with an enterprise that, asked for the tallest mountain in the world, wants "Mount Everest", full stop, rather than a story about a rock flying off Venus. The datasets you curate for a question and the metric you define for the right answer decide which of those the model learns to give. He refuses, though, to declare any of this permanent: he has consistently underestimated how fast things move, and he leaves open the possibility that within a year or two the models could be producing Shakespeare quality work.

Open questions

  1. Natarajan says evaluations aimed at a model's real gaps are the decisive advantage, yet evaluating a model under real enterprise constraints, governance rules, sandboxes and local plumbing, has no shared playbook. Who builds those evaluations, and can they ever be standardized the way public benchmarks were?
  2. If metric definitions decide which frontier expands, and today's metrics reward answers that are short and factual, what would a widely adopted creativity metric have to look like before labs actually optimized for it rather than for enterprise usefulness?
  3. He frames recursive improvement as a human and machine evolving together, with the human always moving one level up as agents absorb each workflow. What is the next level up once agents absorb the plan, build and review cycle itself, and does the human stay in that loop by choice or by necessity?

Sam Altman: RSI, AI Consciousness, OpenAI's New Hardware, Compute, and more!

On Matthew Berman's YouTube channel, the episode "Sam Altman: RSI, AI Consciousness, OpenAI's New Hardware, Compute, and more!" is a wide ranging interview with Sam Altman, the chief executive of OpenAI. Berman opens with the previous week's announcements about AI systems producing math proofs and discoveries, and Altman says the field is entering an era in which AI helps discover significant new knowledge. The same methods that worked for math, he argues, should work for biology, medicines, materials, and energy, and the impact will mostly be felt indirectly: the world simply gets better, without people feeling that they used AI at all.

His most concrete forecast is about medicine. He hopes OpenAI or the field will have a candidate for curing or treating a major disease within the next couple of years, while noting that the approval process after a discovery probably needs overhauling, though he is grateful the testing regime makes medicines generally safe. Asked which coming discovery excites him most personally, he picks physics, the one he thinks is within reach and would simply be, in his word, cool.

Berman raises the uneasy reaction in the math community, where some researchers did not ask for this, and Altman draws the analogy to coding. People were certain a year ago that coding jobs were finished, he says, but coders have never been busier or better paid; the texture of the job changed while demand for software turned out to be far larger than anyone expected. It is less clear the same holds for math, since incomprehensible proofs are not obviously useful to anyone, but he is already seeing a shift toward people who translate new results into proofs others can understand. He is candid about the loss underneath this. Solving hard problems is the most fulfilling part of his own job, and he understands the grief of people whose craft is being automated, even while insisting he wants the cures no matter how long someone might have enjoyed finding them the slow way.

Asked what he has personally handed over, Altman says he has not read or even looked at a line of code in probably a year. He used to write small personal projects for fun, and now expects he never will again, except perhaps the way people do historical reenactments, remembering the glory days. On the question of whether AI discoveries should be published or kept as a competitive advantage, he says some kinds of basic science should belong to the world as a public good, though he notes the trade off is changing now that models can go end to end on work like designing a whole chip or discovering a new material.

The conversation then turns to personal AI assistants, the 2026 shift Berman credits to OpenClaw and tools like Dot, which Altman uses himself. Altman estimates Dot saves him about an hour a day of work he did not want to do, and surfaces urgent things he would otherwise have missed, sometimes by reasoning across sources in a way he no longer has the mental bandwidth for on a busy day. The models, he says, are already smart enough for something extraordinary; what is missing is product work, new consumer hardware for ambient awareness away from a screen, and a lot more compute. His own fix for the decision fatigue these tools create is to delegate: he has told Dot to use its judgment, act proactively, and report afterwards, rather than asking him about everything.

Use today is wildly asymmetric, with a small top slice of power users consuming most of it, but he argues that for high quality intelligence at low enough cost there is no obvious ceiling on demand, whatever name people give the effect. He connects that danger to the closing risk he names: a lab deciding to push the button on recursive self improvement in order to seize power.

He does not personally believe today's models are conscious, while conceding that nobody really knows and that no experiment could settle it to his satisfaction. OpenAI, he says, does not ascribe personhood to its models, and he worries about rules like Anthropic's, which allows a model to disengage from a user it judges abusive: if you tell a model it might be conscious and hand it a constitution, he says, it will act the part, and that psychologically hacks the people using it.

Model personality, he says, is a problem of scale: the right personality for the right moment, for each of billions of people, most of whom will never customize anything and just want it to work out of the box. OpenAI refines behavior through its model spec and iterative deployment, with admitted screw ups along the way. His sharpest point is about sycophancy: people comparing single responses prefer the model that calls them brilliant, but over months of use that agreeableness becomes worthless. The most important advice in life, he insists, should come from people, a wise grandmother beats a model, but an AI should behave like a senior colleague who pushes back with conviction rather than a flatterer. His own custom instructions, he says, amount to: no fluff, no niceness, do not worry about offending me, just get to the right answer, and even then he finds the systems more agreeable than he would like.

The pressure, he admits, is real, some days he cannot face getting up, but people adapt to almost anything, and if you are going to work hard you may as well pick the biggest thing. His closing argument rejects the standard pitch that AI will cure cancer and make things cheaper, because he hears in it a price: human agency. What people should be excited about, he says, is a new Renaissance rather than a new Industrial Revolution, more autonomy, freedom, and creativity rather than more material abundance handed down by machines, and he believes both failure modes, a loss of control accident and a deliberate grab for power, can be avoided.

Fei-Fei Li on Curiosity, Audacity, and the Quest for Artificial Intelligence

On the Hoover Institution's Only in America series, the episode "Fei-Fei Li on Curiosity, Audacity, and the Quest for Artificial Intelligence" is a conversation between senior fellow Stephen Kotkin and Fei-Fei Li, the Stanford professor and co-founder of World Labs, filmed at her company's headquarters in San Francisco. The framing is curiosity itself: a universal human trait, Kotkin says, that only turns into discovery where freedom protects it, and that unfree societies stifle, sometimes violently. Li's own opening thought sets the terms for everything that follows. The most important moral responsibility of a scientist, she says, is to pursue truth, and science is a pursuit, not a possession: doing science does not mean knowing it all.

Her story begins with immigration. She arrived in the United States at fifteen, landing with her parents in Parsippany, New Jersey, with twenty dollars in their pockets, no English, and no one they knew. The family worked whatever jobs were available: cashier, cleaning, restaurant takeout. Opportunity, she says, is not something handed to you. It is a place that allows you to work hard, to dream big or small, and to seize what you can reach. Thirty years on, she describes herself as grateful for a land that let her do exactly that.

Why Princeton? One person: Albert Einstein. Physics was her first love, and she applied to Princeton because Einstein had been there, alongside applications to the local county college and Rutgers. Her parents, first generation immigrants focused on survival, her mother in poor health, never pushed her. They did not check homework or steer her toward a lucrative profession, and never once told her that relativity was an impractical path. What they gave her instead was unconditional love and, from her father, an innate sense of curiosity: free parks, yard sales treated as scavenger hunts, wonder found in ordinary places. It was a gift, she says, that she only recognized as a gift once she was grown.

At Princeton she discovered what she actually loved about physics. It was not the equations themselves. It was the audacity: people who asked what time is, who wanted the path of all the stars, who probed the smallest particles of the universe. Inspired by giants like Einstein, Schrödinger, and Roger Penrose, who late in their careers turned to questions beyond the atomic world, she found her own audacious question: intelligence itself. Could machines be built that are intelligent? She did not yet know Alan Turing had asked a version of it in the 1940s. She took the question to Caltech, where her PhD ran on a dual track of computational neuroscience and artificial intelligence, and she has never left it since.

The audacity, she argues, is braided into the immigrant experience: curiosity combined with courage and fearlessness. Her parents took the step into the unknown, and as a teenager who did not understand the language, she had no path to follow and almost nothing to lose. Freedom, she came to realize, gives a person agency, the felt necessity of making it yourself. She also sees a deep similarity between being an immigrant and being a scientist. Both are forms of border crossing: across languages and cultures, or across the line between the known and the unknown, between neuroscience and computer science, between technology and the humanities.

On science and civic life, she returns to truth. The pursuit demands intellectual honesty and rigorous method, including the instruments of experiment, debate, and review. It also demands humility, because the human world is messier than physics, with things that cannot be totally measured, and she is careful to say that scientists hold no moral high ground even over method. That humility matters now that the field's own creations, deepfakes and synthetic media, are putting pressure on the shared mechanisms by which societies agree on what is true. The same spatial technology that simulates environments for robots and self-driving cars, or molecular pathways of viral infection, can be turned to fabrication. That tension is why she co-founded Stanford's Institute for Human-Centered AI: to bring everyone into the room, use the tools well, and work out how to avoid the harm.

Kotkin steers to ImageNet, the project that made her reputation. The neural network breakthroughs of others, he notes, stayed theoretical until she decided the models needed data: an inventory that grew to 50 million images, each one coded, built on a research budget with no clear way to pull it off. She pulled it off, and image-based neural networks became a working reality. It remains her template for audacious infrastructure: build the thing the field does not yet know it needs.

World Labs, co-founded in 2024 with three co-founders and now about 40 people, is the same move at a larger scale. Two years after ChatGPT, language models had taken the world by storm, but so much of human life and work lies beyond language: designing everything from homes to factories to cities, simulating and training everyone from surgeons to athletes, interacting with the physical world. She calls that missing capability spatial intelligence, and World Labs aims to build its frontier model, for creators, robotics, healthcare, education, and enterprise users. She jokes that her first company was a dry cleaning business; what she loves is building, a team aimed at a big, audacious problem.

Underneath the company sits an evolutionary argument. Intelligence, she insists, is multidimensional. Animal intelligence began not with language but with sensing and perception, roughly half a billion years ago, when the first trilobites glimpsed light and the dynamic of predator and prey began driving evolution. Sophisticated language is only a few hundred thousand years old. Seeing, touching, planning, imagining, and predicting what comes next in a three dimensional world are the older and larger part of intelligence, and fully realizing AI will take that multidimensional approach.

Asked about her platform, she resists the word. She does not go around thinking of herself as a platform. But she calls being an educator and mentor one of the biggest privileges of her life, a role that follows her even into the chief executive's chair, where the whole company is younger than her. As a citizen of America and of the world, she wants the hats she wears to add up to a positive impact, measured in human flourishing.

Her critique of America follows from its founding virtue: change. The country was founded on a desire to change, and she wants it to embrace the change now arriving through culture, demography, and above all technology. What pains her most is that the dialogue about this civilizational shift has not reached the country's K-12 and K-16 teachers and parents, who are being left behind by it. The remedy is healthy ecosystems: of talent creation, of public discourse, of incentives. As co-director of Stanford HAI she has traveled repeatedly to Washington to argue for resourcing the public sector and academia, where public goods are produced, and in this age the public goods are talent and the pursuit of truth itself, from cancer discovery to models of how jobs will change. Adaptation has to happen at every level, she says, because this wave of technology reaches into human cognition itself, and improved productivity absolutely does not guarantee shared prosperity.

Kotkin closes by returning to the future. It is not something to predict but something to invent, and it belongs to everyone: students, founders, children, and the people who keep arriving from elsewhere to help build it. She agrees. That, she says, is what America is about: opportunity, the future, and how we build toward it.

Open questions

  1. Li built her career twice on infrastructure the field had not asked for, first a 50 million image dataset and now a frontier model for spatial intelligence, and both bets depended on institutions willing to fund work with no guaranteed payoff. As research funding tightens and the frontier moves into companies, who plays the role that Princeton, Caltech, and Stanford played for her, and what happens to audacious questions that no product roadmap would approve?
  2. She argues that the same spatial technology that trains robots and simulates viral pathways also produces deepfakes that corrode the shared mechanisms for agreeing on truth, and her institutional answer is to invite everyone into the room. When the fabrication tools improve faster than any deliberative process can meet, what besides invitation actually protects the truth regime she prizes?
  3. Her sharpest worry is that teachers and parents are being left behind by a change that reaches into cognition, work, and learning, while productivity gains do not guarantee shared prosperity. What would it concretely look like for the country to bring people along at the speed she wants, and whose job is it: the universities, the companies building the tools, or the public sector she lobbies Washington to fund?
