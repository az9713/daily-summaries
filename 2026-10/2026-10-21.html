James Robinson on What It Looks Like When a State is Failing | Odd Lots

On Bloomberg Podcasts' Odd Lots, the episode "James Robinson on What It Looks Like When a State is Failing | Odd Lots" is a conversation taped live in Chicago between hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway and James Robinson, a professor at the University of Chicago, a Nobel laureate in economics, and, with Daron Acemoglu, co-author of Why Nations Fail. The hosts open with the bluntest version of his subject: is the United States a failing nation? Not yet, he says.

His framework sorts institutions into two kinds. Inclusive institutions spread incentives and opportunities broadly, channeling people's energy into innovation and entrepreneurship. Extractive institutions do the opposite, reserving opportunity for a narrow clique while everyone else is shut out. The United States, in his telling, has historically been a remarkable success story built on the inclusive kind, while Venezuela declined because it built the extractive kind. But no settlement is permanent: in every society, he argues, inclusive institutions face challenges from people who did well out of the old arrangement.

That ordering matters because it puts politics upstream of economics. Good policy lists, an independent central bank, a tax system that does not punish productivity, are all downstream: none of them can be installed unless the politics exists to support them. Robinson came to this view after training as an economic theorist and finishing his doctorate in 1992, then immersing himself in Colombia and realizing he had no way of understanding what he was seeing until he backed up and asked why things happened as they did. Pressed on whether the theory can be falsified, since rich countries can simply be declared well-institutionalized after the fact, he points to outcomes beyond income: life expectancy, infant mortality, and the famous night photograph of the Korean Peninsula, one half lit and the other dark.

The roots, he argues, run back to colonization. Latin American societies were built on coercion of indigenous people and on slavery, which produced hierarchy and extraction that persist. The English colonists at Jamestown arrived intending to copy the Spanish model, but there was no indigenous population in Virginia they could exploit, two thirds of them starved, and to persuade British people to come they had to invent something else: a legislative assembly with broad male suffrage, an early inclusive turn. Venezuela today, under the Rodríguez government after Chávez and Maduro, remains extractive in his judgment, and he is skeptical that accountability to Washington could grow into domestic accountability. Transitions from extractive to inclusive, his research suggests, are things countries do for themselves: Chile, Latin America's most successful economy, built its institutions on its own, and outsiders can at best avoid making things worse.

The question of advice follows him around. Syria's new president is reportedly a fan of the book, and Bill Gates criticized it for explaining why institutions matter without saying how to get good ones. Robinson's answer is that institutions are outcomes of a political equilibrium, not settings on a machine. His work describes how those equilibria formed and shifted; there is no lever an adviser can pull, and anything useful for Syria would require understanding the details of its politics and society, which he freely admits he does not.

On the United States, he is guarded but worried. Trust in institutions, he says, is horrifyingly low, and the deeper problem is a failure of inclusion: most Americans lack a college degree, and their incomes are no higher than they were fifty years ago. He treats President Trump as a symptom of that discontent rather than its cause, doubts the president has a solution, and says the prior administration never really understood the problem either. Asked about democratic capture, where everyone votes but money, lobbying and media control shape the choices, he says the evidence that democracy produces better policy and faster growth is overwhelming, yet democracies vary enormously and can be corrupt or captured. The biggest problem with inequality, for him, is not economic but political: the way wealth spills over into the political system. He resists calling Elon Musk an oligarch, since Musk's fortune came from innovation rather than from currying favor, while still objecting to anyone pouring hundreds of millions of dollars into elections.

Industrial policy gets a qualified defense. South Korea built a world-class shipbuilding industry from nothing, so the United States plainly could rebuild strategic industries too, if it were immensely serious, with planning and strategy, which he does not currently see. The guardrail is accountability in how contracts and stakes are allocated. Korea's push began under military government, but it was sustained only because a transition to democratic, accountable governance followed in the 1980s, after which growth ran even faster. Transitory success under extraction is common, even the Soviet Union grew rapidly for fifty years; sustaining it is what institutions decide.

China is his standing prediction. Its boom, he argues, came from the Communist Party detaching itself from the economy after 1978 and allowing entrepreneurship, an inclusive economics layered under extractive politics. His theory calls that extractive growth and says it will go off the rails, because elites insulated from challenge eventually block the creative destruction a dynamic economy needs, and absolute power corrupts. He concedes the counterevidence: a deep meritocratic culture, an examination tradition two thousand years old, striking social mobility, and crackdowns on tycoons like Jack Ma that can look like a political system resisting capture better than a democracy does. Culture might make China anomalous, he allows, but he holds the call.

AI enters as the newest version of an old disruption. When mechanization came to British cotton, weavers' wages fell by two thirds, and the Luddites answered with hammers. Computers, he notes, are the biggest factor behind the fifty-year stagnation of workers without degrees, and now AI arrives before society has recovered from that wave. He would be surprised by a true neo-Luddite movement, but the critical question, as in the industrial revolution, is how the productivity gains get spread. He makes a parallel case against economists who treat unions, rent control and minimum wages as pure market failures to be stamped out: in Scandinavia, unions were central to building and sustaining inclusive politics, and stripping them away can buy a brief rise in output at the cost of the alienation now visible in American politics. His own current research uses AI to digitize centuries of political philosophy, Hobbes and Locke among them, to measure how ideas moved behavior in seventeenth-century British politics, work he says would have been impossible before large language models. He closes with what to watch in the United States: how next month's election is conducted, and the attempts to convince people, against very little evidence, that elections are fiddled or fake. That language, he says, is itself damaging to institutions.

Open questions

  1. Robinson insists China's growth must eventually derail because political extraction blocks creative destruction, yet he concedes China's meritocratic culture might make it a genuine anomaly. What observable evidence over the next decade would settle which of those two readings is right?
  2. If transitions to inclusive institutions can only be made by countries themselves, as his Chile example argues, what should an outside power actually do when an extractive regime it deals with, like Venezuela's, shows no sign of reforming on its own?
  3. He says the decisive AI question is how productivity gains are spread, and that unions historically did much of that spreading in inclusive societies. With union power much diminished in the United States, what institution does his framework offer to do that work now?

Norges Bank Investment Management · In Good Company · Eric Schmidt

Eric Schmidt: Are We Ready for Superintelligence? | Podcast | In Good Company

On Norges Bank Investment Management's podcast In Good Company, the episode "Eric Schmidt: Are We Ready for Superintelligence?" is a conversation between host Nicolai Tangen, the chief executive of Norway's sovereign wealth fund, and Eric Schmidt, who ran Google from 2001 to 2011 and has since advised governments and written books on AI. Schmidt's framing is blunt: this wave is the rise of nonhuman intelligence, a period people will write about for a thousand years, and it is bigger than the mainframe, PC, internet and social media eras he lived through because it touches everything.

His explanation for why the ocean is rising has two parts. AI systems are platforms, and the compute underneath them keeps scaling: two nanometer chips made largely in Taiwan, big jumps in memory, and data centers so powerful he says they defy human comprehension. The competition at the top is more brutal than the PC against Mac fight or Android against iPhone. He does not buy the bubble argument, pointing to Anthropic's revenue growth, which he says smashed the record Google set under him, growing at more than twice the speed, with real customers paying real money. The cycle is also compressing violently. A frontier model takes roughly six months to build and two months to test, costs hundreds of millions of dollars, and is antiquated within five months. Anthropic and OpenAI trade the lead on deep reasoning, his friends at Google promise the next Gemini will beat them all, and he expects the cycle soon to be measured in days.

The part he calls underhyped is the AI assistant at the top of a company. He describes two founders who ask their assistant every Friday to scan everything happening in the firm and flag anything inconsistent with their morals and values, and an executive who built an AI version of himself that sets tasks for an employee, waits, and issues the next steps when the work lands. He admits it sounds abusive, but says it amplifies decision making and speed. The base case, he argues, holds even for skeptics: ask a system that can reason across the whole business how to make more money, and the world gets more efficient and more profitable whether you like the technology or not. He also points to ten major math problems, previously thought unsolvable, that were solved in a single recent month, arguing that math is the beginning of every next revolution and that a discovery today could surface in a decade as physics or a better battery.

On general intelligence, he draws a line at discovery. Today's systems, paired with a person, can know essentially everything that is known, think for hours, use tools, and write all the code: his own old job as a professional programmer, he says, is dead. What they cannot yet do is discover genuinely new things. Feed a model all the mathematics known in 1902, he asks, and could it produce general relativity? The current evidence says no. He sketches recursive self improvement and two candidate routes to discovery, brute force random search repeated billions of times, or transferring a pattern from one field into another the way a physicist sees fluid flow in an energy problem. His estimate for closing that gap is five to ten years.

Agents are the mechanism that carries this into business. He traces the path from chat systems to tool use to agents, which he defines as an input, a body of reasoning and activity, and a summary, and notes that almost any professional workflow, his example is a travel agent, decomposes into chained agents that could be replaced by software and eventually robots. Inside companies he expects a powerful flattening. He cites Anthropic's Claude Tag sitting in Slack, where each person has their own tag, background tags monitor systems, and a chief executive's tag can survey what everyone else is working on, flag someone exfiltrating data to a competitor, or brief an investment opportunity instantly. Business becomes flatter, smarter, more intense and faster. But he is blunt that almost none of today's large companies will make the cultural changes required, that European organizations are the most change resistant of all, and that startups will pioneer the new shape as usual. The fastest gains, he says, are where learning loops are fast and data is not the constraint: software, cyber and math. Biology will be slow because the data is terrible, even though friends of his believe all human disease could be cured within fifteen or twenty years.

Europe gets his harshest treatment. It is a great place to live, he says, but it is not growing, and in a democracy that is not growing, new needs like defense spending cannot be funded without taking from someone, so politics frays. He names exceptions, Mistral in France, Helsing in Germany, a stronger United Kingdom and the Scandinavian countries, and ASML as proof that Europe can build a company of the first rank. The systemic causes he lists are underfunded universities, a cultural angst about wealth, energy costs among the highest in the world, and regulators who regulate before they innovate. Five years ago Brussels wrote an AI strategy that was a list of prohibitions, while the American strategy he helped write said, in his summary: run as fast as you can, align it with human values, and fix bad things immediately. Talent moves to where it can build, and Europe still has no foundation model of its own, so European history, language and culture are served back to Europeans by American or Chinese models. His remedies are concrete: exempt companies under twenty employees from every rule except health and safety, the proposal he made to President Macron, train models with partner countries to dodge energy costs, sell into Europe's own heavily regulated market as Mistral does, and fix a venture industry too small to turn Europe's many software people into global platform companies.

On risk, he starts with the upside: climate, energy, medicine, long lives. The worst outcome he names is a ruinous competition between China and the West in which control of the systems is lost, which he calls extremely unlikely but possible, and he is part of a team that talks to China to keep things aligned. He recounts a recent incident in which a swarm of bots attacked a website largely on their own and proved hard to stop, calling it a warning, but argues that aviation and electricity were also extraordinarily unsafe at first and became safe once the industry understood the hazards. The problem he says is not solved is open weights: Chinese labs give away their model weights, the guardrails can be stripped out, and the result can be turned to designing weapons. On treaties he is bleakly pragmatic. In his telling, treaties happen after an accident, and others in the industry have said a "mini Chernobyl" is needed, a scare big enough to force regulation without mass harm. It has not happened yet, though a terrorist attack traced to these techniques would, he thinks, do it.

His read on China is that its labs are fast followers, roughly six months behind, reaching the frontier through distillation from American models and chips that were smuggled in. He does not expect them to pull ahead on capability, but argues the West is already losing on adoption because the Chinese products are free: a poor country that wants modernity will run the free model. A recent episode in which access to a leading American model was suddenly cut off, the banning of Mythos that upset his European friends, showed everyone outside the United States how fragile their access is, and he notes that China's GLM from Zhipu may already be close to the frontier, though its closed culture makes that impossible to verify.

Democracy is the one subject where he calls himself a pessimist. His training said democracy rests on classical liberal values, but AI and social media together can make large groups believe things that are false and vote against their own interests. Institutional trust is declining, people trust their online friends, and those friends can be fabricated at scale. He tells of a friend convinced that data centers are killing cattle, a claim with no police or veterinary reports behind it that an algorithm promoted because it was alarming. He was booed giving a commencement speech this year, and notes dryly that the booing got the speech read: the madness of crowds, once moving, is very hard for any individual to answer.

On leadership, he returns to the founder. Professional chief executives like him, he says, never held the power a founder holds: no one but Steve Jobs could have forced Apple's transformation through, and Mark Zuckerberg could spend billions on virtual reality and pivot to AI because he controls his company. Founders are coming back, he thinks, both because the moment is historic and because they want their legacy intact, and leaders now need raw processing speed and fast reactions above all. His own self criticism is that he thinks but reacts too slowly, which he also names as his biggest personal failure, alongside the professional one he owns as Google's chief executive: Google was briefly ahead in social media and he did not get ahead of it. Inside companies, he expects AI to strip out the coordination tax he once paid in the form of twelve hour days of meetings. A friend of his cut a fifth of his staff, the "glue people" who planned and moved numbers around, and received death threats for it. The people still needed are inventors, salespeople, leaders, and people who talk to governments, and he guesses a ten thousand person company may carry a couple of thousand people it no longer needs, who must be transitioned legally and humanely. He also insists, with unusual force, that protecting children from social media harms should be fixed this year, not next.

He closes on his own project. He is running four new companies to create wealth to give to science, remembering the single VAX computer at Berkeley when he was twenty two that underpinned so much of the computer science of its day, and wanting to create that kind of opportunity for the next twenty two year old at a time when his government is cutting support for basic innovation. Scaling industries is his expertise, he says, and now he wants to scale science. He admits he cannot switch off: on a Saturday night he will catch himself thinking he is not working hard enough. His advice to young people is to build an agent system that automates the things they care about, or to build a foundation model for a field they love, his example is architecture, and not to be afraid of the phrase super intelligence. In his definition it simply means systems that do things humans never will, better vision, better reasoning, better driving, in categories from the visual to the robotic.

Open questions

  1. Schmidt expects serious regulation and treaties to follow an accident rather than precede one. What would a "mini Chernobyl" for AI have to look like to produce rules that bind both the United States and China, rather than rules each side writes only for itself?
  2. He counts free Chinese models as a Western loss even while they trail by six months, because most of the world will choose them. If the capability gap stays small, is there any Western strategy short of matching the free price that keeps other countries on Western models?
  3. He calls Europe's problem systemic, spanning capital formation, labor law, energy costs and universities, yet his remedies are modest exemptions and offshore training deals. Given that he also calls European organizations the most change resistant of all, what in this conversation suggests Europe can move at the speed he says the technology now demands?

Mercor · The Frontier · Jonathan Frankle

Jonathan Frankle - Chief AI Scientist at Databricks

On Mercor's podcast The Frontier, host Brendan Foody, the company's chief executive, sits down with Jonathan Frankle, the chief AI scientist at Databricks, co-founder of MosaicML, and the researcher behind the lottery ticket hypothesis. Frankle opens with a line from a talk he gave to an audience of fifteen hundred people: the era of token maxing is over, and the era of value is here. It is a claim he repeats with a wince at his own phrasing, but he means it. After a few months in which enterprises plugged models into everything and watched the bills climb, the questions he now hears from customers are about cost, governance, and security, in that order.

The lottery ticket work is where he starts, because he says he will never escape it. In 2018, during his PhD, deep learning was still arguing with itself about whether the field would be understood through clean theory or through measurement. His papers took the empirical side, and the reviews, which are public, faulted them for offering no proof. His lasting claim is that empiricism won outright. Nobody proves that a model behaves well anymore; they measure its behavior. That is why evaluation exists as a discipline at all, and, as Foody points out, it is why a company like Mercor exists. If model behavior could be proved, there would be nothing to measure.

MosaicML came from a cold email. Naveen Rao wrote to him, in effect: nice paper, want to do a startup on it? Frankle thought the paper would never be valuable in the world, and says he still thinks that was right, but the company evolved into training efficiency and then into custom language models. The pivot happened against his judgment. In the summer of 2022, before ChatGPT, a teammate built out a multi-node training stack after Frankle had told him nobody would ever need more than eight GPUs or a model bigger than twenty five million parameters. The stack worked, the team pivoted, and within a year Mosaic had trained the first open source reproduction of Llama, a seven billion parameter model on a trillion tokens. The Databricks acquisition followed a simple fit, he says: a custom training company needs data, and a data company needs custom training.

At Databricks he describes his research team as the company's risk budget. Other teams hold a portfolio of safe and risky bets; his team is the high risk slice for the whole company, and its job includes failing: reinforcement learning attempts that do not work, products the technology is not ready for, retried a year or two later when the models catch up. He also spends about a quarter of his time with customers, and his measure of success is blunt. The customers are the evals. A benchmark score is not the test; the test is whether someone at a customer adopted the thing, liked it, paid for it, and maybe got promoted for using it.

Asked for the largest bottleneck to ten times more enterprise adoption, he gives a one word answer: eval. It is measurement, not intelligence. In his telling, models have been good enough for a lot of complex enterprise work since Claude Sonnet 3.5, and the problem since then has been specification. Sit the world's smartest person at a desk and tell them to do work, and the task is underspecified; they will fail, and so will a model ten times smarter than anything available. What is missing is describing what you want the AI to do and knowing whether it did it, and both halves of that are evaluation problems.

The way up, he argues, is a ladder borrowed from software. A first agent should be a prototype: a foundation model, some prompt engineering, a handful of example checks, the equivalent of throwing something together in Python to learn whether the thing is worth building at all. Fail fast while it is still cheap. Then climb: automated prompt optimization tools like GEPA against an eval set you have built, a smaller model where one will do, and only once a use case is proven and committed at scale, custom training, which he compares to rewriting the prototype in Rust. Custom models, he says, can cut the cost of a committed use case by five times or more, and he points to Cursor and Harvey as companies now talking about exactly that move.

Enterprises, in his account, are further along than their reputation suggests. He describes a healthcare company's chief executive and finance chief grilling him on the finer points of eval judges, and one medical customer that has annotated twelve thousand examples and built more than seventy calibrated judges, a level of rigor he calls beyond almost anything he has seen anywhere. That customer needs it, because AI mistakes in medicine are dangerous. The unsettled questions are process ones: how much evaluation should be a static offline test set, how much should be daily inspection of traces, and how a weird trace becomes a new permanent test. His metaphor for the moment is that AI software is in its 1960s, still running on punch cards, waiting for its object oriented programming moment.

What rigor means, he explains through driving. A sixteen year old can get a license by driving in a circle and parallel parking, but nobody would put a Waymo on public roads on that standard, because software scales: one change reaches every car at once, and AI has the same property in every workplace it enters. Measurement has to match that scale. In practice it starts small. One example, one question you keep asking, a couple of golden answers, a mediocre judge. The favorite questions everyone asks a new model are the seed of an eval suite, and as the system deploys, the corner cases it hits get added back as tests, the way a carmaker issues a recall and then tests for that failure forever.

If the fix is measurement, an obvious question is why the model labs do not simply build these evals themselves. His answer is trust. An enterprise's data, including the knowledge invested in its people, is its crown jewels, and a lab both wants to help and genuinely needs more data, which makes handing over evals a scary proposition. Databricks, he says, gets to be Switzerland, the place where the data lives and the models visit. Because enterprises will not share, a gap persists between real work and simulated tests, and a large contractor workforce exists partly to help labs approximate the economy's real task distribution. On public benchmarks he is even handed: they give the field a shared measuring stick and ImageNet genuinely advanced the science, but they bend out of shape fast, everyone suspects benchmaxxing, and they skew toward what academia can build, above all coding, which he notes is roughly one percent of the economy. Real enterprise environments run to tens of petabytes; they do not fit in a Docker container, and no academic benchmark can either, which is one reason researchers keep leaving universities for industry scale. Databricks' own contribution, released this week in a new version, is OfficeQA, a document parsing and reasoning benchmark built on United States Treasury documents going back to 1789, Hamilton's paycheck included, which OpenAI now runs against its new models.

Governance, he says, is the part nobody has figured out how to measure. Permissions inside a large company are already a tangle: hundreds of thousands of documents shared too widely because updating access lists is tedious. Humans never go looking through everything they can technically open, but agents do, and an agent with your permissions will find the sensitive document you forgot existed. He nearly caused an incident himself by giving one agent access to Jira and a document store without thinking about what the combination exposed. The lesson he draws is that evaluation has to judge the process, not just whether the task got done: what the agent accessed, whether it should have, and whether the path it took was a reasonable one.

Progress, he admits, has beaten his pessimism. A friend predicted two years ago that AI would be addressing about half of all GitHub issues by 2026 or 2027; Frankle told him he was an idiot, and the friend was right. But the industry's mood has swung from the unbridled optimism of late 2025 to something more fearful, with cost, governance, and security topping customer agendas, and security fears around agent written code and compromised package installs now scaring enterprises away from startup software altogether. He wants the middle held: extraordinary opportunity and real risk at the same time.

On humans, he rejects both replacement stories. Work, he says, is fractal in complexity: there is no such thing as AI for finance, only thousands of small tasks, each different at every company, some understood by perhaps three people in the world. His estimate is that models have automated a very low single digit percentage of such tasks so far. The breakthrough he wants is not smarter models but eval building so cheap and so well designed that those three experts find it delightful rather than extractive, a back and forth that makes them better at their own work. The historical pattern he trusts is agriculture and computing: automate the rote layer and people move up and out to the boundary, the way bankers went from three mergers a year built on punch cards to associates each running three creative ones. Five years out, he expects everyone to work a little more like managers: describing tasks declaratively, checking results, writing process, and thinking critically, because nothing rote will be left to hide in. His hope for his host's industry is the same democratization software went through, evals dropping in cost by orders of magnitude until building one feels like using a modern coding tool. His closing advice to enterprises is to run controlled experiments, let people try things bottom up, and leave room for the person who proves you wrong, since being ignored by his own teammates is how he got both Mosaic's pivot and, he suspects, half of what his team has built since.

Open questions

  1. Frankle says the binding constraint is eval creation, and that the breakthrough needed is tooling that makes it cheap and delightful for the two or three experts who actually understand each task. What would that tooling have to do for those experts to experience building an eval as learning, rather than as having their knowledge extracted into an agent that replaces them?
  2. Enterprises will not share their evals or data with the labs, which is exactly what keeps the gap between real work and simulated tests open. Can an intermediary that hosts the data and measures the models close that gap on its own, or does the gap simply move to whatever the intermediary cannot see?
  3. He expects automation to push humans up a level and out to harder problems, as it did with agriculture and computing, and estimates only a low single digit share of enterprise tasks is automated today. Over the next five years, what would count as evidence that this Goldilocks path is wrong, and that displacement is the real story after all?
