Ryan Peterman · Brian Attwell on big tech and engineering orgs

Atoms CTO: What He Learned Working At Amazon and Google | Brian Attwell

On Ryan Peterman, the episode "Atoms CTO: What He Learned Working At Amazon and Google | Brian Attwell" is a short interview segment between host Ryan Peterman and Brian Attwell, the chief technology officer of Atoms, who previously worked at Google and at Amazon and has spent time on engineering organization design. In just over four minutes, the conversation takes up two questions: why large companies reward their highest performers less than one might expect, and what starting his career at Google taught Attwell about how big tech companies actually run.

Peterman opens with pay. Companies like Amazon, he notes, are famous for not really rewarding their high performers that much, and he asks why. Attwell says a large part of the answer is that they can get away with it. When the stock has gone up a lot, people are mostly happy with the appreciation, so a company can quietly hold back elsewhere. Attwell calls it unjust to pay someone less because their equity went up: that appreciation is the employee's own investment result, and it should not factor into future pay decisions. He says his company made a point of not doing this, and that designing company processes around being just works out better in the long run, though a company has to believe that for the stance to hold.

Peterman then asks what Attwell took from starting out at Google. His answer is skepticism: he says the main thing he got from working at Google was a deep skepticism about how well tech companies are run. In his first week, he noticed a teammate, a man he calls Frank, who did no work. He raised it with his manager, who told him to lay off because Frank was such a nice guy. He raised it with his engineering director, who was a little embarrassed but said much the same thing, that Frank was a really great guy and everybody liked him. Frank was eventually moved off the team, not out of the company, but onto a team where no one would complain because no one on it did any work; they were building demos for the gift shop, or something like that.

Attwell is clear that Frank should have been fired, or at the very least given a serious conversation along the lines of: you have been here a while, you have done good work, we are still paying you, could you do work again? Asked why that hard conversation never happened, he starts with a general answer. At large companies, the performance of the company is fairly decoupled from the performance of individual teams, so it becomes easy for managers to do what is convenient for themselves even when it hurts the company. There is also an energy asymmetry: it takes a lot of energy to raise a standard and very little energy to lower one, and few people complain if everyone is suddenly asked to do less work. With that entropy in the system, standards keep falling over time until the company dies, or until some kind of trauma, such as an existential threat, kicks them back into place. He points to Google's DeepMind team today as a group working hard under exactly that kind of threat to Alphabet.

On top of that, Attwell describes something he calls Google specific. He says that around a decade ago, Google had a policy of keeping as many smart people on staff as possible, even if they were no longer working, because they might work hard again in the future and because Google did not want competitors to have them. He is careful about the status of the claim: he says he cannot prove it existed and never saw it in writing, but a vice president and several managers told him it did, and he knows of no other company that has done it. Peterman asks whether there is any merit in that kind of hiring strategy. Attwell says no, because there are a lot of smart people in the world, and it is fairly difficult to have a monopoly on them. The clip ends there, mid-thought.

Open questions

  1. Attwell says big companies under-reward top performers largely because rising equity lets them get away with it. What happens to that arrangement in a flat or falling stock market, when the appreciation stops masking the pay decisions?
  2. His entropy argument holds that standards fall unless someone spends energy raising them or a crisis resets them. Is there a way for a large company to spend that energy deliberately, rather than waiting for an existential threat?
  3. The claim that Google once kept idle smart people on staff so competitors could not hire them comes with Attwell's own caveat that he never saw it in writing. How much weight should a single insider's unprovable account carry in judging how Google was actually run?

the ZGX Fury local AI station for teams

ZGX Fury - Local AI for a Whole Team

On Sam Witteveen, the episode "ZGX Fury - Local AI for a Whole Team" is a hands-on look at the HP ZGX Fury G1N AI Station, HP's version of NVIDIA's DGX Station, which Witteveen frames as the bigger brother of the desktop DGX Spark. HP sponsored the video and shipped him a loaner unit halfway across the world so he could run his own tests. His central claim is a change of unit: where the Spark or the ZGX Nano sits on one person's desk and serves one person, the Fury sits beside the desk and is built to serve a whole team at once.

The hardware is data center silicon in a tower. Inside is NVIDIA's GB300 chip, the Blackwell Ultra part from the GB300 NVL72 racks, paired with a 72-core Grace CPU, good for 20 petaflops of AI compute at FP4. Memory is the real headline: 748 gigabytes shared between CPU and GPU, of which 252 gigabytes is HBM running at 7.1 terabytes per second. Witteveen contrasts that with the usual local AI choice between unified memory in quantity and a discrete workstation card that is faster but capped near 96 gigabytes per card; NVIDIA's fastest professional card, the RTX Pro 6000, moves data at 1.8 terabytes per second. The Fury, he argues, refuses the trade-off, offering server-class capacity and bandwidth in one box. Two 400 gigabit per second network ports let owners link multiple units together, the machine is liquid cooled, and HP designed it to rack-mount with proper airflow as well as stand beside a desk.

HP's customization is the differentiator he keeps returning to. He says the Fury is the only DGX Station whose maker was allowed to customize what ships on the box. That includes HP Z Runtime, or ZRT, a command line toolkit: setup, serve a named model, and the tool downloads the weights from Hugging Face, configures them for vLLM, and exposes a shareable endpoint, with monitoring, benchmarking for time to first token and throughput under concurrent users, and a quick chat mode for testing. A companion HP Z Toolkit extension brings the box into VS Code. The Fury also ships with Ubuntu but is, he says, the first GB300 station certified for Red Hat Enterprise Linux, the operating system most data center clusters actually run.

The serving numbers come from his own load tests and one partner optimization. In aggregate across many streams he saw over 3,000 tokens per second; in a RAG test with 16 concurrent users on a large Qwen model the box held well over 1,300 tokens per second with the fans not yet kicked in, and simulating 64 users at once it still delivered per-user generation in the high 80s to around 90 tokens per second, passing half a million output tokens in minutes. Separately, Infinity AI used its auto research system to tune a quantization of NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Super specifically for the GB300, and HP ships that build with the machine; on identical hardware it doubles the number of people the box can serve at once and doubles overall token throughput without slowing individual responses.

The pitch closes on the year agentic work changed the shape of demand. Teams no longer just chat with one model; they run an orchestrator model with sub-agent models, embedding, image, and video models, plus sandboxes and tools on the many CPU cores, all at the same time, and all without a per-token API bill. HP's tagline, quoted in the video, is "shareable on-prem AI, scale without token cost." Witteveen adds the control argument: because the box is on-prem, data never leaves the building, which he presents as a requirement rather than a preference in healthcare, government, and manufacturing, and a cybersecurity team chooses its own model and guardrails instead of depending on a closed provider that can refuse a request or quietly change its rules. His verdict is that the Fury is out of reach for most individuals but delivers state-of-the-art on-prem AI for an enterprise or a team, and because it runs the same stack as the big NVIDIA systems, work prototyped on it scales up without a rewrite. A follow-up video will test how many models, including multimodal image and video models, it can run at once.

Open questions

  1. The Fury's case rests on one shared box beating a Spark on every desk, but the video shows throughput tests rather than a team working through a normal week. How do latency and fairness hold up when inference, fine-tuning, and agent jobs from many people collide unpredictably on the same memory and compute?
  2. Infinity AI's tuned Nemotron build doubles concurrent users and throughput on identical hardware. How much of that gain is specific to that one model and quantization, and can the same auto research treatment be repeated for the other models a team would actually want to serve?
  3. The control argument assumes an open model on your own hardware stays capable enough for sensitive work like security research. If frontier closed models pull further ahead, at what capability gap does the on-prem argument start to lose to the API, guardrails and all?

Franklin Templeton, AI and the state of the market

Franklin Templeton CEO on SpaceX, Anthropic, Circular Deals & the State of the Market

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, the episode "Franklin Templeton CEO on SpaceX, Anthropic, Circular Deals & the State of the Market" is a conversation with Jenny Johnson, the chief executive of Franklin Templeton. Johnson's grandfather founded the firm in 1947 with 2.5 million dollars under management; it now manages about 1.8 trillion, and Johnson herself started in the mail room before reaching the top job. Filmed in the firm's New York heritage room, the talk moves from the state of the market to AI, private markets, tokenization, and the acquisition spree that reshaped the company.

Asked where the market stands, Johnson first sets the AI noise aside: the circular financing among hyperscalers and the questions about how they recognize revenue deserve scrutiny, but they are not the whole economy. Inflation looks sticky and rates will likely stay higher, though she notes the current level is unremarkable by long historical standards. The American consumer is still strong, productivity is running around 2 percent, and roughly 70 percent of companies beat their earnings projections, with markets broadening beyond the hyperscalers. She is careful to say the productivity gains owe nothing to AI so far; they come from digitization and cloud tools that have been spreading for two decades, because new technology takes a long time to work through an organization, where the real obstacles are data quality and change management rather than systems integration.

The headwinds she names are geopolitical. The war in the Middle East looks hard to end, a cheap drone can threaten a tanker and its insurance, and yet more oil is moving through the Strait of Hormuz than many expected. She sees no quick resolution in Russia and Ukraine either, and describes the United States and China as constructive rivals who need each other, for rare earths on one side and consumers on the other, while keeping technology fenced off.

On growth, Johnson calls AI the biggest lever and a massive disruptor, but argues as a student of history that the pattern is familiar. BusinessWeek's 1997 cover predicted the internet would eliminate the broker; instead, financial advisers now do more for clients than ever, using the tools rather than being replaced by them. New jobs arrive that nobody imagined, and she gives a small example: wearing Meta glasses on a golf course, she wondered why they could not find her golf ball, and set her programmer son to building an app for it. She also points to biosciences, customized medicine, materials science, and 4D printing as tailwinds nobody talks about as much.

On the bubble question, Johnson recalls the Gartner hype curve from her years running technology: a new technology is hailed as the greatest thing ever, rises, crashes into disillusionment, and then settles where it should be. There is froth in places, she says, and she agrees with Ken Griffin's worry about chip supply and demand. Financing long-lived data centers means betting that today's technology holds for twenty or thirty years, which she calls unusual in a field moving this fast, so she prefers the shorter end of that financing. These are not dot-com companies without revenue, she stresses; the hyperscalers have significant earnings and can pay their debts. Still, she warns that revenue accounting makes comparisons tricky, which is one reason she favors active management.

The conversation then explains how a mutual fund giant ended up owning pieces of SpaceX and Anthropic. Franklin Templeton is headquartered in Silicon Valley, and as companies wait longer to go public, its portfolio managers missed the performance kick they used to get at the IPO. Mutual funds and ETFs may hold up to 15 percent in illiquid assets, run in practice at six to eight percent, so the firm began investing in late stage venture rounds, the rounds that now sit where IPOs used to. Clients who saw the deals asked for a dedicated fund, and a venture group followed. Access, she says, comes from being local: entrepreneurs are neighbors whose kids go to school with yours. On SpaceX itself, she praises Starlink from personal experience at her place in Montana, credits Elon Musk with transforming space investment, and says his Mars goal may well be reachable, with the main risk being how much depends on one person.

Johnson became chief executive just as the firm's biggest deal landed. The 4.5 billion dollar Legg Mason acquisition was announced five days after she took the job and doubled the firm's assets. It filled gaps in core fixed income and real estate through Clarion Partners, brought a private credit manager, and flipped the client mix from three quarters retail wealth to an even split with institutions. Three weeks after announcement, lockdown arrived and markets tanked; she says it never occurred to her to regret the deal, since a good strategic fit is worth riding through a downturn, and it closed sixty days early despite the team building happening over video.

That deal began a run of 17 acquisitions in six years, driven by clients who want to work with fewer managers, which rewards breadth and scale. Johnson says the firm now has nearly the broadest capability set of any asset manager, with infrastructure the one gap left. The deals bought product capability and distribution: Canvas, whose direct indexing technology lets separately managed accounts stay tax efficient, grew from about 2.5 billion dollars when acquired to 30 billion roughly four years later. Lexington Partners more than doubled, Clarion more than doubled, and private credit grew from 26 billion to just under 100 billion through bolt-on deals, most recently an asset backed origination team. Most deals were cash, sometimes with debt; the Putnam deal was largely Franklin stock, because its owner wanted diversified exposure to a scaled manager, and Putnam's assets have since grown from about 136 billion to over 220 billion. She adds that private market carry, paid out over years, gives acquirers a natural retention that traditional managers lack.

Johnson then makes her case for tokenization, drawing on her years running technology and operations. She believes blockchain will replace the rails of financial services because it does three things well: it is a single source of truth, updated immediately instead of through overnight batch processing; it makes agreements programmable through smart contracts, so collateral moves automatically; and it carries payments. Franklin ran the first SEC approved money market fund issued natively onchain, and when it processed in parallel with the old recordkeeping system, the cost gap was stark: a traditional account needs a 500 dollar minimum before other shareholders subsidize it, while the onchain account opens at 20 dollars and could go to 5. The fund accrues interest every second, so a hedge fund holding it as collateral for a few hours earns exactly that slice, and investors can reach their cash daily rather than at month end.

Inside the firm, Johnson describes more than a thousand AI agents working alongside over 800 investment professionals, in three buckets. For investing, agents strip administrative work from analysts so they spend time with companies, and even a small performance gain dwarfs any cost saving. For distribution, a multi-agent system built with Microsoft pulls product, holdings, social, and ratings data together so salespeople meet the right clients; early results show a 23 percent uplift in client visits and an 11 percent increase in sales. For the back office, AI writing code simply moves more projects through a pipeline that is always constrained by prioritization. She closes the loop back to the hyperscalers with a caution about their revenue: companies will eventually govern which agents run and how often, and route many queries to free models instead of the top tier, and that discipline will shape what the AI builders actually earn.

The talk ends with history and a verdict. Her grandfather built a brokerage and named a subsidiary for Benjamin Franklin; her father took it over in 1955, when it held 2.5 million dollars and earned 10,000 dollars a year, and the firm did not reach a billion until the early 1980s. The founding idea was to give ordinary people pooled access to equity returns they could not reach alone, and Johnson sees the same democratizing impulse now in private markets, with the caveat that those vehicles are genuinely illiquid. Asked who the modern day Benjamin Franklin is, she chooses Elon Musk: inventor, public figure, and, in her words, the person who has had more significant impact on where technology stands today than anyone.

Open questions

  1. Johnson prefers shorter term financing for data centers because betting on today's technology for decades is risky, yet the buildout depends on long term capital. If informed lenders keep shortening maturities, who ends up holding the long dated risk in the AI infrastructure boom?
  2. She predicts a governance layer will route many employee queries to free models and prune how often agents run, cutting into hyperscaler revenue. How quickly will that discipline actually arrive inside large companies, and which AI sellers are most exposed when it does?
  3. Johnson argues blockchain rails can cut the cost of serving a small investor from a 500 dollar minimum account to 20 dollars or less. If tokenized funds reach that economics at scale, what happens to the pricing of ordinary mutual funds that still run on overnight batch systems?

Ondas Sentinel and sovereign AI

Ondas Sentinel: Sovereign AI Is National Security | Sovereignty Bootcamp 3

On the Palantir channel, the Sovereignty Bootcamp 3 talk "Ondas Sentinel: Sovereign AI Is National Security | Sovereignty Bootcamp 3" opens with a simple claim: whoever controls the AI that runs a country's national security, defense, or critical infrastructure ultimately controls a significant portion of that country's sovereignty. Today, the speaker notes, many of the models behind those systems were built elsewhere, trained on data the user does not own, and governed by terms the user does not set. For nations that take sovereignty and security seriously, he argues, that is a risk they can no longer accept.

That argument is the reason Regna AI exists. The talk presents sovereign AI as essential to national security, and says governments and businesses need open-weight models built for defense and critical infrastructure, models that allied nations and allied entities can trust and customize. Trust, in this telling, is not a vendor's promise. Trust is transparency: the ability to inspect and adapt a system, and confidence that it will keep working when the network goes down or the environment turns hostile.

The talk then lays out the trade-off governments are stuck with. They want to move faster with AI, but commercial providers own the models, the data, and the training of those models, and mission data cannot freely cross security boundaries. Building a model from scratch can cost hundreds of millions, if not billions, of dollars, and take years, so most institutions cannot justify the expense or the time. The speaker's view is that they should not have to.

Regna AI is offered as the way out. It is a standalone business powered by Palantir and bootstrapped by Ondas, and its job is to help customers develop and deploy mission-specialized, fine-tuned models inside their own sovereign environments. Those models are designed for edge hardware, from drones and stratospheric balloons to ground vehicles, surface vessels, and command and control nodes, and they are built to keep working through denied, degraded, or intermittent connectivity.

The partnership structure is deliberate, because, as the talk puts it, no one delivers the entirety of a sovereign AI solution alone. Ondas is Regna's first customer: it deploys the fine-tuned models that run autonomous missions orchestrated at the edge, and it brings a fleet of hardware, from stratospheric balloons to unmanned aircraft, into an operational environment. Palantir provides the model training, the infrastructure, and the operational platform, and works alongside Regna on forward-deployed engineering to tailor capabilities to each customer's workflow. Regna itself brings the open-weight models and the sovereign deployment approach together, running from the model all the way to the mission.

The first foundational product shown is Skyweaver C4I, an open architecture system that Ondas and Palantir have been working on for years. It integrates layered surveillance from the stratosphere to the tactical edge, and it gives Regna a working base for mission-specialized models across multiple domains and in denied environments.

To show what that means in practice, the talk walks through a scenario. A mission operator passes a secure tip-off about a high-speed vessel moving along the Vietnam-China border in the Gulf of Tonkin, possibly tied to illegal fishing. Skyweaver's retasking agents turn that natural language command into a new mission plan and reroute assets to the area of interest. A maritime detection system works out what the boat is doing and where it is going, and starts collecting additional data from AIS signals and radio frequency data, all processed at the edge to assess the threat. Skyweaver then surveys the other assets in the area, weighs their remaining endurance based on the fuel on board and the types of sensors each aircraft or drone carries, picks the right one to collect still more data, and assembles a decision package for the mission operator.

The key point of the scenario is where the authority sits. The models assess evidence, compare options, and propose changes. A human reviews and authorizes every decision. All of it happens at the edge, it keeps working through any disconnection, and it produces a decision packet the operator can audit and fine-tune line by line before releasing a decision. The decision and the evidence stay with the operator, which the speaker calls sovereignty in practice.

The close is aimed at allied nations: the question, he says, is no longer whether to adopt sovereign AI, but who to build it with. Regna is starting with defense and intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance, and plans to expand into security, critical infrastructure, and industrial markets.

Open questions

  1. Which open-weight models will Regna fine-tune for these missions, and how will their performance be judged against the frontier commercial models that governments are being asked to go without?
  2. What does ownership actually mean for a customer here: does the customer own the fine-tuned weights outright, and what happens to a deployment if the partnership behind it changes?
  3. As Regna expands from defense into critical infrastructure and industrial markets, will the same rule hold that a human reviews and authorizes every decision the models propose?

Why robots did not take the factory jobs

Ex-Yahoo Insider: Robots Were Supposed to Take Factory Jobs. What Happened?

On Inside the Silicon Mind with Firas Sozan, the episode "Ex-Yahoo Insider: Robots Were Supposed to Take Factory Jobs. What Happened?" is a conversation with Devin Bhushan, the founder and chief executive of Squint, an industrial intelligence platform for manufacturers. Bhushan's answer to the question in the title is that the robots were never the bottleneck. The knowledge of how a factory actually runs lives inside people's heads, and the generation holding that knowledge is about to retire. Customers tell him the average operator on their plant floors is 57 or 58 years old, often someone who has held one job for more than 30 years and built the processes personally. When that one person takes a vacation, plants sometimes shut down that part of the line entirely, because nobody else knows how to run it, or even how to read an order number and kick the process off.

Bhushan calls this tribal knowledge, and he argues the real race in industrials is capturing it before it walks out the door. His company, Squint, starts by recording expert operators at work. The platform watches the video, works out which processes the person performed during a shift, segments them, and writes step by step documentation on its own, capturing recipes, adjustments, precautions, protective equipment, materials, and tools. It then combines that applied knowledge with the manufacturer's equipment manuals, which can run to thousands of pages, and with data from systems such as SharePoint and SAP, including work order history. The result is what he calls an industrial brain, a context layer that an operator can query on a tablet mid task, with agents that can also take work off operators' plates, for instance by running a time and motion analysis over a 12 hour shift video so engineers can focus on improvements.

The first customer's reaction explains the appeal. At a three person company, on what Bhushan remembers as the first sales call that went well, the customer's eyes lit up at a single question: did the software just watch a video of him and write the guide for how to do that task? He had spent decades writing work instructions by hand, or paying staff whose job was documentation, and every process change meant writing them again to stay compliant. Squint now works with large manufacturers and energy companies, naming PepsiCo, Continental, and Michelin among its customers, typically firms scaling operations across hundreds of facilities and tens of thousands of people.

Bhushan's path to the factory floor ran through consumer software. He built fantasy sports products at Yahoo, where sending a push notification to every affected player the moment someone scored a touchdown taught him to build for scale. He then followed his mentor Tim Tully to Splunk, where his job was bringing data analytics to the industrial workforce. The Splunk approach showed operators sensor data, such as a motor's speed running out of range. What he learned is that the data was not the insight. The insight was what an experienced operator knew to do next: the fault code, the cause, and the fix. That knowledge sat nowhere in any system, so in 2021, before ChatGPT but after the transformer paper made clear that context would power generative AI, he and colleagues from Splunk and Yahoo left to build the context layer first.

Two management lessons run through the conversation. From Tully, who moved him to a new team every time he got comfortable, starting when he was 22, Bhushan took the rule that comfort is complacency: as soon as you feel settled, someone else is about to win, so keep seeking out the uncomfortable parts. From running Squint, he preaches owning the outcome rather than the process. A deployment is not a success because usage numbers look good; it is a success when a customer's downtime actually falls. He feels strongly enough about this that Squint is moving toward outcome based pricing, charging for results like reduced downtime instead of per user fees. Early on, serving a Fortune 500 customer as a five person company, the team described itself as five raccoons in a trench coat: coordinated, adaptable, and one bad step from tumbling apart.

Looking ten years out, Bhushan does not expect lights out factories. AI and robotics will both transform the floor, but much of manufacturing is not assembly in the way people imagine. Making a potato chip, he points out, involves baking, subjectivity, and something close to craft, and two factories making the same product visibly differ in judgment and taste, in their attention to quality, brand, and pride in the result. His vision is a mix of humans and robots, with Squint as the orchestration layer that knows which tasks robots can do, tells them what process to follow, and plugs people in for the judgment calls. The reason robots cannot simply replace the workforce today, he argues, is that nobody has written down what the workforce knows, so there would be no way to direct them. He closes with a book recommendation, The Goal, the business novel about a factory manager who turns a failing plant around by finding its bottlenecks, copies of which sit in the Squint office.

Open questions

  1. Squint's pitch depends on capturing expertise from operators who are 57 or 58 on average. How much of that knowledge can video and documentation really hold, and how much of it is judgment that only shows up when something unexpected breaks?
  2. Bhushan predicts human and robot mixed factories rather than lights out plants, with a context layer orchestrating both. If that layer becomes the industry's control point, who ends up owning it, the manufacturers, the platform, or the robot makers?
  3. Outcome based pricing ties Squint's revenue to measured results like downtime reduction. How should a customer and a vendor agree on what caused an improvement, especially when the customer's own changes happen at the same time?

Everyone having their own database

Why Everyone Is About To Have Their Own Database

On Y Combinator, the episode "Why Everyone Is About To Have Their Own Database" is a Lightcone conversation with Paul Copplestone, the co-founder and chief executive of Supabase, the open source Postgres backend. The numbers he gives describe a company being pulled upward by agents. Supabase now has 13 million signups, up from roughly 3 million a year ago, revenue is on track to grow fourfold this year, and the platform spins up over a million new databases a week. Databases under management are approaching 50 million, against 8 million a year ago, and something like 70 percent of the new activity now comes from agents rather than people.

Copplestone describes three chapters in the company's life. Supabase began as a dashboard company, became an API company, and is now, in his words, a code first company, because agents build with code and command line tools rather than dashboards. Being open source was, he believes, critical to being chosen: when an agent gets stuck it can read the source, and enterprises increasingly want to inspect the code of anything they adopt. He also credits timing, since Supabase had become a default database choice inside the large models just as agent building took off.

The newer shift is who is building. Alongside software engineers, a fast growing segment of builders has no engineering background at all, and unusually, activation and conversion have risen alongside raw signups rather than falling. Copplestone's favourite example is a taxi driver in Brazil, a man of about 50, who built an entire taxi app for his local market from his phone and turned up at a Supabase conference to say thank you. The hosts argue that domain experts are the ideal builders of this wave: they already know which problems in their niche are unsolved, and they no longer have to persuade an engineer to build for them.

The title's claim grows out of personal software. One host describes his own "control tower," a single app holding his email triage, finances, health tracking, and files, with feature parity to dedicated finance products and cross-context touches no commercial app offers, such as excusing a weight gain because his calendar shows he was traveling. Finance and health, Copplestone says, are the two areas where people most want their own data in one place they control. The pattern underneath is ownership: your personal CRM and records live in a database you own, and any agent can be attached on top, instead of being boxed inside one agent vendor's store.

Asked whether Postgres can survive the resulting scale, Copplestone does some arithmetic: if a chat product with 1.2 billion weekly users gave each of them a database a month, that is over a billion new databases a month. His answer is a two-ended architecture. A recent acquisition, Turso, brings a Postgres compatible design that also works with SQLite, and it powers a new tier called Supabase Spark for small getting started workloads, under a tagline of prototype to petabytes. At the other end, Multigres serves the scale up enterprise workloads. He concedes that serving both ends at once, at this growth rate, is the hardest problem the company has.

Inside Supabase, the agent shift is just as steep. The company has no AI policy and simply gives engineers whatever tools they want, and a recurring internal poll shows the share of engineers writing 80 percent of their code with coding agents rising from 10 percent to 60 percent in six months. A growing share of engineering effort now goes into testing and evaluation rather than writing features, because the models produce so much that knowing the result is good has become the bottleneck.

Winning the agents, Copplestone says, has two stages: being picked by the model in the first place, and then not being abandoned when the agent gets stuck. The concrete work is unglamorous, such as rewriting error messages so that instead of a bare failure code they tell the agent what went wrong and how to fix it, or where to read about fixing it. Supabase runs evaluation suites across models to see which ones build best with Postgres, and builds skills so models can adopt its conventions.

Host Garry Tan describes his own GBrain, a personal retrieval system built on Supabase that turns half a million markdown files of his communications into a searchable graph and vector store an agent can query, with agents looping against an evaluation set to improve retrieval on their own. The discussion treats this as an early sketch of a general pattern: a durable, structured store of record sitting between a person and whichever agents they happen to use.

The closing advice is about durability and adaptability. Copplestone leans on the Lindy effect: databases have been around for 50 years and, whatever AI does, he is confident they will be around for 50 more, so the category is worth betting a career on. Beyond category choice, he says Supabase tries to adapt faster than anyone else, dogfooding its own tools to find the gaps, partnering with the key players in the ecosystem, and deliberately leaving capacity in the team so new bets can be tried quickly, knowing most of them will flop and a few will let the company ride a wave. He ends where the conversation began, predicting that everyone in the world with access to AI will become a builder of some sort, and saying he is genuinely pumped about 2027.

Open questions

  1. The personal database vision assumes people want one store of record they own, with agents attached on top. Will mainstream users actually maintain such a thing, or will the convenience of letting each agent vendor keep the data win, leaving the owned database a power user pattern?
  2. Copplestone's scale arithmetic points toward billions of mostly idle personal databases. Can a Postgres compatible architecture really serve that many tiny, sporadically used databases cheaply enough, or does the economics of the Spark tier depend on most of them never growing?
  3. If being picked by the model becomes the main distribution channel for developer tools, what stops the model providers from favouring their own or partners' backends, and how durable is a default position once the models change?
