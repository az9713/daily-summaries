Brock Mesarich · AI for Non Techies · Claude Duet

I Tested Claude Duet and It’s… Good??

On Brock Mesarich's channel AI for Non Techies, the video "I Tested Claude Duet and It’s… Good??" is a first hands-on test of Claude Duet, Anthropic's new personal AI assistant. Mesarich did not get early access. He came off the waitlist, sat down in front of the camera openly unsure how he felt about the product, and tested it live.

Duet presents itself as an assistant that works across a person's whole digital life. Inside the app it can reach files and documents, mail, calendar, and team chat. A payments section exists, although the assistant cannot actually make payments yet. Its welcome message sets the pitch: today is the first day of working together, it will get to know him a little better every day, and over time it will start spotting what he needs before he asks.

The first test is voice mode. The spoken replies are quick, with low latency, and when Mesarich asks what is on his calendar, Duet answers from the calendar itself: two open days, then a Saturday flight home from Lisbon to Washington and on to Dallas. It is a small moment, but it establishes the idea that the assistant is wired into his real accounts rather than a demo sandbox.

The interface borrows its organizing idea from Slack. Channels give each topic its own place for messages, tasks, and files, so his work does not collapse into one endless conversation. He creates a channel for his YouTube videos, and Duet offers to suggest tasks it could take on. What impresses him most is where Duet's context comes from: it can pull from the database he built inside Claude Code holding his past videos and ideas, which means the context from his earlier work in Claude Code, Claude chat, and Cowork follows him into the new app.

That shared memory turns into visible output. He asks for a dashboard of his previous three short-form videos, and Duet builds one from the stored analytics, complete with views, likes, and engagement, alongside a note that his channel brought in about 1.35 million views in the last twenty-eight days. A separate section called Make collects every artifact the assistant produces, from dashboards to presentations and documents, including ones he made earlier in other Claude products.

The tasks view is the other piece he keeps returning to. It shows what the assistant is working on, what is ready for his review, and what is up next. Crucially, it can add items he never asked for: if an important email arrives, Duet can spot it and put the follow-up on tomorrow's list. That proactive streak, he suggests, is what could make the experience feel different from an ordinary chatbot.

The clearest gap shows up in integrations. Mesarich wants to ask about his email marketing results from his phone, but the tool he uses, Drip, has no connector in Claude, and there is currently no way to add connectors from inside Duet itself. His workaround is to route through Zapier's MCP service, which advertises connections to more than nine thousand apps. Once Drip is connected that way, he asks Duet on his phone for the last campaign's numbers and gets back a dashboard with the open rate, click-through rate, unsubscribe rate, delivery rate, and where readers dropped off.

All of this is measured against the tool he actually lives in. Mesarich has used a rival assistant called Grok Bot every day for two months to help run his business, and calls it his favorite AI tool he has ever used. Its design is almost the opposite of channels: several specialized agents, including a main agent, an invoice agent, and a YouTube agent, that talk to each other and share context, wrapped in a simple, colorful, beginner-friendly interface.

His early verdict is split. He prefers Grok Bot's cooperating agents to Duet's channels, which he finds less clean, while Duet wins on the Make section, because Claude's talent for building artifacts has no equivalent in Grok Bot, and on the tasks view with its proactive additions. Two caveats hang over everything: he has only used Duet for a few hours, and only on his phone, while most of his real work happens at a computer. For viewers, access is a waitlist signup, and for now you have to be in the United States to get in.

Open questions

  1. Will Duet's proactive habit of adding tasks on its own, like flagging an important email for tomorrow, prove genuinely useful over weeks of real work, or will it turn into noise he has to manage?
  2. Can topic channels match the clarity of Grok Bot's separate cooperating agents, or will Anthropic eventually move Duet toward a team of specialized agents instead?
  3. Will the gaps he hit close quickly, meaning connectors that can be added inside Duet itself, working payments, and a desktop experience as strong as the phone, or will workarounds through services like Zapier stay necessary?

Big Think Clips · Ethan Siegel · The fate of the universe

Cosmology’s oldest question: How the universe will end | Ethan Siegel

On Big Think Clips, astrophysicist Ethan Siegel takes up the question that drove much of twentieth century physics: how will the universe end? By the end of that century, cosmologists thought they had the answer. The universe would expand forever, with bound structures drifting ever farther apart until only whatever each one was gravitationally tied to remained. Siegel's point is that this fate is no longer certain, because the dark energy driving the expansion may itself be changing.

The classic picture is a race between two forces. Cosmic expansion, which began with the hot Big Bang, works to drive everything apart. Gravity works to pull everything back together. Working from Einstein's general relativity, Alexander Friedmann showed in 1922 that the contents of the universe decide which force wins.

That gives three possible fates. If there is enough matter and gravity, expansion slows, halts at a maximum size, and reverses into a collapse Siegel calls the big crunch, the Big Bang run backwards. If expansion wins, the universe coasts outward forever, growing at an ever slower rate. In the Goldilocks case the two forces balance so precisely that the expansion rate approaches zero without ever reversing.

In the 1990s, measurements of how the expansion rate has changed over time ruled out all three. The universe followed the Goldilocks path for billions of years, and then, about 6 billion years ago, the expansion stopped slowing and began to speed up. Today every galaxy farther than roughly 16 to 18 billion light-years away is receding from us faster than the speed of light, and Siegel notes that about 94 percent of what we can observe is already unreachable, even for a signal sent at light speed.

The expected fate that follows is a cold, empty universe, often called the big freeze. Our Local Group, which includes the Milky Way, Andromeda, the Magellanic Clouds, and perhaps a hundred smaller galaxies, stays bound together. Every other galaxy, group, and cluster accelerates away and eventually disappears from view.

The acceleration is powered by dark energy, a form of energy inherent to space itself, first revealed by observations of distant supernovae. Unlike matter and radiation, which thin out as the universe grows, dark energy appeared to keep a constant density, which is why it has come to dominate and now dictates the universe's future.

The wildcard is that dark energy may not be constant after all. Some recent evidence suggests it is weakening today. If it decays away entirely, or even reverses sign, distant galaxies would stop accelerating away, and the universe could yet end in a big crunch after all.

The opposite change would be just as dramatic. If dark energy grows stronger, it would eventually tear apart structures that are bound today: the Local Group, the Milky Way, the solar system. In Siegel's telling, the final moments would see stars, planets, and finally atoms and subatomic particles themselves ripped apart, an ending known as the big rip.

The evidence comes from a fossil ruler. In the hot early universe, pressure from photons pushed back against collapsing normal matter, imprinting a preferred distance scale, about 500 million light-years today, at which galaxies are more likely to be found near one another. By measuring that acoustic scale in huge galaxy surveys across cosmic time, astronomers can reconstruct how the expansion has proceeded. The recent data shows the scale is not growing as fast as a constant dark energy predicts, which is what hints that dark energy is weakening or otherwise changing how it affects the universe.

Open questions

  1. Is the hint that dark energy is weakening a real physical effect, or a measurement artifact that larger and cleaner galaxy surveys will eventually erase?
  2. If dark energy can change strength, and perhaps even direction, what underlying physics sets its value, and does any current theory actually predict the change the surveys seem to see?
  3. Could observations ever settle which of the three endings is ours with real confidence, or will the fate of the universe remain a matter of probabilities no matter how good the data gets?

MTS · Ben Woodington · AI-powered brain implants

AI-Powered Brain Implants Will Treat Brain Cancer | Ben Woodington

On MTS, the episode "AI-Powered Brain Implants Will Treat Brain Cancer | Ben Woodington" is a conversation with Ben Woodington, the co-founder and chief executive of Coherence Neuro. He joined the show on the day the company announced a 45 million dollar Series A, led by Founders Fund and the OpenAI Startup Fund. Coherence builds electrical devices that interface with biology: they record electrical activity from the body and use electrical stimulation to influence it.

The first product is a brain implant for brain cancer. It is a small glass device, about the size of a fingertip, with thin thread-like leads that connect to the brain so the company can record from it and stimulate it. External peripherals move data out of the body. The implant is not the goal in itself. It is a way to listen to a tumor and, over time, to act on what the recordings show.

The surgical flow is what makes this practical now. Coherence's patients are already very unwell and already having brain surgery, often at UCSF. The surgeon simply leaves the implant behind when the operation ends, so there is no second surgery. The patient goes home, and the company can then work with the disease remotely, adjusting stimulation settings over a wireless link and uploading the data the device captures.

Asked why this is possible in 2026 and was not a few years ago, Woodington points to two curves. On the hardware side, the pace of innovation in brain-computer interfaces and neurotechnology has rocketed over the past five to ten years, helped by new materials and by electronics that can be made far smaller than before. On the data side, modern AI and large-scale data processing have transformed what his neuroscience team can do. Work that once meant people grinding through data by hand is now automated, and he estimates the team's output has multiplied several times over in just the last two or three years.

He is careful to place Coherence in one of two camps. Some brain-computer interface companies aim to augment healthy people, helping them control cursors or computers. Coherence belongs to the therapeutic camp, alongside work on depression, Parkinson's, addiction, brain injury, and stroke. The underlying technologies are fundamentally the same, he says. The difference is the direction you push them in, and Coherence has pointed its stack at cancer, starting with brain cancer and looking toward cancers throughout the body.

The science underneath the company is cancer neuroscience, a field that barely existed a decade ago. Woodington began a PhD in neurotechnology around 2017, and his co-founder Elise, working in the same lab, drew his attention to a strange finding: tumors do not sit in the body in isolation. They form communication networks with the brain and nervous system, talking to the body in its own electrical language, in both directions. Until roughly seven or eight years ago, he says, nobody knew this dimension of tumors existed.

That finding shapes everything the device does. If a tumor is wired into the nervous system, then an electrode recording from a nerve can reveal what is happening inside the tumor and how it responds to treatment, and stimulation delivered through the same interface can modulate it. Woodington also stresses how different every tumor is, which is why the flood of data matters: electrical recordings alongside genetic and other measurements are what allow new therapies to be designed, rather than guessed at.

The bottleneck, in his telling, is not engineering. His team moves exceptionally fast, and neurosurgeons are eager adopters who already operate with virtual and augmented reality tools and were, he says, no hard sell at all. The constraint is regulators, and the pace at which studies that must run for five years or more can move through approval. He understands the public frustration at hearing about cures that never arrive, but from inside the field he is optimistic: the next decade or two of medicine, he thinks, will be unrecognizable.

The conversation closes with what comes next. A clinical study is already running in Melbourne, Australia, and a first long-term study is coming, in which patients keep the implant for the rest of their lives. A new lab in South San Francisco, relocating from Palo Alto, will test the same approach across other disease models, including pancreatic cancer, training systems that learn how to stimulate and control biology more effectively. On the wider field, he notes that the number of patients worldwide using devices like these went from about five to somewhere between thirty and fifty very quickly, and he expects that population to keep doubling or tripling.

He also touches on the question everyone asks: whether implants become consumer products. Neuralink and others clearly believe they will, and in a lighthearted survey at his own company's offsite, some colleagues guessed a consumer-grade implant could arrive within seven to twelve years. Woodington would not rule it out, and notes that people accept inherent risks in everything from cosmetic surgery to alcohol. But Coherence itself, he stresses, is aimed at the hard diseases in front of it now, not at consumers.

Open questions

  1. The device records from tumors and stimulates them, and the company plans systems that learn to treat more effectively over time. What evidence, and over what timescale, would show that this closed loop actually changes outcomes for brain cancer patients rather than simply measuring their disease in far greater detail?
  2. Woodington names regulators as the binding constraint and asks for ways to move faster. Which parts of the approval path for a permanently implanted, remotely adjustable device can genuinely be accelerated, and which delays are doing real safety work that speed would put at risk?
  3. If tumors throughout the body communicate with the nervous system the way brain tumors do, does the same implant approach generalize to cancers like pancreatic cancer, or does each cancer's wiring demand a fundamentally different device and evidence base?

Dwarkesh Patel · Intel fab tour · The future of computer chips

What will computer chips look like in 2045?

On the Dwarkesh Podcast, Dwarkesh Patel tours Intel's fabs and research labs for the episode "What will computer chips look like in 2045?", interviewing the people who build Intel's process technology: Chris Auth, who leads development of the processes now in manufacturing, integration lead Kevin Fischer, research scientist Jack Kavalieros, advanced packaging director Tyler Osborn, and Intel CTO Pushkar Ranade.

The occasion is a demand shock. AI has pushed computing demand, in Auth's words, easily 40 to 50 percent above Intel's maximum capacity, something he has never seen before. Because AI progress has been driven by exponentially growing compute, Intel has to make far more leading-edge chips, and make each chip better, at the same time.

Auth walks through where the 14A process stands. It has been in development for more than three years and is now close to final: the tool set is being locked and each of the roughly 2,000 steps in the flow is being fine-tuned so the line can run thousands of wafers a week, repeatably. Yield climbs through a loop of inline metrology, electrical test, and physical cross-sections of defects, which pinpoint which section of the flow a failure came from.

The governing lesson comes from the 10 nanometer debacle, a node targeted for 2016 that did not manufacture until 2018. Intel tried to hyperscale without new tools, skipped EUV as not ready, and loaded the process with innovation. The technology ended up excellent but years late. The response is discipline: maturity metrics and phase gates that a technology must pass to enter a node, and a rule against packing too much novelty into any single step.

That discipline matters because each transistor generation takes decades to mature. The episode traces the arc from the planar transistor of 1960, to FinFET, which wraps the gate around three sides of a silicon fin, to gate-all-around, which slices the channel into ribbons the gate fully surrounds. Gate-all-around was demonstrated in 1988, a year before FinFET, yet reached production at Samsung only in 2022, because stacking new materials, placeholder layers, and high-k dielectrics into high-volume manufacturing is slow, iterative work.

The research culture changed with the end of Dennard scaling. Where the 1980s and 1990s simply shrank the same flow, Auth describes a golden age of invention since the 2000s: strained silicon, high-k metal gate, FinFET, gate-all-around, backside power, and a materials palette that has grown roughly tenfold, controlled atom by atom.

Lithography shows how the same tool can be right for one maker and wrong for another. Intel uses High-NA EUV for 14A; TSMC says it does not yet make economic sense. The difference is backside power: by moving power lines beneath the transistors, Intel loosens front-side pitch enough that a single High-NA pass suffices, while TSMC, with tighter pitches, would still need double patterning. High-NA is left as an option in the 14A design kit, decided customer by customer.

Becoming a foundry is its own learning curve. For decades Intel shared transistor data informally with its own product teams and had no formal process design kit. 18A started without one, customers pushed back, and 14A is being built to ship with a mature PDK from the start.

Asked how different 2045 could look, Auth points upward. Once transistors float in interconnect with power delivered from behind, the natural question is why not stack transistors and wiring in repeated layers, the way NAND memory already does. The next step is CFET, stacking the n-type and p-type transistors of each pair directly on top of each other. The obstacle is heat: logic burns far more power than memory, and stacked layers trap it. Auth names the three limits on compute as switching speed, power per switch, and getting the heat out.

Fischer's Hot Topics meetings are where yield problems meet finite engineering time. Engineers bring experiments, integration decides how much risk to take without complete data, and priority lots called hot boxes race through the fab at the cost of overall efficiency. One live problem: epitaxy nodules, tumor-like growths that short neighboring source and drain crystals, attacked in parallel from several directions at once.

In the research lab, Kavalieros works on what replaces copper wiring. As copper lines shrink, the liner they need eats the conduction path; ruthenium needs no liner and wins below roughly a 20 nanometer pitch. Intel once bet on cobalt, then watched copper come back; every node is a fresh contest among materials judged against integration, not just conductivity. He also explains why a working lab version of CFET is still a decade from a phone: a simplified one-ribbon stack has to evolve, step by qualifying step, until it beats the side-by-side technology it would replace, and most research ideas die on something as basic as a film nothing will stick to.

Packaging is where stacking already ships. Osborn describes hybrid bonding, fusing two chips' copper interconnects directly without solder bumps. First-level interconnect pitch has fallen from around 140 micrometers fifteen years ago to 25 micrometers in current production, sub-10 in development, and a published 0.45 micrometer result points to another order of magnitude. Chip-to-wafer bonding, which allows known good dies to be placed deliberately, looks central for logic.

Ranade closes on capacity. ASML makes fewer than 100 EUV tools a year and does not plan to change that, every other tool is in shortage too, and a locked process flow cannot swap suppliers. Squeezing the installed base might double leading-edge wafer output; ten times is not available, while AI compute demand has been compounding far faster. Scale is the industry's purest economics, he argues: a fab's costs are mostly fixed, so utilization and yield gains are worth fighting for, which is why only three advanced foundries remain.

The wider picture he paints is an industry swinging from horizontal back toward virtual vertical integration, with hyperscalers designing chips and single model companies able to consume whole fabs. Intel's partnership with Elon Musk on Terafab follows from that scarcity. And because fab costs double every four to five years while a High-NA scanner package runs about 400 million dollars, Ranade ends by questioning inherited practice itself, down to whether clean rooms need to be as elaborate as they are, in an industry too risk-averse to run that experiment.

Open questions

  1. If EUV tool supply stays capped near 100 machines a year, who effectively decides how the world's leading-edge wafer capacity gets allocated among Intel, TSMC, Samsung, and the memory makers?
  2. Can CFET and 3D-stacked logic beat the thermal wall, or does heat cap logic stacking long before the density gains run out?
  3. If a few billion dollars of fab capital spending can underpin a hundred billion dollars a year of AI revenue, does that value eventually flow down to the foundries and toolmakers, or keep pooling at the top of the stack?

Latent Space · Anekha Sokhal · Recommendation engines for traders

The Recommendation Engine for Commodity Traders — Anekha Sokhal, Moshi

On Latent Space, the episode "The Recommendation Engine for Commodity Traders — Anekha Sokhal, Moshi" is a studio conversation with Anekha Sokhal, the founder of Moshi, recorded around the AI Engineer conference, where she gave the event's first finance-focused talk. Sokhal is a former JP Morgan derivatives trader, and the host is a former trader himself, so much of the conversation is two people comparing how trading actually works with the tools each of them wished they had.

Her path into trading started with a degree at Warwick in mathematics, operational research, statistics, and economics, the combination now retroactively read as an AI degree. She interned in investment banking, joined JP Morgan on the emerging markets desk, and moved into commodities, spending most of her career in energy derivatives. Because of the data background, she says, she always wanted to break a market down into its component parts: the fundamentals, the news flow, and the physical events underneath the prices.

The founder instinct came early. At university she side-hustled a computer vision startup for virtual jewelry try-on, an idea that grew out of her parents' jewelry business. To reach the American venture scene she won a Fulbright scholarship, the only one of its kind offered in the United Kingdom that year, and her interview happened to land in 2020, when COVID had sent gas markets and household energy bills haywire, which made her pitch about data and the energy crisis easy for the panel to feel. In the United States she went deep into machine learning research, publishing at ICLR with Stability AI and collaborating with NASA, and that work led her to Moshi.

Moshi, she explains, is the program she wishes she had while trading. It gives financial traders real-time insights, starting with commodity and energy markets and macro, by processing more data than a person possibly could. Her favorite measure of the scale: in the four seconds it takes her to say one sentence, Moshi has learned three hundred new things.

The inputs mirror how she used to trade. There is structured data, the time series of futures prices with technical indicators and moving averages layered on top, and the fundamentals series behind supply and demand. There is unstructured data: news, posts on X, audio. And there is the user's private data, the analysts a given trader trusts, their subscriptions, and internal feeds, such as a firm's own count of LNG ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz. Moshi cleans and transforms all of it, tags it with statistics and machine learning under an ontology, places it in what she calls an n-dimensional graph, and then pushes relevant items to the trader, who can also pull answers from a language model in about thirty seconds for questions that would otherwise take days.

Asked whether Moshi is a new Bloomberg, she pushes back. Bloomberg has real strengths, she concedes, including the chat network across which traders transact enormous sums. But Moshi is a different kind of software: if Bloomberg and Claude and Netflix had a baby, she says, it would be Moshi. The Netflix part is a recommendation system. A trader watching one thing usually needs the second and third order effects right now, and would not know to ask. If an energy equities company does something that indirectly moves oil, a news stream will not connect those dots, but a connected graph with a recommendation layer can surface it in time.

That is why she thinks recommendation systems are the most underrated part of AI. The host notes they power YouTube, Netflix, Instagram, and TikTok, yet engineers barely show up to talks about them, partly because good ones make the hard part invisible. Sokhal adds a linked claim about interfaces: everyone can now build their own dashboard, but a dashboard is one person's brain made visible, and getting anyone else to adopt it is a user interface problem, not a data problem. Platforms like Spotify and Instagram push things at you that hook you before you notice. Her thesis is that solving for the interface and solving for the recommendation system are really solving the same problem.

The obvious objection is noise. The host recalls his buy-side years, when his fund was among the first to buy the full Twitter firehose at roughly three hundred thousand dollars a month, alongside satellite and credit card data, and much of the stream was random talk or deliberate manipulation. Sokhal's answer is that traders already solve this socially: they follow trusted voices, and in commodities many of those voices are suppliers and facility operators posting status updates because it is the fastest channel they have. Moshi attaches a confidence score to an item and shows everything happening around the conversation, and when sources disagree, Reuters saying one thing and Bloomberg and the Wall Street Journal another, the disagreement itself is a data point, because it may mark an opportunity nobody has resolved yet.

Private data raises a second objection: how do you integrate with systems you cannot see? For messy PDFs, Moshi runs its own vision language model to break documents down and rebuild them in its database, partly so client data never has to be sent to a third party processor. Whether to build or buy such models, she says, depends on the team: if you have engineers, you will want your own, and most of her team, she notes, has worked with NASA in some capacity.

The last part of the conversation is about what happened after her talk. Inbound flooded in: traders at funds, consultants, and, unexpectedly, AI engineers who said management now tells them they are the whole team, expected to build alone what ten people used to build, and who wanted to know whether they need swarms of agents and what happens when something breaks. She extended her New York stay by a week of meetings, with energy firms pulling her toward Houston, and Moshi is planning its first webinar because the one-on-one meetings do not scale. The host, recalling how banks used to run five-funds-at-once meeting rooms for investor relations, floats doing the same at the conference: pre-assigned slots and rooms, so a speaker's follow-ups happen on site. Hiring, by contrast, is not her bottleneck. One Moshi job posting drew twenty-five hundred applicants in four days before they closed it, a stream she says is full of noise, from rival companies shopping to applicants who are not legally able to work. Clients, not candidates, are the priority.

Open questions

  1. Can a recommendation engine for traders surface second and third order effects fast enough to matter without also learning to hook attention the way consumer feeds do, with all the false positives that would bring to a trading desk?
  2. Will trading firms hand a young startup their private data, trusted analyst lists, subscriptions, and internal feeds, and is running its own document models enough for Moshi to win and keep that trust?
  3. Does the flood of interest after one conference talk convert into paying clients at the pace her extended week of meetings suggests, or does finance still buy through slower channels no matter how strong the inbound signal is?
