I Tested Claude Duet and It’s… Good??

On Brock Mesarich's channel AI for Non Techies, the video "I Tested Claude Duet and It’s… Good??" is a first hands-on test of Claude Duet, Anthropic's new personal AI assistant. Mesarich did not get early access. He came off the waitlist, sat down in front of the camera openly unsure how he felt about the product, and tested it live.

Duet presents itself as an assistant that works across a person's whole digital life. Inside the app it can reach files and documents, mail, calendar, and team chat. A payments section exists, although the assistant cannot actually make payments yet. Its welcome message sets the pitch: today is the first day of working together, it will get to know him a little better every day, and over time it will start spotting what he needs before he asks.

The first test is voice mode. The spoken replies are quick, with low latency, and when Mesarich asks what is on his calendar, Duet answers from the calendar itself: two open days, then a Saturday flight home from Lisbon to Washington and on to Dallas. It is a small moment, but it establishes the idea that the assistant is wired into his real accounts rather than a demo sandbox.

The interface borrows its organizing idea from Slack. Channels give each topic its own place for messages, tasks, and files, so his work does not collapse into one endless conversation. He creates a channel for his YouTube videos, and Duet offers to suggest tasks it could take on. What impresses him most is where Duet's context comes from: it can pull from the database he built inside Claude Code holding his past videos and ideas, which means the context from his earlier work in Claude Code, Claude chat, and Cowork follows him into the new app.

That shared memory turns into visible output. He asks for a dashboard of his previous three short-form videos, and Duet builds one from the stored analytics, complete with views, likes, and engagement, alongside a note that his channel brought in about 1.35 million views in the last twenty-eight days. A separate section called Make collects every artifact the assistant produces, from dashboards to presentations and documents, including ones he made earlier in other Claude products.

The tasks view is the other piece he keeps returning to. It shows what the assistant is working on, what is ready for his review, and what is up next. Crucially, it can add items he never asked for: if an important email arrives, Duet can spot it and put the follow-up on tomorrow's list. That proactive streak, he suggests, is what could make the experience feel different from an ordinary chatbot.

The clearest gap shows up in integrations. Mesarich wants to ask about his email marketing results from his phone, but the tool he uses, Drip, has no connector in Claude, and there is currently no way to add connectors from inside Duet itself. His workaround is to route through Zapier's MCP service, which advertises connections to more than nine thousand apps. Once Drip is connected that way, he asks Duet on his phone for the last campaign's numbers and gets back a dashboard with the open rate, click-through rate, unsubscribe rate, delivery rate, and where readers dropped off.

All of this is measured against the tool he actually lives in. Mesarich has used a rival assistant called Grok Bot every day for two months to help run his business, and calls it his favorite AI tool he has ever used. Its design is almost the opposite of channels: several specialized agents, including a main agent, an invoice agent, and a YouTube agent, that talk to each other and share context, wrapped in a simple, colorful, beginner-friendly interface.

His early verdict is split. He prefers Grok Bot's cooperating agents to Duet's channels, which he finds less clean, while Duet wins on the Make section, because Claude's talent for building artifacts has no equivalent in Grok Bot, and on the tasks view with its proactive additions. Two caveats hang over everything: he has only used Duet for a few hours, and only on his phone, while most of his real work happens at a computer. For viewers, access is a waitlist signup, and for now you have to be in the United States to get in.

Open questions

  1. Will Duet's proactive habit of adding tasks on its own, like flagging an important email for tomorrow, prove genuinely useful over weeks of real work, or will it turn into noise he has to manage?
  2. Can topic channels match the clarity of Grok Bot's separate cooperating agents, or will Anthropic eventually move Duet toward a team of specialized agents instead?
  3. Will the gaps he hit close quickly, meaning connectors that can be added inside Duet itself, working payments, and a desktop experience as strong as the phone, or will workarounds through services like Zapier stay necessary?
