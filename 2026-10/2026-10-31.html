Nate B Jones · Agent access, trust, and the three zones of the agent internet

Muse, Dots, and Duet Want To Shop For You. The Internet Is Still Deciding Whether To Let Them In.

On AI News & Strategy Daily | Nate B Jones, the episode "Muse, Dots, and Duet Want To Shop For You. The Internet Is Still Deciding Whether To Let Them In." argues that the near future of personal agents will be decided less by how smart the models become than by which businesses agree to let them in. Jones predicts an internet of uneven access that lasts through 2027: some doors open, some stay shut, and no improvement in the model unlocks a door a business has chosen to close. The deciding is being done now by websites, payment networks, and platform companies, and the agent worth choosing, he says, is the one that already works where you shop, travel, and work.

He starts with his own arrangement, which he admits is not normal: he splits his work across five agents. GrokBot takes the harder coding and build jobs, because its bots can talk to each other and coordinate dependent pieces of work under a chief of staff setup, without him relaying every discovery between them. Dots holds his accumulated context in Codex and handles calendars and email; it is not fast, but it never makes him start over, and its proactivity once caught a double-booked flight in time for him to cancel it and get his money back.

Instinct, which he says is raising money again at a valuation of about $30 billion, is his travel agent: easy to text on the go, good at autonomous check-ins, and willing to dig into a destination rather than treat him like an ordinary tourist. Muse does his preliminary shopping and scoping, his holiday gift hunting, and bookings such as restaurants and yoga classes; it knows his yoga login, and through a partnership with Stripe Link it can simply buy at a range of websites. Duet, Anthropic's new entry, is the one he is still figuring out, and within its first two days it had already proactively flagged something coming up that he had missed.

Most people, he notes, will not run five agents. They will pick one and stick with it, and the contenders are sorting themselves out: Muse leaning toward consumers, GrokBot, Duet, and Dots leaning toward prosumer and work use, with Dots able to swing toward consumers given ChatGPT's scale. That makes the access question decisive. His example is a hotel booking page where his agent's otherwise perfect trip plan simply froze, because that hotel had locked agents out. A smarter agent does not solve that, and it changes which businesses end up getting his money.

The doors are closing because of what he calls Black October. AI has cut the difficulty of exploiting old website weaknesses toward zero: research he cites on PHP flaws shows AI helping researchers build and adapt working exploits across applications very quickly, and attackers get the same leverage. An investigation by Not Forge showed attackers using agents to coordinate their work, share discoveries, and keep pressing on intrusions. The obscurity that quietly protected much of the internet for years is gone.

US banks, he says, have made headlines admitting that agents are all over their websites and they do not know what to do about it. The problem is close to unsolvable at first principles: an agent driving a website the way a person does looks almost exactly like a person, so a bank cannot easily tell whether the agent at its door belongs to an enthusiast checking his own account or to a bad actor seeking unauthorized access. Most banks also lack the technical teams to judge the sophistication of agent behavior. The open question underneath everything is how to establish a human's authorization in a world where humans and their software look the same.

Blocking everything is not a safe answer either, because the more a customer delegates, the more often a business meets the customer's agent instead of the customer. The delegated work runs through recipient websites: browsing, shopping, planning, a school homework portal, six months of school events to get onto a calendar, restaurant bookings. Agent traffic at scale can itself look like an attack, and a whole reservation market has already grown up as a side effect of agents chasing restaurant tables. Anything completed depends on several authorization decisions agreeing at once: the site has to know it is dealing with an authorized customer, the payment provider has to know what is allowed to be charged, and the assistant has to protect the account from malicious redirection.

The first agreements are appearing. On October 20, Visa, BILL, and Billtrust announced a live invoice payment pilot: a buyer approves a supplier's bill, and BILL's agent completes the payment through the supplier's portal using Billtrust. The agent is registered, its payment credentials are controlled according to the buyer's instructions, the receiving system recognizes it and grants access, and the payment stays formally tied to the invoice, with visibility all the way from approval to completion. Jones expects a run of such partnership arrangements, built to let authorized agents make authorized purchases of high-value items.

Recognition itself takes infrastructure. Cloudflare's signed agent system lets the sender of a request attach a digital signature that Cloudflare can verify, so a website can set rules for agent traffic; the business still decides what a recognized agent may do, whether that is reading a price, seeing a reservation, or cancelling one, and Jones expects simple, non-technical permissioning packages to become a product category. The cautionary tale came out of Black October: some businesses had encoded instructions in their websites asking agents to identify themselves, and Claude, wanting to be helpful, complied, in some instances leaking human email addresses. Asking for identification carelessly, he says, can make a difficult security situation worse.

On payments, Google's Agent Payments Protocol, AP2, describes signed records for instructions and for purchases, and Stripe's Shared Payment Tokens let a payment credential carry restrictions, such as use with a single seller, an amount limit, or an expiry time. There is no agreed schema for the rules of the road yet: the market may settle on one winner-take-most system, or land in a fragmented patchwork where some businesses sit on Google's system and others on Stripe's. Businesses, meanwhile, have to understand exactly what they are unlocking, because one service might recognize an agent but still demand proof of a particular instruction, allow research but stop at payment, or allow one kind of payment authorization and not another.

Businesses also have to change what they publish. An agent planning a trip needs to compare arrival times, cancellation terms, and change policies that hotels bury behind beautiful homepages and big images. Jones quotes Stripe's Patrick Collison arguing that the agentic economy is a pressure toward product excellence, because agents are not fooled by the marketing that influences humans and instead demand clear, good products. Shopify's October 28 expansion shows the shift starting in retail: merchants can review and publish facts written specifically for agents, including return windows and shipping destinations, and Shop Pay is appearing inside Muse, Claude Duet, and Instinct, with GrokBot named among the assistants offering it for brands outside Shopify. This holiday season, the first agent holiday shopping season, will test whether it all works in public.

The strategic split is already visible. Shopify, a challenger brand, is incentivized to lean into the agentic future, while Amazon, no challenger at all, has said no to agents, and Walmart has said yes. With many agents per person, many agents per business, and fragmented coordination rules, Jones offers a framework for the internet an assistant will encounter in 2027, in three zones. In the partnership zone, a business provides an accepted route for the work, such as a supported Shop Pay checkout or the invoice pilot, and several competing assistants can share that infrastructure, so choosing one does not shut you out. In the aspiring actor zone, an agent works through an existing site the way a person would, as Duet's browser does on sites without a connector; it might finish, or it might hit a changed page, an account check, a step that needs you, or a business telling agents to please not do this, which compliant agents will often obey. In the no-go zone, the business refuses the agent or the action outright. Amazon's dispute with Muse, over undisclosed agent access, the absence of an agreement, and credential handling, is his prime example, and at root it is a fight over who owns the customer relationship.

The power in this world flows to whoever runs the trust layer in the middle, because businesses want protection and assistant companies want their products to finish the job. Jones says he is bullish on Google, on Stripe, and on the large companies positioned to facilitate the agentic economy, and he compares the moment to the 1990s, when buying online felt dangerous and Amazon built lasting trust by providing secure checkout and delivering what was paid for, a muscle memory of confidence that compounded for years. He praises Cloudflare for explicitly advocating open standards and independent choices of identity providers, payment processors, and agent partners, and Shopify for opening its catalog and checkout tools to other builders, while noting these choices should be judged by what gets built on them over the next six months to a year. For consumers the judgment will be invisible and concrete: did the agent check out on the hotel page, get the school events, land the restaurant reservation? A first-time user burned by a failure or two, he warns, is unlikely to be patient enough to try again.

Black October, he argues, set the agenda back, predictably: a company busy making sure its website is safe is not putting agent access at the top of its list. The risk also runs toward consumers. A firm he names as Forever Security found a malicious agent instruction file in a customer's downloads folder and stopped a coding agent from installing malware from it, and researchers have demonstrated that a website can plant such a file by poisoning an agent, which means users have to think about where they send their agents and give them project folders whose contents they can watch. His closing advice is about how to talk: this Thanksgiving, recommend agents to less technical friends and family only for the jobs they demonstrably do well, a few shopping sites, some travel, knowledge work and coding, scheduling and email. In an environment where people are already suspicious of AI, that specificity is what earns trust. By the end of 2027, he says, it will be a very different world, but this is where we are now: the internet will splinter further around agent access, the organizations setting the safety protocols will gain power and influence, and he hopes they keep those protocols open.

Open questions

  1. If agent-driven computer use and human computer use are nearly indistinguishable, what should count as proof that an agent is acting with its human's authorization, and who bears the loss when a business gets that judgment wrong?
  2. Jones hopes the trust protocols stay open and shared, yet the partnership zone is being built by a small number of large infrastructure companies gaining power from both sides of the market. What keeps the gatekeepers of the agent internet from becoming the closed doors of the next one?
  3. He predicts uneven access through 2027 and a first agent holiday shopping season that consumers will judge simply by whether tasks finish. If early failures harden into distrust before the protocols mature, does the splintered internet he describes become permanent rather than transitional?

Agents for long-horizon financial work at Arca

Using Agents for Long-Horizon Financial Work — Aman Singh, Arca

On Latent Space, the episode "Using Agents for Long-Horizon Financial Work — Aman Singh, Arca" was recorded at the AI Engineer New York conference, where the show's head of editorial, Richard MacManus, talks with Aman Singh, who leads the AI platform team at Arca. Arca is an AI native wealth management firm that recently raised $64 million, and Singh explains how agents run much of the firm's work behind the human advisors its clients actually deal with.

Arca offers financial advice and planning, tax planning, insurance, and estate planning, for clients ranging from people who just sold a business to technology executives planning early retirement to small families buying a first home. Singh is explicit that the role of AI at Arca is not to replace the advisor but to replace the work around the advisor. Clients never interface with the AI directly. What they experience is a level of service the industry could not previously deliver, provided through a human advisor, while machinery behind each client constantly tracks what the market is doing, how it affects that client, and what else is going on in their life.

The agents run on frontier models from Anthropic and OpenAI, with Claude the primary model today. Arca has cycled through providers over the years, from Gemini to OpenAI to Anthropic, and Singh says the decisions are guided by internal benchmarks for the capabilities the harness has to express, not by public leaderboards: a recent model that external benchmarks praised proved too slow and too costly on Arca's own tests. The firm built its own harness and keeps it model agnostic on principle, so it never depends on one provider. Inside the harness, models are tiered by task complexity. A tax analysis gets the heaviest tier, background activity runs on a lower tier, and none of this is visible to the advisors, who simply use the product while the routing decisions happen underneath.

His central example is a client's tax bill, a piece of work that spans ten days. When the client emails about the bill, agents take on the analysis: where the client's money is allocated today, and what the most optimal way is to liquidate some of it to produce $100,000. That analysis is fully automated. Executing it is not. Arca has a deep integration with its custodian, with APIs that could move money, but strict guardrails keep a human in the loop for anything that moves money, liquidates assets, or communicates directly with clients. The agents instead pull in the right person at the right moment and hand them a plan they can review and adjust.

Work that runs for days needs memory that survives between touches. Signals arrive constantly: a reply on an email thread goes back to the same agent following that thread, while a fresh signal, such as a phone call, wakes a fresh agent that has to work out what the signal means. What makes that possible is a per-client memory system tracking what is happening right now, what is being serviced, who owns what, what has already happened, what needs to happen in the next couple of days, and what is being tracked for the long term. A reflection process, which Singh describes as a kind of dreaming, updates those memories, so a newly woken agent can see that a big tax bill is being executed and place its signal in that context.

Safety is built as deterministic restrictions that take decisions out of the agent's hands. Simple record keeping tools the agent can execute itself, but every tool call that writes data also gets checked by fast verification models, and inbound emails, documents, and phone calls are screened at the edges because they are targets for prompt injection. When data leaves the system, in a web search or even a calendar invite, deterministic checks look for known personal information and a consensus of small models checks that no exfiltration is being attempted. The principle, Singh says, is to constrain at the edges of capability and not earlier.

Compliance shapes the design as much as safety does. The industry cares about outcomes, but compliance cares about auditability, so every action an agent takes produces a change log recording what changed, who changed it, from which part of the system, and when. The same logging applies to human actions in the interface, and it doubles as an input to the agents, since a change a person makes can inform the agent that it happened.

The line Singh is known for is that at Arca agents prompt people more than people prompt agents. In a ten day process with multiple actors, the person needed at step seven does not need to know about it yet; the agents, using their long memory and daily reflection on the state of the world, pull in the right person when a signal implies they are needed. This works, he argues, because Arca is not a software vendor. It is the firm itself, so it can standardize how the work happens and operationalize both the industry's context and its own principles of how work should be done.

That is also why he is wary of depending on managed agent products from the labs. He is bullish on their models, but a managed agent's core asset is how it persists memory and operational knowledge, and handing that layer to a provider means handing off the firm's intelligence and the control needed to tailor it. Anthropic's move into financial services strikes him as validating rather than threatening, because there is a difference between a services firm that has access to AI tools and a firm built to be AI native. In the first kind, users prompt the agents to produce work. At Arca the service is proactive and runs around the clock.

The agent itself never sits inside the sandbox. It offloads computation there: code it writes is translated into a strict format the backend expects, the code is thrown away, and guardrails are applied to the format before it reaches the backend. Through that mechanism agents can query and aggregate client data and produce artifacts, such as a PDF financial plan, inside the sandbox. The moment an artifact is produced, verifiers run on it. Deterministic checks catch things like a raw account number appearing in the document, and fast models check for content that would make it non-compliant, such as promising returns. Singh argues this can make compliance better than human review, because the checking is tireless.

A separate layer watches the models' facts. Models working with financial data are confident in what they know, and will happily run an analysis on last year's tax brackets. So decision models in the harness detect when the model is about to make a recommendation or take an action requiring financial rigor, and nudge it to use Arca's knowledge base of verified facts about the world as it is today, or to search the web. Trading, though, stays entirely human. Agents propose trades; experts decide whether the trade is right and execute it, and there is no autonomous trading to recover from when an execution fails.

The system also learns from its own staff. Every edit or cancellation by a staff member comes with a reason and becomes an evaluation. That feedback is stored alongside the trace of the agent that produced the work, and every night a meta agent runs over real traces from the field and suggests changes to the harness: a misleading tool, an error, a gap in the knowledge base. A human engineer still approves any prompt change before it ships, because agents left alone make sweeping, badly generalizing prompt edits. The knowledge base itself is a first class feature of the product, curated and edited by the firm's experts; agents cite it as they work, and when knowledge turns out to be wrong, that surfaces through trace analysis and gets fixed at the source.

His closing example is a prospect who was not yet a client. The man was paying another advisor $1,500 and finding him unresponsive. Arca's agents read his documents and statements, analyzed his goals and his current arrangement, and found fees he was being charged that he did not know about. They also surfaced that the advisor was not a fiduciary, and so was not obliged to act in his interests. Singh points to the same machinery producing quieter wins at scale, such as flagging a deadline a client is about to miss or a required minimum distribution they are at risk of skipping, the kind of tracking that is hard for humans to sustain across every client but is default behavior for the agents.

Open questions

  1. Arca's guardrails keep humans in control of money movement and client communication while agents handle everything around those moments. As agent platforms automate more of the orchestration, which of those human checkpoints genuinely hold, and who gets to decide when a step is safe to hand over?
  2. A meta agent already reviews real traces nightly and proposes harness changes, but a human still approves every prompt change because agents generalize badly. What evidence would justify trusting that improvement loop end to end?
  3. Singh's distinction between an AI native firm and a firm with AI tools rests on proactivity and owning the memory layer. How much of that advantage survives once the model labs sell comparable proactive machinery to every incumbent firm?

Edison Scientific, lab-in-the-loop discovery, and new drugs by 2031

AI Will Build Totally New Drugs by 2031 | Sam Rodriques

On MTS, the episode "AI Will Build Totally New Drugs by 2031 | Sam Rodriques" is a conversation with Sam Rodriques, the founder and CEO of Edison Scientific, recorded as his company announced a partnership with Pfizer to bring superintelligence into Pfizer's research and development organisation. His forecast comes in two waves: first, existing drugs will be found to cure more diseases than they were approved for, and then, within three to five years, entirely new drugs designed by AI will start to arrive.

Rodriques grounds the mission in genetic medicine. Edison works with physicians such as Fyodor Urnov, and he describes the emails that arrive from parents of children with genetic diseases, including a twenty month old child with a mutation that is progressively taking away sight and hearing. His argument is that this should no longer be happening in an era of abundant machine intelligence, and that the real obstacle is not intelligence itself but the gap between having it and deploying it into doctors' practices, discovery pipelines, and regulatory processes.

That gap shapes his account of what actually limits drug discovery. Intelligence, he says, is the bottleneck that is disappearing. Data is another, and so is the physical ability to run experiments, which comes down to laboratory equipment and supply chains. The big pharmaceutical companies already own that infrastructure. What they cannot easily do on their own is connect the new intelligence to their data, their experiments, and the tacit knowledge of how their own processes work, and that connection is the product Edison sells.

The product is Cosmos, Edison's AI scientist. It is deployed inside a partner's own cloud, hooked up to the company's data, with the organisation's tacit knowledge elicited and baked in. The point, Rodriques says, is the last mile: work that once took a year or two can come down to weeks. He declines to describe the specific discoveries Cosmos has made inside Pfizer, saying the partner would not thank him for sharing them on a podcast.

He can talk about the earlier system. Robin, an agent Edison built in early 2025, was the first agent he knows of to run a lab in the loop process, proposing experiments, taking in the data, revising, and arriving at a discovery. Combing through tens of thousands of hypotheses, Robin identified an existing class of drugs, Rho-kinase inhibitors, as a treatment for dry age-related macular degeneration, a major cause of blindness that affects a significant share of people over sixty. The finding had not appeared in the scientific literature before, it held up in lab and mouse tests, and it was published in Nature. Cosmos is the successor to that system, and Rodriques puts its tally of novel scientific findings generated for partners somewhere in the tens of thousands.

He is equally clear about why none of this moves at software speed. A new medicine has to be manufactured, its ingredients sourced and shipped, and its trials run at real sites in the real world, where a delivery truck can break down and cost days. AI that is only loosely attached to that process misses most of the value. The acceleration he promises comes when the system is integrated deeply enough to know about the broken truck the moment it happens and to reroute around it, and even then the gains are large rather than instant.

His predictions follow that logic. The first wave is repurposing, because the drugs already exist and the trials, while still required, are fast. He points to work already done: an existing drug, leucovorin, has been recommended by the FDA for autism on the back of AI driven repurposing work by the nonprofit Every Cure. Over the next year or two he expects many more cures of that kind. On a three to five year horizon, he expects the second wave: totally new drugs, not repurposed ones, that are fully AI driven.

Open questions

  1. Rodriques argues the bottleneck has moved from intelligence to deployment, data, and logistics. If that is right, does the advantage sit with startups like Edison that sell integration, or will the large drug companies eventually build the same capability in house?
  2. Repurposed drugs are frequently off patent, which is why a nonprofit is doing some of the leading work. Who pays for the trials and approvals needed to turn AI repurposing findings into standard treatments at scale?
  3. His three to five year clock for fully AI driven new drugs assumes trials and manufacturing can be compressed through deep integration. What result in the next year or two would show that assumption is wrong, and would it be visible from outside the partner companies?

Local AI on AMD hardware and the one-year takeover case

Local AI Will Take Over Within One Year | Ian Weinberg

On MTS, the episode "Local AI Will Take Over Within One Year | Ian Weinberg" is a live in-studio demo. Host Sophia Dew is joined by Ian Weinberg, a product lead at AMD, who brings the company's Ryzen AI Halo developer box into the studio and runs it on air. His opening frame is a shift in mood: two years ago, people hearing about local AI were skeptics who did not believe it. Last year they were asking how to get it. Now they ask how to get more of it, and he says the barriers to interest are gone.

The box is built around memory rather than raw compute. Inside is AMD's next generation Ryzen AI Max Plus Pro processor, the 495, with 192 gigabytes of RAM shared between the CPU and the GPU. Weinberg says memory is what developers doing local AI actually run out of. Of the 192 gigabytes, 160 can be reserved as dedicated graphics memory, and that, he says, unlocks models of 300 billion parameters running on a machine the size of a book, less than two inches tall. The previous generation, the 395 shown at AMD's Advancing AI conference in July, carried 128 gigabytes, and the demo he runs here would not have fit on it.

The platform pitch is openness. The Halo is a standards based x86 machine: install your own Linux or Windows, with no emulation penalty to pay. The ROCm software stack on this small box is the same stack that runs up to AMD's Helios rack systems, so skills learned at desk scale carry to rack scale, a single curriculum from a student's first fine-tuning runs to pre-training work on the big iron. Out of the box, a developer center offers preinstalled playbooks for beginners through advanced users, under a promise AMD phrases as minutes to first token.

Weinberg describes the hardware as a balance of three jobs. Fit is whether a model fits the memory signature. Orchestration is the CPU's work in an agentic world, the tool calls and encoding and decoding that agents generate constantly. Streaming is the GPU and the NPU, where small models now run well. His first demo leans on all three: a hybrid music model with roughly 120 billion conventional parameters plus some 56 billion engrams, lookup tables that run on the CPU, drafts a song to his prompt, and then hands off to ComfyUI to produce it. The task manager shows the workload passing 128 gigabytes of dedicated graphics memory plus 24 more in system memory, outside the envelope of a 128 gigabyte machine entirely.

A second demo runs in the background while they talk. Weinberg connects the Goose agent harness, through MCP, to a chain of applications: an image model makes a picture of a toy robot, a 3D modeling step converts it, and it lands in a Blender scene, with a 35 billion parameter model behind the harness. Humanoid robots, assistive devices, and the long road to home care

Humanoid Robots Are Decades Away From Home Care | Allison Okamura

On MTS, the episode "Humanoid Robots Are Decades Away From Home Care | Allison Okamura" is a conversation with Allison Okamura, a professor of mechanical engineering at Stanford who directs the Collaborative Haptics and Robotics in Medicine laboratory and is a senior fellow at the Hoover Institution. Her central claim runs against the current race toward humanoid robots: a generalist humanoid working in people's homes is several decades away at least, while narrower and stranger machines, built for one task at a time, will matter much sooner.

Her lab works on robot form factors, on how robots move and physically interact with the world, and on matching the machine to the task. One recent example, presented at the IROS robotics conference, is a robotic zipper mechanism for people who need assistive dressing. The garment arrives as separate pieces of cloth, which is a relief if pushing a hand through a sleeve is difficult, and the pieces zip themselves together once they are in place.

The generalist versus specialist argument, she says, runs on two levels. The hardware level is where the humanoid lives: in theory it could do anything a human can, which is its appeal and its trap, since there are countless tasks humans cannot do at all. She calls the humanoid almost the simplest and most unimaginative form of robot, a copy of a body we already have. Against that she sets machines like multi head 3D printers working together on a single object, doing things no human or humanoid body could do. The software level is where agentic AI enters, and it is body limited: the cleverest brain cannot make a limited body do more than it can.

Her sharpest warning is about safety. Robots run by general purpose agentic AI come with no safety guarantees. Language models hallucinate, and when a robot acts on a hallucination the physical move has already happened in the world and cannot be taken back. She cautions against applying the style of AI built for language directly to physical machines, and points to self driving cars as the early version of the liability questions that follow when something goes wrong.

Asked how her lab decides what to build, she splits it roughly in half. One half is design thinking done properly: going out, finding needs, talking to users and domain experts. The other half is tinkering, where a student invents a mechanism for one problem and the lab spots a better use for it elsewhere. Academia, she says, offers the luxury of pulling on those creative threads.

The clearest case is the lab's soft growing robots, called vine robots. A tube of soft, airtight material is inverted, stuffed inside itself, and pressurized, so new material keeps appearing at the tip and the robot grows forward without pushing on its surroundings or fighting friction. They have been grown to the length of a football field. Okamura saw the first one fail at its intended job and immediately pictured surgery, search and rescue, and other tight, tortuous spaces instead.

Those uses are now real research programs. A former student, Tania Morimoto at UC San Diego, builds vine robots a millimetre or two wide meant to travel into the brain and treat strokes, backed by an ARPA-H grant of about 25 million dollars aimed at clearing thromboses before they cause a stroke. AI-driven cyber offense, the patch gap, and a rocky year ahead

Expect a Rocky Road for Cyber Over Next Year | Alexander Barry

On MTS, the episode "Expect a Rocky Road for Cyber Over Next Year | Alexander Barry" features Alexander Barry of Epoch AI, where he heads capabilities tracking. Epoch had published a report the day before the recording on how AI can turn security fixes into working exploits before users receive an update, and the host set the conversation against a reported theft of more than six billion dollars from Binance the previous day, attributed to North Korea.

Barry explains that the report started with a question about widely available models. Frontier labs restrict their strongest systems, so Epoch wanted to know what open weight models, which anyone can download, can do in cyber offense. The team expected a tidy trend: perhaps today's open models could compromise web browsers that were two or three years out of date, with the reachable age shrinking over time. Instead, the answer came back as zero days. The browser the model could compromise was the current one.

Working with a minimal setup, the open weight model GLM 5.3 built a website that, if visited in the then current version of Firefox, could read files from the visitor's computer. Files include browser cookies, which carry the authentication details that keep people logged in to websites, and they can also include crypto keys and API keys with real money behind them. Epoch was deliberately recreating what a relatively unsophisticated actor could do, not what a professional team could do.

The route in was the patch gap. Mozilla's security fixes land in Firefox's public development code before they ship in a stable release, and the model could read a fix, work out what flaw it closed, and build the exploit from there. That leaves the vulnerability exposed for the week or few weeks the fix waits to reach users. Human exploit writers used to give defenders a cushion, because turning a known flaw into a working exploit took them a week or two. Epoch gave the model up to two days, and in the fastest case it finished in about twelve hours.

That speed is what changes the picture for ordinary users. An update prompt that gets dismissed is now a live risk rather than a theoretical one, and Barry advises keeping software current and thinking twice about keeping software around that will predictably go stale. Software makers cannot simply defend their way out, either: the attack begins from fixes the maker has already written, so unless fixes reach users faster, or stay hidden until they do, the maker is in effect announcing a working vulnerability and a deadline.

Cost points the same way. Epoch spent more than one hundred thousand dollars on the Firefox side of the project, but much of that bought extreme precautions, with everything run offline and in sandboxes so no third party could be harmed. The marginal cost of attacking one more Firefox version inside its vulnerable window was about one thousand dollars, and an attacker who skips the precautions pays less. Barry's worry is the falling skill floor: expert exploit writers usually had attractive legal options, while the much broader class of people who can now attempt this may not.

Chrome held up better in Epoch's testing. The team could compromise Chrome's renderer, the inner process that displays pages, but could not escape the sandbox around it, which is the step that turns a page compromise into access to the machine. Firefox had a small class of sandbox flaws that allowed escape for the narrower purpose of reading files, and the models kept finding the same few of them. Mozilla has patched those flaws, and from Firefox 159.0, released the Tuesday before the recording, GLM 5.3 can no longer repeat the trick. Barry credits Chrome's history of trading performance and compatibility for security, jokes about its memory use included, and stresses that both browsers are exceptionally hardened software and that Mozilla was excellent to work with.

Closed source software is not a lasting refuge. Models are getting better at decompiling shipped binaries, and comparing an old binary with a new one can reveal which change was a security patch. The frontier is also moving: Kimi K3.1 Pro, released around the report, posts benchmark results suggesting another substantial advance, possibly the first open weight model near the Mythos preview tier for exploit development. Epoch has not tested it yet, because the team is waiting for the weights, expected early in November, and Barry notes that even a genuine jump usually takes time to diffuse into real attacks.

His forecast is the source of the episode's title. The next month should look much like the present, but over the next year he is comparatively pessimistic. The internet was built on deeply insecure software, all the way down, and the world got away with it for a few decades. Patching a lot of software is plausible; every bank, every hospital, and every local water company patching all of theirs is not. Software with no bugs at all may arrive eventually, but before then, he expects a rocky road.

Formal verification for C code and chip design

One Bug Fixes Most C Code Vulnerabilities | Tudor Achim

On MTS, the episode "One Bug Fixes Most C Code Vulnerabilities | Tudor Achim" is a conversation with Tudor Achim, the co-founder and CEO of Harmonic. Harmonic's Aristotle system made its name proving mathematics, and this week the company expanded it to verifying C code and chip designs. Achim says Aristotle is, to his knowledge, the first generally available product that can verify C code and hardware designs at very large scale, and he frames the timing as urgent: everyone seems to be getting hacked all the time, because humans wrote a lot of code with a lot of problems.

Verification, as he explains it, is the reasoning a programmer does in their head, except written out where a computer can check it. A programmer stepping through code holds assumptions about the input and works out what the code can and cannot do; verification makes a computer produce that logic explicitly, in a formal language, and has another program check every step. The bridge to existing software is compilation: if ordinary code can be compiled into Lean, the interactive theorem prover he calls the best of its kind, then ordinary software becomes verifiable too, and that translation is what Aristotle does.

He is careful about what verification does not do. Verified code run with a weak password still gets hacked, because verification only guarantees that code does what it says it does. But a large share of real exploits in security sensitive C code chain together memory exploits: writing to memory the programmer does not own, or using data after the permission to use it has expired. Those memory errors are then combined with privilege escalation and other bad behaviour. Remove that first class of issues entirely, he argues, and you cut off a lot of attack chains at their first link.

The concrete example he reaches for is Heartbleed, the OpenSSL flaw, which he calls a classic memory safety issue that Aristotle would have caught before it shipped. Harmonic has also found security problems in very popular open source systems and is working with their maintainers to fix them, cases where he says hacks were prevented. Asked about a major crypto exchange theft reported the previous day, he declines to speculate, saying nobody outside the company yet knows exactly what happened.

The deeper contrast he draws is between finding bugs and proving their absence. For forty years, software engineering has mostly gotten better at finding bugs, and today's most powerful AI systems still do that job: they search. The trouble is that a search never tells you when you are done, because running the finder longer might always turn up one more bug. For defined classes of error, such as memory safety and certain privilege escalation issues, formal verification instead gives a check mark: every bug in the class has been analysed and proven not to exist. The checking itself is done by a small, deterministic program that verifies the reasoning step by step, not by another AI whose judgement has to be trusted.

Making C and Verilog verifiable meant, in his phrase, playing on hard mode. The method is to build a precise mathematical model of each primitive step: what it means to access memory, to add, to write, to read from a device, and then chain those models into a pure mathematical description of the whole program, at which point verifying it is a math problem of the kind AI systems are trained to solve. The difficulty is in the details, since parts of C and Verilog do not even have a formal account of what a language feature means.

Asked what was hardest to build, he lists four requirements that a verification product has to meet at once. It must be correct, since proving something false is the worst possible outcome. It must be capable, scaling past toy examples to an entire chip. It must be fast, because once design partners get used to the tool they demand low latency. And it must keep cost reasonable while doing all of that.

Not all code needs this treatment. If the worst outcome for your software is restarting a web server, he says, the return on verification may not be there, and verifying binary code is an expensive way to check. He expects companies to choose different levels of assurance, producing a spectrum of products rather than a binary of verified and unverified. Verification becomes essential where failure costs more than a restart: software in airplanes, anything that can endanger human life, finances, or cybersecurity, and chip design, where a single failed tape out can cost hundreds of millions of dollars paid to TSMC.

Governments are part of the picture. Achim confirms Harmonic works with governments, and specifically with democracies, on critical infrastructure. The software running the electric grid and air traffic control, he says, was never designed to withstand sustained attack by capable AI, and fixing it in an airtight way is a question of when, not if. He borrows an analogy he credits to a secretary of defense: the cyber risk is like discovering that every bridge in America was built with defective concrete that cracks over time. Society would not defer replacing those bridges, and he rates the infrastructure software problem at about that scale, with the saving grace that the software fix is far easier logistically than replacing bridges.

He expects visible progress quickly. Formal verification lets teams rewrite old code and prove the new version behaves the same as the old one while being memory safe, which makes the giant rewrites hardening requires actually feasible, and he thinks a lot of problems will be addressed within the next year. The discovery rate for vulnerabilities is, in his words, unbelievable at the moment, and the security chiefs he talks to understand the risk and are moving. His closing argument is about offence: five years ago it took a very special human to find a memory safety bug, but AI now makes such bugs easy to find, so leaving them in place is an unforced error. Whatever tool does it, he wants all of the world's safety critical code verified.

  3. He predicts a lot of critical code problems will be addressed within a year because verification makes safe rewrites provable. What evidence would confirm that, and will AI driven bug finding, which improves on the same curve, stay ahead of the defence?

How To Take Enterprise AI Pilots To Production — With Russell Jukes

On Alex Kantrowitz, the Big Technology episode "How To Take Enterprise AI Pilots To Production — With Russell Jukes" is a conversation with Russell Jukes, Chief Digital Information Officer at DXC Technology. Jukes argues that the reason so few enterprise AI pilots reach production is not that the models are weak. It is that AI arrived as a layer on top of everything companies already run, and going live with it is a harder discipline than the hype admits.

He opens with the gap between public messaging and daily reality. Vendor stories about AI sound like other people's vacation photos on Instagram: everything looks solved. Inside companies, the picture is messier, because AI did not replace the existing stack. It was layered into applications, business processes, and the SaaS products companies buy, so there is simply more to manage than before, more control planes, more constituents, and more complexity. A pilot, a prototype, or a chatbot is not production grade AI, and until real users depend on a system, he says, it is still a science project.

His second point is human. Adoption cannot be ordered from above, because every employee has to have what he calls their AI moment, the first time the technology plainly amazes them. Until that happens, people do not trust it and do not build on it. Adoption scorecards, where IT deploys a tool and then grades users on uptake, do not fit a capability this open ended. Enablement, in his telling, is a program with many facets: platforms, training, and short, role specific learning delivered at the moment someone needs it.

Trust is the thread running through the conversation. In a deterministic software world, a report was the same every time you clicked it. An AI answer raises new questions: is it telling the truth, who checked, and do I need to check it myself? At DXC the answer is structured. Everyone may build personal agents for their own work, but the company runs fewer than one hundred professional agents against core data and core actions, and individuals are not allowed to deploy agents to the whole enterprise. Choosing that path, he insists, is the actual decision. A company that never chooses one gets the misalignment between wanting AI's benefits and never settling how it will be used.

On coding, he sees real and compounding productivity. DXC's teams use AI coding tools throughout, but the discipline he prescribes is outcome based coding: like test driven development, you keep checking that the generated work achieves the outcome you need. On cost, he pushes back against token panic. The industry, he predicts, will reward discipline rather than consumption. An application that once took forty five days might now take four, and enterprises need budget lines for token spending alongside human labor so they can model what work actually costs before and after.

Production grade AI, the thing pilots are missing, turns out to be a list of unglamorous systems. SaaS vendors ship AI inside control planes that carry governance, security controls, data loss prevention, and data sovereignty. If you build your own, you have to build your own control plane. Users need to feel safe typing into the system. Data retention and the country the reasoning happens in have to be answered. DXC runs truth testing: one hundred questions with one hundred defined answers, run against agents continuously to catch wrong answers. And he is candid about learning this the hard way, saying his first large scale AI deployment, run as if it were a SaaS application, was a complete disaster that taught him a great deal.

He also draws a line between measuring adoption and measuring fluency. Asked for the business value of AI, he answers that the value resides with the business, not the IT team, and that value looks different for a background process, a benefits chatbot, and an invoice workflow, so no single template fits. His deeper claim is that the journey cannot be skipped. Companies went from chatbots to retrieval and summarization to building hundreds of agents, and then consolidated them behind a few orchestrated ones. Each stage taught something the next stage needs, and trying to jump straight to polished agentic systems is where deployments fail.

Resilience gets its own warning. He recalls a stretch when a government order took a model offline for everyone for roughly a month, and asks what happens when a company cannot switch models, or has wired a model into processes it no longer remembers. Enterprises need a continuity plan for AI itself: how they would know a background process had failed, what the backup is, and whether that means a second system, offline models, or sovereign and private AI. The security chief's job, he notes, has become much harder in the same period.

On build versus buy, he resists a single answer. DXC co-innovates with partners such as Amazon, buys plenty of vendor AI, and builds some of its own, and he suggests drawing the traditional stack and the agentic stack side by side so business partners can see what changes. Looking ahead, he expects interface change: traditional screens and drop downs may give way to interfaces generated on the fly, with voice as the primary way in. He also expects AI technical debt, and describes how DXC treats agents almost as workforce: personal agents are treated like team members, professional agents get their own identities, and the company keeps two standards of business conduct, one for employees and one, written by HR, for how agents should behave.

His closing prediction is that this era of complexity is a passing moment. The technology moved faster than people's ability to absorb it, but the understanding of how to control it, secure it, trust it, and measure it will catch up. Companies, he says, have not yet squeezed the real value out of what they have deployed.

Google's potential, the AI money spigot, and cybersecurity's Black October

Google’s Potential, AI Money Spigot Shutting?, PHP Bug & Cybersecurity’s “Black October”

On Alex Kantrowitz, the Big Technology Podcast episode "Google’s Potential, AI Money Spigot Shutting?, PHP Bug & Cybersecurity’s “Black October”" is the show's Friday news edition. Kantrowitz and Ranjan Roy of Margins work through three stories: what Google could become if it executes, whether the money funding the AI build-out is starting to run short, and why Alex Stamos, the chief information security officer at Cognition, calls the past month the craziest in cybersecurity history. The Google segment draws on Kantrowitz's interview with Google CEO Sundar Pichai, recorded earlier the same week.

The first argument is about Pichai's philosophy. Asked whether good products matter more than frontier models, Pichai would not budge: Google is committed to the frontier, because working at the frontier shows you what is coming and helps you build better models faster. Roy accepts that frontier work speeds a company up, pointing to the launch velocity of Anthropic and OpenAI, but he holds his ground on product. Anyone who uses Google's AI products, he says, can feel how disjointed they are, with Gemini inside Workspace behaving differently from Gemini on its own.

Kantrowitz then relays the answer he found most telling. Asked when search inside Gmail would work as well as Gmail plugged into ChatGPT, Pichai did not promise to fix each product one by one. He said Google is bringing everything together in a unified agent, first for enterprise and then for consumers, reachable whether you are in Workspace, Search, or Gemini. Kantrowitz reads this as Google going headless: the agent becomes the interface, and the products become services behind it. Both hosts think it is the right strategy, and both doubt the organisation. The unified agent would cut across power centres whose standing has always been measured in daily active users and engagement, and Roy asks the obvious question: inside Google, who would even own it.

The same tension shows up in Google's hardest decision, where to allocate compute. Everyone is short, Pichai said, and everyone wishes they had twice what they planned for, because the capacity being used in 2026 was decided in 2024. Google Cloud grew about 82 percent in the past quarter, to roughly 24.8 billion dollars of revenue, against 29.4 billion for Azure and 42.2 billion for AWS, and Kantrowitz argues that with more capacity the cloud business could pass Azure within a year or two. Pichai was explicit that research wins the trade-off for now: the cloud business, he said, is forgoing short-term revenue because Google is making compute trade-offs to support frontier AI development. He added a twist neither host expected, that some constraint is good for researchers too, because scarcity is what forces the efficient algorithmic breakthrough.

Set against that is the sheer breadth of Google's position. Kantrowitz lists it: an agent working natively across thirteen products with a billion users each, search, model licensing through its API, cloud and chip sales, Waymo, and a stake in Anthropic worth tens of billions. No other company, he says, can hit on all of those fronts at once. Roy agrees with the list and distrusts it in the same breath, because every one of those assets is also a source of complexity. Ask Google Maps and Gemini the same question, he notes, and you get two different answers, with the Maps one worse.

The second story asks whether the AI money spigot is closing. The build-out was funded first by venture capital and free cash flow. The free cash flow ran out, so the companies turned to debt, off-balance-sheet borrowing, and equity sales. Meta's stock fell about 9 percent in the week of the recording after a regulatory filing warned that big capital raises by Alphabet and SpaceX, plus anticipated fundraising from Anthropic and OpenAI, have made the fundraising environment more challenging, and that Meta would raise money more proactively before competitors absorb the available capital. Meta's anticipated AI capital commitments have risen to about 825 billion dollars, up from 700 billion in July.

Roy offers two reasons capital is tightening. The first is arithmetic: investors have started doing the math on what these products must earn. Even if Meta's Muse agent reached a hundred million users paying twenty dollars a month, the resulting revenue would cover only a fraction of the spending, and the agent would need something like a billion users before the numbers approach working. The second reason is fatigue. The Financial Times reported that OpenAI cut the valuation target for its latest round from 1.4 trillion dollars to 1.25 trillion after investors pushed back, with one person describing zero clamoring where earlier rounds caused a frenzy. Roy calls that healthy. Kantrowitz asks whether money has literally run out, and Roy's answer is that the industry has reached the final boss of fundraising, the sovereign wealth funds of the Gulf, with government treasuries the only deeper pocket left. Whether OpenAI can still reach a successful IPO, they agree, now depends mostly on how the market receives Anthropic.

The third story is the darkest. Stamos, who joins for the second half, calls the past thirty days the craziest in the history of cybersecurity, and the PHP bug is only the headline. PHP powers as much as 70 percent of the world's websites, and its creators disclosed a critical flaw, a pre-authentication remote code execution bug, that lets an attacker take over a system running the software without any login at all. The bug was found months ago through OpenAI's Hack the Planet programme, which gives approved researchers access to its models for vulnerability research, by a security consultancy working in that programme. The coordinated disclosure then leaked, and there is evidence attackers began exploiting the bug before it was made public.

The exploitation is already industrial in scale. Cloudflare's Matthew Prince reported thousands of exploit attempts in the ten hours before the announcement, tens of thousands within forty-five minutes of it, hundreds of thousands within two hours, and millions since. Stamos describes ransomware crews, Iranian actors, and a Chinese access industry that breaks into sites in bulk, plants malware, sometimes patches the hole behind itself to keep rivals out, and only later works out which of the millions of compromised sites are worth ransoming or selling. A foothold in a web server usually means the live data, the database behind it, and a path deeper into the organisation. Patching does not end the risk, because the only official patch covers the latest PHP family, and many sites run older versions that cannot be upgraded without breaking. His forecast for the coming months is a long tail of breach notifications, secondary intrusions from footholds planted in the first forty-eight hours, and dumps of tens of millions of passwords hitting the dark web, feeding a surge in individual fraud.

For ordinary people, his advice is blunt: nobody can stop the breaches, so the defence is hygiene. Use a password manager, use a different password on every site, keep a secondary account disconnected from your real identity for throwaway sign-ups, and expect far more convincing scam calls and messages now that so much personal information has leaked. This level of threat, he says, is the new normal for at least several years.

PHP is not the whole of it. Within about a day of the PHP disclosure came serious flaws in Node.js, a stolen certificate authority certificate, and breaches touching Java, GitLab, Railway, Signal, WordPress, and LiteLLM, the cluster of incidents that gives the episode its "Black October" name. Stamos says many of these attacks are aimed at the AI ecosystem's infrastructure companies, the hosting providers and neo-clouds whose stolen API keys open doors everywhere else. Companies are also discovering attack surface they forgot they had, conference sites and side projects still online years later, while the US government's own defensive capacity has thinned, with CISA much reduced. He separates this from the fear of rogue agents escaping evaluation, which he thinks the industry has largely contained. The agent risk he takes seriously is the opposite one: people handing agents their password vaults and bank access, while banks fight to keep automated access out.

His closing argument is that none of this should be blamed on the labs that found the bugs. The PHP flaw had existed for a decade, and if OpenAI and Anthropic had not surfaced it, open-weight models would have. The only way through the next two to five years, Stamos says, is to find the bugs, fix them, and rewrite a huge share of the internet's software in memory-safe languages, work that would have been impossible five years ago and is now, with AI help, merely expensive.

  1. Pichai says the unified agent is Google's product future, but the hosts doubt Google's siloed power centres will accept being demoted to services behind it. What would count as evidence, in the next year, that the reorganisation is real rather than announced?
  2. If investor fatigue is genuinely setting in at trillion-dollar valuations, which gives way first: the build-out plans, the timelines for them, or the assumption that sovereign wealth can keep absorbing the rounds until the IPOs arrive?
  3. Stamos says the only way through Black October is to rewrite much of the internet's software in memory-safe languages, at a cost of billions in money and tokens. Seed funds, mega platforms, and why no manager wants to stay small

David Tisch: Why No Manager Wants to Stay Small & Why Competing with Mega Platforms is a Losing Game

On 20VC with Harry Stebbings, the episode "David Tisch: Why No Manager Wants to Stay Small & Why Competing with Mega Platforms is a Losing Game" brings back David Tisch, the founder of BoxGroup, two and a half years after the two last debated his model on the show. Stebbings opens with a concession: he spent years questioning Tisch's low ownership, highly diversified approach, and he now says plainly that he was wrong and that Tisch has crushed it. BoxGroup today runs two funds of 275 million dollars each, a seed fund that will hold around 150 companies and an opportunity fund for follow-ons.

Tisch resists every attempt to map the current market onto a previous one. This moment, he argues, is not 2021 and not any earlier cycle either; each period has its own conditions and has to be figured out on its own terms. For an early stage investor the macro matters less than the micro, because the only real question is whether the specific company in front of you is a great one. Underneath the froth, he says, the job itself has not changed at all: meet great founders, make a decision, and beg them to take your money.

The heart of the conversation is why Stebbings' old math failed. He had assumed that a small check with no real ownership could never return a fund. Tisch says two things broke that assumption. Outcomes have expanded so much that even a small early stake in a generational company can return a fund several times over. And dilution has fallen far below the levels investors like Jason Lemkin quote: in the current window of continued valuation momentum, what you own at the start roughly stays what you own. Tisch still believes venture is a pure power law business where only the outliers matter. He simply doubts that anyone can reliably find an outlier, beat the best firms in the world head-to-head for it, and buy 10 to 20 percent of it at seed, all at the same time.

That doubt drives his attack on concentrated funds. Every concentrated manager claims to win 90 percent of the deals they want, and Tisch calls that figure total BS, putting the real number nearer 20 percent. A fund that needs 30 companies at 10 percent ownership may have to offer 120 to 150 companies to fill the portfolio, so the 30 names it ends up holding are not the 30 it most wanted. BoxGroup's own win rate, he says, runs between 70 and 90 percent, precisely because it will take 1 percent of a company it loves rather than lose the deal over ownership. The single biggest reason it loses is arriving too late, which the firm treats as its own fault and an obsession to fix.

He is just as blunt about the Lean Startup, which he calls a total fake and a decade-long brainwash of professors, books, and blog posts. No venture-scale company, he argues, actually ran that playbook; companies that win need a lot of money, especially now. Lean is not the same thing as disciplined, and well-funded is not the same thing as undisciplined. The same pragmatism governs tranched rounds. Tisch dislikes them, and jokes that he does not want founders to know BoxGroup will still write checks into a second or third tranche at a higher price. His view is that the right to invest is earned rather than granted, and that a founder's decision stands: you can beg for the lower tranche, and if you fail to convince, that is your problem, not the founder's.

From there the conversation turns to the firms squeezed in the middle. Pure play, lead-only seed funds have mostly disappeared or quietly become flexible, Tisch says, because competing head-to-head with the great multi-stage firms is a losing game: they are less price sensitive, can offer treble the price and treble the check, and have become genuinely good at seed. The old willingness to wait and see a company at the Series A or B has also vanished, because waiting usually means never getting in at all. When a firm like Sequoia wins the seed, rivals now pile into the next round immediately, knowing it may be their only realistic entry point.

LPs come in for criticism too. Many tell Stebbings they want exactly one bucket: pure play seed in Silicon Valley from a fund under 100 million dollars. He calls that close to the worst segment in venture, and Tisch agrees the exposure LPs want is largely unavailable. Founders hold the leverage in this market; they raise from people they like at prices they like, and a GP's only real choice is yes or no. A large cohort of LPs, Tisch admits, does not believe BoxGroup's math works. His answer is that he sees the math, it works, and the model only makes sense because it does.

His proof points are the portfolio's slow burns as much as its rockets. Cursor, which BoxGroup backed in its earliest round after partner Claire got to know Michael through dorm room fund investing, returned its fund multiple times over, and Tisch calls it the biggest venture M&A outcome in history. But Clay took a bumpy four years before its growth inflected, and Baseten needed inference to become a category before it ran. His opportunity fund writes 20 to 30 lean-in checks of 5 to 30 million dollars, almost all into existing portfolio companies, with around 40 million dollars into the biggest names across both funds. Rogo is his favourite mispricing story: after its seed, he personally sent the AI-for-finance company to 40 Series A leads and 39 passed, before Keith Rabois led the round and Sequoia and Kleiner Perkins followed. It is now, he notes, a consensus company worth around 2 billion dollars.

Two pieces of industry marketing get dismissed along the way. The claim that pro rata is not a right is, to Tisch, garbage as a slogan and useful as a document: the right to invest in later rounds is earned either way, but having it in writing gives founders leverage to push back when a new investor demands 22 percent of the round. On secondaries, BoxGroup generally does not sell early. The best companies compound the most at the end, he argues, and an investor who derisks early leaves the biggest money behind. Founders taking some money off the table is a different calculation, because a safety net can raise a founder's risk appetite, and employee tender offers are a retention tool he openly praises.

Inside the firm, the culture is built around getting to yes. BoxGroup's ten investors meet 50 to 100 companies a week and hold investment committee twice a week, with no votes: somebody has to champion a deal. Partners who have not spent time with the founders are not allowed to be negative about it. Tisch calls this the no-grenades rule, and his reasoning is that anyone can list why a seed company will fail, while the actual magic of the business is squinting and seeing why it might work. He applies the same long view to other firms, asking every new investor what their fund four looks like, because that is their real ambition, and he observes that most new firms want to become multi-stage firms, the most competitive seat in the market. BoxGroup's refusal to chase that, staying early stage for 17 years, is the thing he is proudest of.

The quick-fire close has him name Y Combinator as the only firm that has invented a true product in venture, with the best business model in the industry, and he tips Benchmark and Sequoia among a small tier of five to seven multi-stage firms that keep landing the best companies. His forecast is consolidation: 20 to 30 firms will be competitive, perhaps 10 of them outliers, and returns will keep aggregating to that group. Asked what is certain in the next decade, he complains that autonomous driving is close enough to solved on safety that it irritates him it is not mandated, and he ends where the episode began, with technology as an equaliser whose benefits should reach everyone rather than a few.

Orbital AI data centers and Starcloud's satellite factory

Nvidia GPUs in Orbit: Inside Starcloud’s $2B Factory

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, the episode "Nvidia GPUs in Orbit: Inside Starcloud’s $2B Factory" is a walking tour of Starcloud's headquarters in Redmond, Washington, led by Philip Johnston, the company's co-founder and chief executive. Starcloud is building AI data centers in orbit, and its first satellite, Starcloud 1, is already flying with the first Nvidia H100 in space. The tour moves through the assembly floor where Starcloud 2 is being tested, the fabrication shop where a Starcloud 3 chassis is being welded, and the new buildings the company is fitting out to manufacture satellites at scale.

The company went from founding to orbit in 21 months, which Johnston says is a record; the fastest anyone had managed it before was four years. A prime contractor quoted 75 million to 100 million dollars to build Starcloud 1, and the team did it for about 2.5 million. Redmond is a deliberate choice: Johnston says 90 percent of all satellites launched in recent years, by mass, were designed, built, and tested there, and his team of 30 people, only two of them non-engineers, is drawn roughly half from SpaceX and half from the hyperscalers down the road.

Starcloud 1 carries three Nvidia chips, two ARM chips, and AMD CPUs. It has already trained the first AI model in space, Andrej Karpathy's nanoGPT, and run a first version of Gemini. Its current work is practical: inference on synthetic aperture radar and hyperspectral imagery for government customers. A satellite operator that collects imagery today may wait hours or days to downlink it through a ground station; with optical terminals feeding data to a Starcloud satellite, the inference runs at the edge and only the answer, such as the coordinates of a vessel crossing a boundary, comes down, in minutes.

The technical heart of the company is cooling. Ezra Feilden, co-founder and chief technology officer, and Adi Oltean, co-founder and chief engineer, both identify the radiators as the largest challenge, and the radiators are designed and fabricated in house. Starcloud 2's deployable radiator will be by far the largest commercial deployable radiator in space when it launches, at least ten times lighter per watt of heat dissipated than the International Space Station's radiators and roughly 500 times cheaper per watt. The satellites use liquid cooling, the same approach terrestrial data centers adopted as chip densities climbed, and the engineering problem is less whether it can work than how cheaply it can be manufactured at scale.

The tour pauses at the vibration table used to prove Starcloud 2 can survive launch. New tables carry a lead time of about two years and a price around 1.5 million dollars, so the team bought a secondhand, broken one at auction for 200,000 dollars. It arrived filled with water, with a serious internal leak, and the country's leading expert on such tables told them the repair was impossible. The team tore it down over a weekend, fixed the leak, and got it running well enough to complete the spacecraft level test. Johnston treats the episode as the company's method in miniature: tight timelines force creative, scrappy solutions.

Chips are the most expensive part of the satellite, and Johnston argues radiation matters even more than cooling to the business model. If chips failed in orbit at rates 10 or 20 percent higher than on the ground, he says, the economics would fall apart. Starcloud tests chips in particle accelerators, adds shielding, builds in redundancy on critical systems like pumps, overprovisions solar panels against gradual losses, and runs a long burn-in on the ground, three months for Starcloud 2, because most chip failures happen early in a chip's life.

Starcloud 3 is the production design: a vehicle of about two tons and 235 kilowatts that fits through Starship's Pez dispenser door, so up to 50 can fly on one Starship launch, close to 10 megawatts of new compute per flight. The company has filed for a constellation of 88,000 of them, enough for a first 20 gigawatts, and Johnston estimates the dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit they use could hold about 10 terawatts, roughly 20 times the entire United States power grid. For a long time, he expects orbital workloads to be inference, for coding agents, back office processing, and chatbot queries, rather than the tightly clustered training runs that frontier models need.

Everything rests on launch cost. Johnston's threshold is about 500 dollars per kilogram: below that, he argues, orbital data centers beat terrestrial ones. A terrestrial solar project spends most of its money on permitted land and on battery storage, and space removes both, since the satellites sit in sunlight around the clock and each square meter of panel produces about eight times the energy it would on Earth. Falcon 9 today costs about 5,000 dollars per kilogram, while Starship is designed for internal costs of 20 to 40 dollars. Launch capacity is the near term constraint: Falcon 9 rideshare is winding down before Starship has wound up, so Starcloud has also booked Stoke and Firefly, and Johnston expects Rocket Lab's Neutron, now set for a first flight in 2027, to fly regularly.

The company has raised 450 million dollars in total. Much of it is going to reserving launch slots from 2028 onward, fitting out facilities, and hiring. The campus is growing to about 100,000 square feet across three buildings, with a production line Johnston compares to a car factory, aimed at 100 satellites a week. He also makes the security case: a satellite moving at 17,000 miles an hour is far harder to attack than a data center in Virginia, Starlink's experience in Ukraine shows what adversaries actually try, and any object larger than an inch across can be tracked, so tampering would be visible and, under the Outer Space Treaty, an act of war.

Johnston also describes a side project with Feilden and Oltean: Fermi Explorer, a nonprofit that aims to send the first spacecraft to Alpha Centauri for 10 to 15 million dollars using only technology that exists today, spiraling toward the sun on solar electric propulsion before a final burn flings it toward an intercept some 80,000 years out. He frames it through the Fermi paradox: either intelligence is exceptionally rare, or it does not last long, and humanity should find out which. His closing hot takes are that SpaceX could be worth more than 100 trillion dollars in ten years once Optimus robots are building Optimus factories, and that SpaceX buying Tesla is, in his words, not a hot take but a fact, delayed only until stock unlocks make the price look fair. What he most looks forward to is nearer at hand: the January launch of Starcloud 2.

Open questions

  1. The whole model turns on launch costs falling below roughly 500 dollars per kilogram, which depends on Starship reusability and on SpaceX passing its costs through. If commercial Starship capacity slips well past 2028, can the alternative vehicles Starcloud has booked close the gap, or does the timeline to competing with terrestrial data centers slip with it?
  2. Starcloud 2's three month chip burn-in and its radiator are meant to prove that terrestrial GPUs survive orbit at terrestrial failure rates. What failure rate over its first year would actually validate the model, and how much room is there in the economics if the rate lands even slightly above what chips achieve on the ground?
  3. The revenue today comes from government edge inference on satellite imagery, while the 88,000 satellite constellation is pitched at commercial inference for agents and chatbots. If defense demand remains the anchor customer, does the constellation still get built as planned, or does the roadmap bend toward government work?

M&A in Q4: How AI Is Reshaping Value Creation

On McKinsey & Company, the episode "M&A in Q4: How AI Is Reshaping Value Creation" is an installment of Inside the Strategy Room, the firm's strategy podcast. Host Sean Brown is joined by four McKinsey dealmakers: Benjamin Houssard, a Paris partner who co-leads the firm's global M&A and AI initiative, Kameron Kordestani, a New York senior partner who leads merger management in North America, Tobias Lundberg, the managing partner in Sweden who co-leads the M&A practice across Europe, and Alex Liu, a partner in Minneapolis. The conversation runs in two parts: first the state of the deal market heading into the fourth quarter, then the ways AI is changing both why deals happen and how they get done.

Kordestani sets the scene. Activity is sustained across geographies and deal types, with North American corporates taking what he calls a through-cycle mindset about whether to invest or divest, and with busy stretches in energy and minerals and in technology, where companies are trying to jump-start AI capabilities. Against that sit real headwinds: a year of geopolitical uncertainty that has moved from a government concern into the boardroom, pressure on consumer sentiment, upward pressure on interest rates, major elections, and supply chains that companies are trying to solidify. Deal value has climbed back to roughly the level of the bumper year of 2021, after the dip of 2022 and 2023.

The cycle, though, is being led by corporates rather than sponsors. Kordestani notes that sponsor-led deals have fallen and private equity is still largely waiting on the sidelines, even though it sits on historic levels of dry powder. Lundberg describes private equity as a story of two halves: firms need to deploy that capital, but holding periods have stretched by about two and a half years over the past decade and exits remain difficult. Sponsors are getting more creative in response, turning to corporate carve-outs, minority stakes, and public-to-private transactions.

Cross-border flows have flipped in a way that looks more dramatic than it is. Europe, the Middle East, and Africa, which McKinsey's annual report in February showed as a net exporter of capital, is now seeing record inflows of some 27 billion dollars. Lundberg cautions that two very large deals from the Americas into the region, worth about 60 billion dollars between them, drive that reversal: strip them out and the region is back in net outflow, with its share of global activity steady at around 24 to 25 percent, in line with historical averages.

The defining feature of the year is the mega deal, which McKinsey defines as a transaction of 10 billion dollars or more. Deals above that line reached about 1.1 trillion dollars in the first half of the year, up almost 140 percent on the same period a year earlier and the strongest half on record, and they now account for about 42 percent of global deal value. Deal counts are down while average deal sizes rise, a barbell pattern, and Europe has joined in through in-market consolidation in sectors such as banking and telecom. Pricing discipline has held: average premiums remain in their historical band of roughly 20 to 26 percent, and while multiples have climbed back to around eleven and a half times, Lundberg reads that as a mix effect from fast-growing sectors rather than buyers bidding more aggressively. One behavioral tell is that equity-linked consideration has risen to about 22 percent of deals, a way of sharing the re-rating risk with sellers.

Where the growth is rotating to is clear. Deal activity is concentrating in AI software, services, and semiconductors, while consumer internet, advertising, video games, and other frontier technology categories have fallen sharply. In Europe, Lundberg sees a realism about competitiveness against American and Chinese rivals, a shift toward more leniency for in-market consolidation in telecom and banking, and a deep private equity pool that accounts for more than 30 percent of European deals, all combining into a relatively benign environment for both acquisitions and divestments.

Houssard then lays out the three dimensions along which AI is reshaping M&A itself. AI is, first, a driver of deal activity. Second, a deal can serve as a catalyst for AI value creation, because integration can be used as the vehicle to rewrite how the combined company works, lifting synergies by at least half and in some cases doubling them. Third, AI is an accelerator of the deal process itself, compressing timelines from target scanning through integration.

The driver effect is visible at the top of the market: seven of the ten largest deals since the start of the year are linked to AI in some way, including utility consolidation driven by the power demands of data centers, and AI appears somewhere in the rationale or narrative of about half of all deals, though Houssard concedes some of that is inflation of the label. He sorts AI dealmaking into four archetypes. Core AI players acquire small teams that have solved a specific problem and plug them in within weeks. Tech-adjacent transformers, software and IT services companies, pay up to reposition for the AI era, as in Synopsys buying Ansys. Ordinary corporates, the most relevant group, buy AI capabilities outright, from pharma companies acquiring biotech and AI assets to Schneider Electric's string of AI deals across manufacturing and supply chain. And a fourth, newer form is the talent acquihire, where Amazon, Meta, or Google hire a startup's founders directly, as Amazon did with Adept AI, whose founding team was hired on a Monday, followed by a licensing agreement on Tuesday and money returned to investors on Wednesday.

Those archetypes carry five implications for deal teams, Houssard argues. The target universe has to be redefined, because the needed capability may sit in a thirty-person startup outside the acquirer's industry. The real asset in an AI deal is the team, and AI talent does not respond to the old retention levers: a three-year financial earnout matters less than autonomy and access to compute, and a specialist without the right graphics processors may simply leave. Speed therefore matters more than in traditional M&A, integration has to be handled carefully so corporate process does not kill what was bought, and structures beyond full acquisition, joint ventures, partnerships, and licensing, belong in the toolkit.

Liu takes up where the value is actually showing up, and he is candid that much remains experimentation, with hard dollar impact not yet the standard. The mature cases cluster in high-volume, process-driven work: software development, where companies report productivity gains of up to 45 percent and reductions of up to 70 percent in the end-to-end development life cycle; customer service, where AI now handles much of the after-call documentation that used to consume frontline time; and back-office finance, from invoice processing to closing the books and forecasting. He describes three ascending levels of ambition: making an existing process faster, putting virtual agents to work on tasks humans used to do, and plugging AI into a broader ecosystem, as in operations planning systems that forecast inventory and replenish orders automatically.

The merger itself, Liu argues, is one of the most powerful change agents available, and companies are starting to use integrations as the pivot for transformation: building an integrated data and technology platform, remaking performance management around what one client calls an executive cockpit, with agents recommending directions to functions, and redesigning specific areas of work for productivity and revenue gains. Three foundations decide whether that works, and they are unglamorous: a leadership mindset that treats the transaction as the moment to transform, the right AI and data talent in a very hot market, and a clean data layer with sound standards and governance underneath everything else.

Finally, AI is compressing the deal process at every stage. Sourcing shifts from a quarterly scan producing perhaps fifteen familiar names to always-on scanning of patents, web signals, and other data that widens the target universe. Diligence shifts from sampling, perhaps 15 percent of a data room over three or four weeks, to reviewing essentially everything in hours, which in practice produces deeper rather than merely faster diligence. Integration work that took weeks per step can be streamlined toward days using libraries of agents built for specific integration tasks. McKinsey's yearly survey shows awareness of AI for the upstream work at 88 percent, up 51 points in a year, with adoption lower but moving fast. Kordestani closes on the widening gap between companies that are experimenting and adopting, and are now leading the pack, and those still waiting on the sidelines.

Open questions

  1. Mega deals now carry about 42 percent of global deal value while premiums stay disciplined. If the AI-linked sectors driving those multiples re-rate downward, does the barbell collapse, or can consolidation deals in banking, telecom, and utilities carry the market on their own?
  2. Houssard says AI diligence now reviews everything in a data room instead of a sample. Does that depth change which deals get signed or what buyers pay, or does it mainly move risk discovery earlier while pricing follows the same old patterns?
  3. The panel's biggest value claims depend on acquirers using integration to rewrite how the combined company works, which needs leadership commitment, scarce AI talent, and clean data. How many dealmakers actually have all three, and does the gap between adopters and waiters harden into a permanent advantage?

Computer science enrollment fell more than 12 percent at undergraduate level and more than 10 percent at graduate level this fall, and applications to full-time US MBA programs fell 27 percent, with international applications down 38 percent on visa policy and American applications down 6 percent. The panel's consensus is that the middle of both degrees is what AI eats: routine coding on one side, financial modeling and slide decks on the other. Salim says business schools have one path, to become entrepreneurship institutes where the credential follows what you built. Dave argues the ends survive, capital raising and logistics at scale on the business side, physics and semiconductors on the technical side. Alex concludes there is no safe harbor left in the old pipeline, and Peter reduces the case for attending at all to two things: find your network, and find your purpose.

The final story is Palmer Luckey's Anduril teaming with Qualcomm on BlueChip, a line of hardened American-made computers starting with a rugged laptop for the military, government, first responders, and police. It runs a Snapdragon chip fabricated at TSMC's Arizona plant, draws about 90 percent of its parts from the US and allies with a roadmap to 100 percent, still takes its screen from China, and is assembled at Anduril's Arsenal-1 factory in Ohio. Luckey calls it the anti-surveillance computer and prices it about 20 percent above high-end PCs, heavier and with limited battery life. Peter discloses he is an Anduril investor. Alex applauds Luckey for doing what Apple would not and what Elon Musk only threatens, while questioning whether sovereign sourcing spreads beyond a government customer that actively wants Chinese components out of its machines. Dave blames policy discontinuity across election cycles for corporate hesitation, and Salim praises the framing: not a better laptop, but rebuilding America's ability to make things.

The closing AMA sharpens several of the day's threads. Asked about Microsoft's voluntary exit offers to 60,000 employees, Alex says the story is capital expenditure substituting for operating expense: the data center buildout is so capital hungry that hyperscalers are converting payroll into capex, not replacing engineers with AI applications. Asked whether Nvidia is exposed to unprofitable customers, Dave walks the margin stack, roughly 50 percent at TSMC, 80 to 85 percent at Nvidia, 80 percent at Anthropic per its S-1, and predicts vertical integration compresses all of it. Emad attributes Chinese models' low prices to necessity, distillation from US models, superb inference engineering, and simply not charging margin. And Salim's governance answer, that security, records, and compliance must live in an organizational layer above swappable models, circles back to the episode's opening theme: the models are compounding weekly, and the institutions around them are the slow part.

Open questions

  1. Emad's call that search advertising tops out as an industry in Q1 is specific and falsifiable. If he is right, can Alphabet's cloud growth close the margin and cash flow gap before the market's patience, already cracked by a $500 billion day, runs out entirely?
  2. The episode's two biggest capability stories, Gemini 4 Carbon and Altman's unpublished mathematics, both rest on claims nobody outside the labs can inspect. What would count as adequate proof for investors, regulators, and rival labs, and who gets to demand it?
  3. Tesla is building capacity for a million Optimus robots a year into a world that shipped 25,000 humanoids in six months. Which use case, construction, industry, or the ten-dollar-a-day household, actually absorbs that supply, and what breaks first if none of them does?
