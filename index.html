Markets · Treasury · fiscal policy

Recent US Treasury Interventions in Bond and FX Markets

A Chicago Booth economist argues the Treasury’s unusual yen and bond-market interventions are really about one thing—borrowing costs—and that tough talk without action won’t move markets that trade on fundamentals.

In “Recent US Treasury Interventions in Bond and FX Markets,” a Hoover Institution interviewer sits down with Chicago Booth economist Anil Kashyap to unpack two unusual moves by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. First, the Treasury intervened in foreign-exchange markets to prop up the Japanese yen—spending dollars, some of it parked at the European Central Bank, to buy yen, the first time the United States has supported another country’s currency this way since the Plaza Accord era. The stated reason was to keep Japan from having to sell Treasuries to fund its own yen purchases. The deeper reason, Kashyap argues, is the connective tissue between both interventions: the Secretary is worried about American borrowing costs. Second, a little over a week ago, the Treasury bought back long-maturity Treasury bonds—unremarkable in itself, but done off the regular pre-announced schedule, breaking the long-standing mantra of “regular and predictable” auctions. On top of that, Bessent has pointed to a large Treasury cash account at the Fed and hinted it could be deployed if long yields misbehave—markets have started calling it the “shadow Treasury rate.”

Kashyap’s verdict on effectiveness is blunt: the yen is back where it was when they intervened. Tough talk not backed by action doesn’t last—markets adjust and revert to fundamentals. For the yen to durably strengthen, Japan would need to tighten monetary policy; for bond yields to durably fall, the United States would need a credible fiscal package. Instead the fundamentals point the other way. The 30-year Treasury sits at a 19-year high, the economy is at full employment, deficits are still large, and the One Big Beautiful Bill, he says, “surely did not pay for itself.” Japan’s new prime minister campaigned on spending—a supposedly temporary consumption-tax cut on food plus rearmament—with vague plans to pay for it, which pressures the yen downward. Both countries’ debt-service costs are rate-sensitive: Japan’s net debt is around 160% of GDP versus roughly 100% for the United States, so each percentage point of higher yields costs Japan an extra 1.6% of GDP. Japan at least has an escape valve America lacks—room to raise its consumption tax toward European levels—though raising it has historically ended prime ministers’ careers.

The conversation closes on politics and the Fed. Kashyap is gloomy about self-correction: Ken Rogoff’s line from the symposium—fiscal repair “won’t happen without a crisis”—gets a nod, though Social Security’s cash-flow trigger in about six years could force a grand bargain. On Fed Chair Kevin Warsh’s much-anticipated Jackson Hole speech, Kashyap gives a grade of “incomplete”: Warsh filled in his framework—policy not currently restrictive, the inflation trend “unacceptable”—but committed to “a discipline, not a decision,” offering no forward guidance. If the next FOMC meeting brings no action, Warsh will have to explain clearly what changed, with dissents likely either way.

If interventions only work when fundamentals move, was the yen operation ever more than a signal—and to whom? Does floating a “shadow Treasury rate” strengthen the Treasury’s hand, or erode the regular-and-predictable commitment the bond market depends on? And if fiscal repair really requires a crisis, what does the bond market’s forcing mechanism look like—a failed auction, a ratings shock, or something slower?

AI · economics · growth

Stanford Professor’s Deep Dive | The Future of Jobs in an AI World

A Stanford growth economist runs the models on AI and jobs: growth probably explodes, but weak links make the explosion slower than Silicon Valley thinks—and the same weak links make the downside arrive faster.

In “Stanford Professor’s Deep Dive | The Future of Jobs in an AI World,” posted by Perfology Clips, Stanford economist Chad Jones—fifteen years into studying economic growth—lays out two caricatures of the AI future and the research that sits between them. Scenario one is Silicon Valley’s: AI automates software engineering (he notes Claude Opus 4.5 already beat every human ever on Anthropic’s own hiring exam), then AI research itself, then everything a remote worker does over Zoom—scaled across millions of GPUs into “a country of geniuses in a data center,” as Dario Amodei put it—designing chips, robots, and drugs until robots run by superintelligence automate physical work too, and growth explodes. Scenario two is business as usual: AI as the latest in a line—electricity, transistors, semiconductors, the internet. The puzzle he keeps returning to: 150 years of wildly transformative technologies, and American living standards rose 2% a year the entire time, never straying far from the line. Each technology didn’t bend the curve up; it kept the curve from bending down. Maybe AI just buys another fifty years of 2%. And history warns that transformations take decades—reorganizing the factory for electric motors, inventing the spreadsheet and the database for IT.

The concept that reconciles the two scenarios is weak links. A chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and every business is a chain of tasks—design the iPhone, source the parts, manufacture to micron tolerances, deliver a hundred million units; one failure and the value is lost, like the Challenger’s O-ring or a single ASML defect. His favorite illustration: you carry a hundred million times the transistors of the 1970s in your pocket, yet you’re not a hundred million times more productive at research—you’re bottlenecked by deciding what data to use and what questions to ask. Weak links are also the source of scarcity, and scarcity is what earns high returns. The data point: the share of GDP paid as a return to computers peaked just under 4.5% in 2000 and has since fallen by a third to 3%—computers got plentiful, humans stayed scarce.

His model—ideas driving growth à la Paul Romer, production functions with weak links, automation arriving endogenously, calibrated to US data back to the 1950s—delivers an elegant rule: automating one task raises GDP by that task’s share of GDP. Infinite software? Software is about 2% of GDP, so we’d be 2% richer. The simulations: in the baseline, growth accelerates but stunningly slowly—2.3% by 2050, 4% richer by mid-century. Full automation sends growth to infinity; reserving 3% of tasks for humans (chess for Carlsen, soccer for Messi) flips the labor share to 100%—humans become the weak link. Even an aggressive “Moore’s law everywhere” scenario needs roughly thirty years for the explosion to complete. So growth explodes in every scenario, but slower than “explosion” sounds—and for the next 75 years you couldn’t tell which world you’re in.

On jobs: a job is a bundle of tasks, and automating 75 of 100 can raise wages, because the remaining tasks become the scarce weak link. Exhibit A: in 2016 Geoffrey Hinton said stop training radiologists; today there are more radiologists, better paid—AI made them more productive. (Uber drivers, he concedes, are probably gone in a decade—though Waymo shows even “simple” physical automation takes twenty-plus years.) On inequality he is cautiously optimistic: a world of explosive growth is a world of abundance with plenty to redistribute—and since AI is automating cognitive elites first, it may even compress inequality in the short term. But he is “very nervous” about catastrophic risk, and weak links cut both ways: slow to improve, fragile on the downside. One jailbroken model in a bad actor’s hands—a virus deadlier than Ebola, a hacked grid or banking system—and one broken link destroys the value. His closing advice for the young: management endures, because some human has to make the final call—and own shares of the S&P 500.

If weak links delay the upside but hasten the downside, is “we have thirty years to prepare” the wrong lesson? Which tasks are truly reserved for humans—is the 3% stable, or does the frontier keep eating it? And if the lasting human edge is judgment and management, what does the economy look like for everyone who isn’t a manager?

AI · infrastructure · talent

Dylan Patel — The talent to build your own AI cloud stack does not exist

SemiAnalysis founder Dylan Patel on the AI cloud boom: GPUs keep getting more valuable even as intelligence gets cheaper, no two clouds are alike—and the talent to build a full AI cloud stack from scratch simply does not exist.

In “Dylan Patel - The talent to build your own AI cloud stack does not exist,” posted by vCluster, co-founder Lucas sits down with Dylan Patel, the founder of SemiAnalysis, at the AI Infra Summit. Patel is one of the sharpest independent analysts of the AI hardware supply chain, and the conversation is a tour of the economics and engineering reality behind the AI cloud boom.

The opening puzzle is one every investor has noticed: an H100 is worth more than it was three years ago, while the price of tokens keeps collapsing. Patel resolves it cleanly. Getting the same level of intelligence costs far less than it used to, because smaller and smaller models can now deliver it, and smaller models serve far more tokens per H100. But the cost per token falls more slowly than token volumes grow, so the total business keeps expanding—which is exactly why GPUs keep getting more valuable even as intelligence gets cheaper.

Then comes the part most buyers get wrong. An H100 is not an H100, and a Blackwell is not a Blackwell. What sits on top of the silicon—the lifecycle reliability, the networking software and security, the cooling, what network was even deployed, whether you get spare nodes when one dies—matters enormously. A bare-metal server is a very different product from a full managed cloud with Slurm or Kubernetes handled for you, and Patel says the reliability gap between tier-one neo clouds and the silver-and-bronze tier is huge, with prices to match. SemiAnalysis tracks more than two hundred neo clouds, sovereign clouds, and telco deployments, and the dispersion in quality is one of the industry’s least appreciated facts.

The interview then turns to the giants. Patel describes the new generation of off-take and bare-metal deals—the SpaceX data center in Memphis, Anthropic reportedly paying on the order of a billion dollars a month for GPU rental—and argues the economics are almost cost-prohibitive for anyone else to replicate. Only a handful of players can play at that table.

That sets up the headline claim of the video. With hundreds of neo clouds chasing the same dream, Lucas asks how realistic it is for each one to build its own software stack. Patel’s answer is blunt: the talent does not exist. The hyperscalers and the biggest neo clouds have already hired the people who have done this before, and building a real cloud takes far more of them than newcomers imagine. Aspire to do as much internally as you can, he advises, but accept that the full stack is beyond reach.

He closes with a prediction about where the market goes next. In Patel’s view, the spot market for GPUs will cease to exist, while a spot market for tokens will emerge—and the clouds that flourish will be the ones that figure out inference, because selling tokens is how you extract the highest revenue per GPU. He flags the unresolved security question for anyone buying tokens from open marketplaces, where the actual provider’s security posture can be invisible.

If GPU spot markets vanish and tokens become the commodity, who sets the price—the biggest inference providers, or a genuine open market? As the tier-one neo clouds consolidate talent and reliability, is there any path left for a new entrant, or is the AI cloud market already decided? And if selling tokens is the only winning business model, what happens to the hundreds of smaller clouds that can’t build the inference stack to do it?

Leadership · startups · Databricks

Databricks’ Ali Ghodsi Never Wanted to Be CEO. Now He’s Among the Best

Databricks CEO Ali Ghodsi on never wanting the job, focusing the whole company on one bottleneck at a time, and why conflict-averse CEOs fail.

Sequoia Capital’s channel carries an episode of the show Long Strange Trip titled “Databricks’ Ali Ghodsi Never Wanted to Be CEO. Now He’s Among the Best,” a 74-minute conversation between the host — a former HubSpot CEO — and Databricks co-founder and CEO Ali Ghodsi. The interview is a tour of how a mild-mannered computer science professor became, in Ben Horowitz’s estimation, one of the best CEOs in Silicon Valley.

The story starts in 2015, when Databricks had seven co-founders, a beloved open-source project in Apache Spark, and almost no commercial success — about a million and a half dollars in GAAP revenue for the year. The board wanted a CEO change and was quietly interviewing outside candidates, while Ghodsi was applying for a faculty job at Berkeley and agonizing over the choice between his lifelong dream of being a professor and a CEO job he never wanted. The deciding principle became a philosophy he still holds: always pick the option that challenges you most, because a life pattern — his parents blocking his dream of becoming a video game programmer in Sweden, turning down a professorship-without-PhD for a harder doctorate, taking a Berkeley postdoc over a professorship — kept showing him that the harder path widened his horizons. Andreessen Horowitz, religious about founder-CEOs, gave him a trial run without even a CEO salary; about a year in, a bigger equity package and a salary bump made it clear the interim tag was gone.

His operating system as CEO rests on two big ideas. The first is the bottleneck method: most companies fail because of one giant bottleneck, so you identify it and focus the entire company on it for one to three years, to an extreme — if you overdo it, great, because you probably still won’t fully unclog it. For him it was the commercial engine, and it meant killing the company’s product-led-growth orthodoxy (a “zero touch” no-sales experiment flatlined revenue for two quarters) and building a real enterprise sales machine. The second is that conviction without staying power is worthless: the lakehouse category, launched in 2018–19 against internal resistance, outside strategy firms, and public ridicule, took four years of the whole company chanting the word in every article, ad, and comp plan before it became the industry’s default vocabulary.

The most counterintuitive stretch is his education in sales. A computer scientist who tested sales candidates on technical brilliance learned that none of the best enterprise sellers are technical — and that a PhD is if anything a disadvantage. What matters instead is professional aggression, emotional intelligence in uncomfortable rooms, and the ability to map a customer’s “power base.” He found his sales leader in Ron, a classic PTC/BMC-style seller who also happened to hold an engineering degree and a Stanford GSB engineering master’s — a combination rare enough to bridge both worlds — and who built the one-million-to-seven-billion ARR engine and is still CRO, a tenure almost unheard of in the role. On hiring executives generally, his rule is to hire ahead of the curve with six-to-twelve-month searches, be so picky you reject future stars (accepting false negatives to avoid false positives), and do an “insane amount” of backdoor references, since front-door references are truthful maybe ten to twenty percent of the time.

On the Snowflake rivalry, his advice is to study the competition obsessively but never follow it: Databricks aimed its strengths at Snowflake’s three weaknesses — a proprietary stack that locked customers in, no real AI story, and high cost — with an open lakehouse, machine-learning roots going back to 2009, and a third of the total cost of ownership, using a coexist strategy that pulled AI-amenable workloads out workload by workload rather than demanding rip-and-replace. He is skeptical of the fashionable flat-org, twenty-five-direct-reports movement: intelligence may be getting centralized, he argues, but the human work of managing careers, unhappiness, and decisions across groups still needs real managers. And on AI inside the enterprise, he says the gap between model capability and actual usage is an “ontology” problem — capturing the context in people’s heads, meetings, and emails and feeding it to the AI — which is what Databricks’ Genie product does internally, to the point where he calls the result magical. He still codes himself, and a two-day connector prototype that embarrassed a three-quarter team timeline taught him that the real bottleneck is process re-engineering, which is why he believes it will take humanity at least a decade to fully absorb AI.

Questions this leaves open: if the winning CEO trait is being “crystal clear” and conflict-seeking, how do you build that in leaders who are naturally conflict-averse — is it trainable, or a filter? When every company is told to flatten and let AI do the managing, which parts of Ghodsi’s human-centric org chart turn out to be load-bearing? And as the lakehouse playbook gets copied by everyone including Snowflake, what is Databricks’ next one-to-three-year bottleneck?

Failure · investigation · accountability

Bill Gurley: Searching for Feynman

Bill Gurley on five catastrophes, the searchers and blockers who define their aftermaths, and why COVID-19 — fifteen million dead by his figures — still has no confirmed root cause six years on.

The All-In Podcast channel carries Bill Gurley’s All-In Summit talk, “Bill Gurley: Searching for Feynman,” a 38-minute presentation that the legendary Benchmark investor — who backed Uber and Zillow and has lived through every tech cycle of the past 25 years — says has been weighing on him for five or six years. It is explicitly not about AI. It is about how societies respond to catastrophe, and about the one catastrophe, he argues, where society never did.

Gurley walks through five disasters and the investigations that followed each. The Champlain Towers collapse in Surfside, Florida (June 2021, 98 dead) drew the New York Times’ 3D reconstruction, a NIST investigation, the building-collapse specialist the town hired, a grand jury, 17 victim lawsuits — and a 37-person Miami Herald team, fresh out of bankruptcy, that won a Pulitzer and helped change condominium rules in Florida and other coastal states. The two 737 Max crashes drew what he calls “the six searchers”: a Senate commission, a House commission, the DOJ, Indonesia’s KNKT reading the black boxes, the press (Dominic Gates’ Pulitzer at the Seattle Times, Peter Robison’s book Flying Blind), 57 whistleblowers, victims’ families, and a pilots’ union that secretly recorded Boeing and filed FOIA requests against it. Hurricane Katrina’s levees, Fukushima’s 5.7-meter seawall (built despite internal documents warning of 10-to-15-meter tsunamis), and the Challenger’s cold-brittle O-rings each got the same treatment. In every case, he says, there were two failures — a technical one and an institutional one — and two kinds of people: searchers, who investigate, and blockers, who circle the wagons. The scientific community even has a formal process for this, CAPA — detect, investigate, confirm root cause, correct, prevent — required by law in pharmaceuticals and aerospace, where prevention is the whole point and you cannot skip root cause.

The hero of his telling is Richard Feynman on the Rogers Commission: fiercely independent, clearing six months of his calendar, investigating NASA facilities before the commission ever met, dunking O-ring material in ice water on live television, and forcing his dissent into the final report as Appendix F after the commission tried to smooth everything over. Feynman’s line — “But seven people died” — becomes the talk’s moral yardstick.

Then Gurley turns to COVID-19: 15 million dead, every life on the planet disrupted, $45 trillion in cost by his figures — three thousand times the combined toll of the five catastrophes he just walked through — and six years later, no confirmed root cause. Where, he asks, are the searchers? No newsroom staffed thirty investigators on it. No bipartisan congressional commissions like Katrina and the 737 Max got. No NIST or NSF-style independent investigation. No massive victims’ lawsuit. Instead, he argues, the five people the public relied on as experts — Francis Collins, Anthony Fauci, EcoHealth Alliance’s Peter Daszak, the Wellcome Trust’s Jeremy Farrar, and UNC virologist Ralph Baric — acted as blockers, exactly the way Boeing and the FAA did. He gives four reasons: they were virology insiders with an instinct to protect their field; they ran, in his telling, a highly successful misinformation campaign (a “proximal origins” paper written in a week, a Lancet letter) that branded lab-leak questions as conspiracy theory and even made the question partisan; they had all been outspoken advocates of gain-of-function research (Fauci wrote a 2011 Washington Post editorial saying the benefits outweighed the risks), which he says made impartiality psychologically impossible; and they never searched — “they stopped searching, but they never started.”

The searchers, in his account, are outsiders: the loosely affiliated scientists of DRASTIC; US Right to Know, the nonprofit filing most of the FOIA requests (which he personally funds); and five women he names as heroes — molecular biologist Alina Chan, Vanity Fair’s Katherine Eban (“manufactured consensus”), US Right to Know’s Emily Kopp, Princeton’s Zeynep Tufekci (whose post alleging “incontrovertible proof of a conspiracy to cover up” drew 400,000 views), and Alison Young, author of Pandora’s Gamble on pre-COVID lab accidents. The centerpiece of his evidence is the DEFUSE proposal, uncovered by DRASTIC three years after the pandemic began: an EcoHealth Alliance document proposing to connect top virologists at Duke and UNC with Wuhan’s Shi Zhengli to insert a furin cleavage site at the S1/S2 junction of a bat coronavirus — a document he says 78 percent of surveyed scientists had never heard of, and which he calls highest-tier primary evidence. He closes with an ask: fund the searchers, get newsrooms to dive in, and assemble a truly independent commission — “let’s find our Kurokawa and our Feynman” — making it about root cause and prevention, not prosecution and not partisanship, and leaving the audience with Plato: “We can easily forgive a child who is afraid of the dark. The real tragedy in life is when men are afraid of the light.”

Questions this leaves open: if the institutional reflex to circle the wagons is as universal as Gurley says, what structural design — what Kurokawa-style independence guarantees — would make the next commission actually independent? When the experts and the investigators come from the same small field, who is qualified to be a searcher? And if prevention is the goal, what does a CAPA-style process for pandemics even look like — who detects, who investigates, and who has the power to enforce the corrections?

AI · programming · coding agents

Programming Feels Very Different

Ben Davis on how AI coding agents rewired his craft: languages and frameworks are now fungible, dependencies are optional, and the real work moved up to architecture, taste, and knowing what to check.

In “Programming Feels Very Different,” posted by Ben Davis (@bmdavis419), the developer opens with a confession that would have been unthinkable for him a few years ago: he no longer cares about programming languages the way he used to. The frontend framework, the backend language, the way the stack fits together—details he once obsessed over now barely register. He is quick to say what he is not arguing: you cannot vibe-code your way into the void and build anything yet. AI has completely changed how he works, but it has not replaced the work.

His exhibit A is Svelte. Two years ago the SvelteKit 3 release candidate would have been a whole video; today he barely thought about it—even though the amount of Svelte code he ships has gone up exponentially. His personal site, his internal tooling, nearly everything public is Svelte, yet the small things no longer occupy him. The reason, he says, is that agents have gotten absurdly good at abstracting the details away: formatters, imports, the ergonomic niceties he used to touch every day. Even the discourse moved—the old React-versus-Solid-versus-Svelte wars are now arguments about which coding agent is better, and most people have simply centralized on React because the models write it well. He hasn’t; Svelte remains his default.

That raises the question of what makes a technology good in the age of AI. His soft rule: what is good for humans is usually good for agents, with exceptions. Full-stack type safety is one of the non-exceptions—models benefit enormously from types, check commands, lints, and the flood of unit tests they write, mostly as a way to verify their own work. Where humans still win is discernment: frontier models will not out-decide a seasoned engineer, so they need steering. But the ergonomic abstractions—tRPC’s seamlessness versus a plain REST API with generated docs—matter less when you never read the code yourself and the model does the legwork.

Languages, he argues, have become fungible. His local dictation app was built entirely by an agent in Swift, a language he doesn’t write, through screenshot feedback loops until the interface went from horrible to polished. Bun’s migration of its runtime from Zig to Rust on an enormous token budget worked. When a model wrote his new server in Swift simply because he never specified a language, he had subagents plan and port the whole thing to TypeScript. Porting frameworks both ways is equally easy now—it is just a very big, very complex prompt. What still matters is the higher level: HTTP or websockets, payload sizes, where the database lives, authentication, scaling. He has abstracted himself up to the architecture, and the implementation details fade into the background.

He is also shedding dependencies. Where his old projects carried twenty or thirty libraries, he would now rather have the model hand-write anything under a thousand lines into a lib directory and keep package.json as small as humanly possible. The Effect team, he notes, got their TypeScript library down to zero runtime dependencies by writing everything with AI. The flip side is the black box: with agents landing fifteen pull requests overnight at plus two thousand, minus one hundred lines each, reading every line is not feasible. His answer is smell-checking—interrogating the model about how the authentication was implemented, scanning diffs for key tells, like spotting an old Svelte load function where the new remote-functions primitive belongs—reviewing how a piece is used on every pull request while trusting implementations he already vetted. He admits he has no perfect prescription. Pushing agents hard without reading gets great results, but going deeper surfaces architectural improvements, and sometimes the cleanup is for his own satisfaction rather than the product’s.

His generous read is that we are climbing to a new level of abstraction, from line-by-line detail to the composition of functions and services, with human taste doing the steering. There is sadness for the old craft, he says, but also something beautiful: one person, one computer, plus agents running on every machine he owns, and a ceiling for small teams that has risen exponentially. His advice is to embrace it.

If languages and frameworks are fungible, what remains a durable skill—architecture, taste, or just the discernment to catch the model being wrong? When agents ship fifteen pull requests overnight, can smell-checks scale, or does the black box eventually bite? And if everyone centralizes on the same model-friendly defaults, who maintains the foundations that nobody reads anymore?
