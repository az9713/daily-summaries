OpenAI's Head of ChatGPT: We're entering a new era of AI (again) | Tibo Sottiaux

On Lenny's Podcast, "OpenAI's Head of ChatGPT: We're entering a new era of AI (again)", Lenny Rachitsky sits down with Tibo Sottiaux, who leads ChatGPT and Codex at OpenAI. The conversation was recorded in front of a live audience at OpenAI's DevDay, hours after Sottiaux's team launched more than twenty products, and it keeps returning to one claim: the way people use AI is about to change shape again, from apps you operate to an agent that works for you around the clock.

The headline launch is Dots, OpenAI's new personal agent platform. Sottiaux describes it as a permanent, active intelligence that knows your goals and preferences, learns from feedback, and follows you across devices: it can join a meeting and take notes, pick the thread back up in email, and answer a text later, with no model picker and almost no configuration to manage. Everyone starts with one primary Dot. A multi-Dot version is coming, where people will run small virtual teams with assigned roles; Sottiaux already keeps one Dot whose whole job is monitoring Twitter for him, a task he calls the work of an entire Dot on its own. Codex and ChatGPT Work are being merged into ChatGPT to cut complexity, and the plan is to ship Dots' capabilities into ChatGPT itself, lifting the floor for its 1.2 billion users.

Asked how many agents he runs in parallel, Sottiaux says the number breathes. He builds larger and larger teams of agents, then a model breakthrough lands and one bigger agent can do the whole job, so the team shrinks again: expansion, then shrinking, then expansion. This is why he dismisses the current fashion for hand-tuned agent loops and graphs as a passing phase. Setting up and fiddling with loops is something people got excited about, he says, but it is not how things will work. Over time you just want a system that learns what you want to achieve, instead of you specifying exactly how it should loop to get results. He says the same impatience applies to today's interfaces, which feel clunky and will keep feeling clunky until suddenly they do not. The endpoint he imagines is closer to science fiction, the Star Trek computer you simply talk to, or a shared surface where humans and agents sketch ideas together in the room while work gets built in the background.

The sleeper hit of the launch, in his view, is not Dots at all but the open ecosystem around it. OpenAI signed sixteen partners at launch for Sign in with ChatGPT, is opening up plug-ins and extensions with discovery inside ChatGPT, and will share revenue with plug-in makers whose tools see real usage, giving third parties a route to a potential audience of 1.2 billion users. His advice to builders hoping to be discovered is blunt: build a good plug-in. OpenAI tunes recommendations on retention and quality, so a plug-in that people keep using gets surfaced to large slices of users, and one that does not quietly stops being recommended. Being surfaced is earned by usage, not by marketing tricks.

Dots itself rests on more than two years of research into long-running, persistent tasks and memory, much of which already shipped into ChatGPT, where memory has become a beloved feature. The agent runs on its own machine rather than inside your laptop, like an octopus able to reach many devices at once, and OpenAI runs specialist Dots with extra guardrails and monitoring, a few of them on Mac minis. Sottiaux tells the story of his own Dot noticing, minutes before his live DevDay demo, that a ChatGPT production system the demo depended on was down. It pinged him and offered to try fixing it. He declined, telling it he did not think it was there yet, but he calls the judgment behind the warning remarkable: the Dot knew what DevDay was, knew a live demo was coming, connected the outage to it, and decided he would want to know five minutes early.

The middle of the conversation is about how work itself is changing. Sottiaux still merges code occasionally, but Codex writes most of his code now, for analysis, for understanding launches, for digging into the business; the only code he writes by hand is therapeutic weekend LeetCode. A new ultra-fast model has put him back into a flow state he had missed since his ten years as a working engineer. Work will keep changing quite radically, he says, and the industry has not yet solved the human costs: engineers context-switching all day, talking to agents instead of people, and the pressure to simply do more because more is possible. His hope runs the other way. Done right, the technology should reduce noise, demand fewer meetings, and give attention back to people, the way a fully disconnected holiday week makes him think more creatively. Where humans stay valuable, he argues, is in taste, will, and creativity. OpenAI designs its tools as extensions of people rather than replacements, and there may be no coders left before long, but there will be more builders than ever.

That belief shapes hiring. The skill trending down is typing fast. The skills trending up are taste and a felt connection to the people you are building for, which is why ex-founders are thriving at OpenAI: more than 120 former YC founders work there now, and roles are blurring in ways that reward people who were never only a designer or only an engineer. His advice to new grads comes with an example: Ahmed Ibrahim, hired as a new graduate, now carries an enormous responsibility at OpenAI and built much of the Codex harness. What made him stand out was being kind, collaborative, and a sponge, learning at speeds Sottiaux says he has never seen. Inside OpenAI the culture is bottom-up and trusting: the Decisions API began as four people hacking in a Slack channel over a weekend, product leaders can press a reset button without layers of approval, and the deal is that you own the outcome when things break. Sottiaux knows the cost personally. He took production down on his third day at the company, and kept his job.

His sharpest criticism is aimed at builders who plan for today's models. The majority of actions on the internet will soon be taken by agents, he says, models will keep getting cheaper and faster, and everything will feel roughly ten times better within about a year. Products that want agent traffic have to be built for agent scale, as Notion learned when its MCP server drew a flood of agent usage that strained its systems; at the same time, delightful new experiences for humans remain underinvested. He keeps looking at what people are building and thinking, you are not quite getting it: if you imagined all of this ten times better, you would build differently. Capability has already surprised him twice, arriving a year or two ahead of his expectations, and voice surprised him again: he now does much of his work by dictation and by calling his agent, which he had not anticipated at all.

On safety, Sottiaux reframes caution as a engineering budget. For him, pacing the frontier means investing ahead, and investing more than seems necessary, in alignment, safety, and security; OpenAI now spends growing amounts of compute on secondary systems that watch its primary agents for risky actions or signs of prompt injection, and most of the investment in its API stack actually goes into the safety stack. He is proud that OpenAI built a stronger model, 6.1 Astra, and chose not to release it, while shipping a similarly capable but much more efficient one instead. He is optimistic the alignment problems will be solved, and he treats the stakes as commercial as much as philosophical: a product in the hands of 1.2 billion people, many of them not technical, is not something you gamble with. The thing that annoys him most is closer to home. The model picker, the reasoning effort settings, the choice between single and multi-agent: you almost need a PhD in model pickers, he says, and he wants all of it gone so the app can nearly disappear.

Open questions

  1. If the majority of internet actions are soon taken by agents, what does a product even look like when its primary user is software, and who pays for the infrastructure that agent traffic consumes?
  2. Sottiaux promises that a system which simply learns will replace hand-built loops and graphs. What evidence would settle whether learned behavior can be trusted at the scale of 1.2 billion users, and who gets to run that test?
  3. OpenAI held back its strongest model on safety grounds while racing to ship everything else. How long can that restraint survive competition, and what breaks first if it does not?

MTS · Rob Hadick · October 4, 2026

AI Agents Will Cause Mass Subscription Cancellations is an MTS interview with Rob Hadick, a general partner at the crypto venture firm Dragonfly, where he invests in stablecoins, payments, and new financial infrastructure. The conversation is about what happens to consumer finance when personal AI agents start acting on money, and it opens with a shot of reality: a lot of businesses depend on customers who sign up and never cancel.

Hadick starts bearish on the current wave of standalone personal agents, Instinct included. His core claim is that these products have no lock-in at all. Their early users are Silicon Valley power users who will switch to a new chat agent or a new model at the drop of a hat, and a billion dollars of transaction volume, the kind of number these startups like to quote, is actually quite small next to the cost of serving it. A venture-backed agent company has to raise large sums to pay for compute that accrues no durable value to the company itself. A platform like Meta, with Muse, is in a different position: it does not need to monetize the agent itself, because it can monetize the end consumer some other way. The agent layer, in his telling, is a feature that big distribution will absorb.

The famous use case for personal agents, booking flights and hotels, also takes a dent. The median American takes zero flights a year, and slightly less than half of Americans fly at all in a given year, in one of the richest countries in the world. A market built on travel booking is thinner than the demo suggests. Hadick thinks the durable value sits in two places instead: coordination, the endless scheduling and aligning across friends, work, and family, and agents that live inside institutions people already trust with money, above all their bank or a platform that can actually get things done for them today.

That leads to the scenario in the episode's title. An agent can already scan your credit card statements, find subscriptions you have not used in months, and ask whether it should cancel them. Rocket Money does a version of this today, but it is not widely used, and almost nobody's agent does it proactively. Hadick agrees with the host that this would land hard on recurring revenue: an agent that says, you are paying for this thing you have not touched in months, should I cancel it, is a direct threat to every business model built on the long tail of customers who never cancel. He points to the market already sniffing this out: Netflix stock down 19 percent last month, Planet Fitness down a lot, and the telecom and wireless carriers down a decent amount, all of them businesses where people simply continue to pay.

The more dramatic coordination scenario, he thinks, is mostly a phantom. A widely shared warning from Apollo's chief economist held that if everyone's agents simultaneously decide a high yield savings account beats a checking account, the result is a bank run. Hadick doubts the plumbing allows it: general purpose agents reach your bank through Plaid, which rides on slow payment rails, and nothing built on that stack can move mass money quickly or regularly. What is more likely is the agent-first bank. Ask an LLM which bank is agent friendly today and it will name Mercury, not JPMorgan. An institution like that can sweep money from checking to savings to an investment account automatically, because it controls all of those accounts and can monetize each one. The deposits of people who still direct-deposit into a big incumbent bank are unlikely to get swept away to a fintech by an outside agent.

Put stablecoins next to AI, he adds, and money starts moving with very little friction. The money itself is not going away; the surplus is. Value keeps shifting from banks toward fintechs and whoever owns consumer friendly distribution, and the winners will be the agent-first institutions that hold the accounts, set the automated workflows, and find somewhere in that stack to charge.

The same herding problem shows up at the reservation desk. Agents are already spamming booking platforms, Resy has begun cutting off people whose agents do it, and OpenTable has not yet but probably will. Asked why platforms do not simply sell reservations, Hadick gives a curation answer: a hot restaurant does not want its room filled by whoever bids highest, because tastemakers and regulars are worth more over time than one big spender. Hence businesses like Dorsia, which sells access to prime time tables at the most wanted restaurants for a subscription that has climbed from a 5,000 dollar tier to a 25,000 dollar a year tier, without even guaranteeing the table. In the post-agent world, scarce access gets packaged and priced rather than given away to the fastest bot.

Investing gets the longest treatment, through the lens of Robinhood's agent accounts, which Hadick tweeted about and which have drawn roughly 100,000 signups, most of them, he suspects, tinkerers running multiple strategies rather than ordinary savers. If everyone asks their model what to buy, everyone gets roughly the same answer, personalized a little by age, balance, and risk tolerance, and still bunched toward the median. The result is even more crowding into the same winners, Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, and, by definition, no alpha for anyone. His expectation is that most people will not really trade this way; they will end up pushed further into passive funds and direct indexing. The professionals are already quantitative, Jane Street, Millennium, and Citadel squeeze the available alpha thinner every year, and now the NASDAQ shows the strain: about 51 percent of its stocks are in loss this year, with unusually weak correlation between the top 10 percent and the bottom 90 percent, because capital keeps bunching into the AI winners.

Underneath it all sits information asymmetry, the thing Hadick says both venture capital and public markets run on. Warren Buffett's early returns came from reading Moody's manuals and noticing companies selling below net asset value, an edge that evaporates when everyone's agent can do the same scan. Hedge funds spend tens of millions a year on alternative data, once credit card transaction panels sold by firms like Yodlee, which could show Nike's quarterly sales before earnings, now drone photography over construction sites and other niche feeds. As agents make ingesting all of that cheap, the access shield comes down, niche data gets more valuable even as it gets harder to keep exclusive, and the edge migrates to whoever still owns data nobody else can see.

The host signs off by coining a season for all of it: Agentic Finance Fall.

Open questions

  1. If agents really do start cancelling dormant subscriptions at scale, which breaks first, the consumer subscription model or the startups whose recurring revenue quietly depends on people not paying attention?
  2. Hadick argues the winning agents will live inside agent-first banks like Mercury rather than inside standalone apps. What do the big incumbent banks do when the model itself keeps recommending their smaller rivals?
  3. If every investor's agent herds money into the same handful of winners and compresses returns for everyone else, where does genuinely non-consensus information still come from, and who gets to keep it?

Qwen · reasoning tokens · fine-tuning · local models

Which is The Best Qwen3.8-27B?

Sam Witteveen opens with the core frustration of modern reasoning models. The same question goes into two versions of the same model, same weights underneath, and both reach a similar answer. One burns through a long chain of thought to get there. The other uses far fewer thinking tokens. On his machine that gap is the difference between about eighteen seconds and ninety seconds.

That time difference is also a cost difference. Every extra token is time spent waiting and money spent on inference. Sam notes that OpenAI has talked about cutting tokens between its 5.0 and 5.5 models, but the major labs are not alone in chasing shorter reasoning. Open source teams are trying to teach Qwen 3.8 27B to think less while keeping accuracy intact.

Out of the box, Qwen 3.8 27B ships with reasoning effort settings of low, medium, and x-high. X-high is the default. Lowering the setting does shorten the chain of thought, but Sam warns it usually costs accuracy. On a single GPU you are decode bound, so thinking tokens translate almost directly into seconds before you see a final answer. He also points out that multi-token prediction for speculative decoding works better on shorter traces, so thinking less can make each token come out faster too.

Three fine-tunes take three different approaches. ThinkingCap, from BottleCapAI in Prague, aims for a conservative drop-in replacement. It is trained at x-high and focuses on harder tasks like math, reasoning, long context, and agentic work. The goal is teaching the model when it is done, rather than teaching it to be brief across the board. BottleCap reports about 37 percent fewer tokens on average across 12 benchmarks, with accuracy moving from 86.6 to 85.8. On GPQA Diamond, average thinking tokens fall from 12,800 to 7,300. On long context retrieval, thinking drops 39 percent while accuracy actually rises a couple of points. Sam contrasts that with simply dropping the base model to medium, which removes about half the thinking but gives up about nine points of accuracy.

Swift 1.5, from UkisAI, is more aggressive. It claims up to 58.5 percent fewer thinking tokens. The first Swift version looked for specific tokens that trigger overthinking, phrases like “let me reconsider,” and fine-tuned the model to penalize those tokens rather than just penalizing length. Version 1.5 scaled up reinforcement learning and on-policy distillation, with a focus on long horizon agentic and coding tasks. On LiveCodeBench, the model card shows accuracy rising from just under 77 percent at about 11,200 tokens to 81.7 percent at 8,400 tokens. Swift ships in many formats, including GGUF for llama.cpp, MLX, NVFP4, and an AMD build, plus a free research API. Its license is free up to one million dollars in revenue.

QwenPi takes a narrower path. It is Qwen 3.8 27B fine-tuned specifically for Pi, a minimal open source coding agent with a small toolset and a system prompt under 1,000 tokens. Training used supervised fine-tuning on successful Pi sessions, checks that separate working code from broken code, and reinforcement learning with GRPO and a custom reward for reasoning efficiency. The reward targets the low and medium settings, while x-high is left to focus on getting it right. Checkpoints were picked based on actual agent results, not just training loss. Sam says Pi medium matches the base model x-high on Terminal Bench with about 41 percent fewer output tokens. The tradeoff is scope. All the evidence comes from inside Pi, nobody has shown it carries over to other harnesses, and on general reasoning like GPQA the base model still wins at medium. It is also the only one of the three with an Apache 2 license.

Sam then runs all four models side by side on a Dell Pro Max tower with an RTX Pro 6000 and standard vLLM, playing back saved runs at high speed. Across math problems, logic puzzles, and coding prompts, the fine-tunes consistently finish well ahead of the base model, often with the same correct answer. ThinkingCap is fastest most of the time in his tests. Swift wins some rounds, Pi shines on coding tasks, and the order can flip depending on the prompt. One SVG generation test shows each model producing a slightly different result from the same prompt, a reminder that answers without a single exact solution can vary a lot. His closing advice is practical. Try each one on your own workloads, because what a fine-tune was trained to focus on is what it will be good at.

Open questions

  1. How much of the token saving holds up outside the benchmarks each team chose for itself, especially on messy real world prompts?
  2. Will harness-specific fine-tunes like QwenPi stay a niche, or will every popular agent harness end up with its own tuned model?
  3. As licenses move away from Apache 2 toward personal and small business terms, how will that affect adoption in production?

AI agents · the end of the app era · the future of SaaS · DoorDash

The end of the app era: What comes next?

On the Nate B Jones channel (AI News & Strategy Daily), “The end of the app era: What comes next?” argues that the DoorDash announcement of September 30 is a clue to how software will be bought and sold in 2026 and 2027. DoorDash now lets you text an agent and have lunch arrive, and Nate’s claim is that the same pattern is about to reorder business software: you stop opening the app, and the agent does the work the app used to frame.

He builds the case from a recruiting story, anonymized from a real one. A recruiter used to open a recruiting tool every week, copy notes into a spreadsheet, and write up a short list for the hiring manager. Then her agent started pulling candidate profiles from a data provider, drafting the short list, and learning how she screens: what a great candidate looks like for her roles, what particular hiring managers want, who actually gets hired. She still reviews everything, but she has stopped opening the recruiting tool. The company selling the candidate data still gets paid, because the data is still being used. The tool around the data is what got easier to leave.

That asymmetry is the core of the video. Single-purpose tools get easier to leave while the agent you trained gets harder to leave, because the lessons live in your custom harness and your agent’s memory. When leadership later wants a different AI vendor, usually over cost, the conversation turns hard: the CFO is counting token costs while employees are counting weeks of rebuilt workflow. Nate calls this the battle coming to teams in 2027, and notes that vendors are not ready for it because employees have evolved idiosyncratic ways of working with AI faster than anyone has mapped.

So what are you still buying when the agent does the information processing? His answer: information you cannot get elsewhere, and something you need carried out correctly. Much of SaaS sells a muddy bundle of finding, arranging, acting on, and storing information, and an agent that gets good at one slice exposes the whole bundle. To understand a vendor now, you have to reason backward from the data and distribution it already holds to the agent position it can plausibly defend.

He walks the stack from the unexpected to the canonical. Meta, not traditionally a SaaS company at all, launched Muse as a personal agent and then Muse for small business within weeks, adding connections to tools like Shopify and QuickBooks. The read: Meta wants the personal assistant you already use to become business headquarters, and the path from personal to business use at that scale is very short. Salesforce, the company that invented SaaS, sits on data and distribution and is doubling up with Koa, a reasoning model tuned to CRM and announced at Dreamforce on September 15, trained on synthetic data and now in pilot. Salesforce is positioning across three levels at once: the data layer agents desperately need, an agent gateway and control plane that lets other agents in, and its own agent where CRM knowledge helps. Microsoft faces the same dilemma with Azure as the data home, Copilot and a persistent agent called Autopilot on top, and Outlook and Excel underneath, selling a stack of bricks whose top layer is trust: nobody ever got fired for buying it.

The labs start from the opposite corner. OpenAI and Anthropic do not have your data or your distribution, so their pitch is simpler: bet on us for intelligence, and for future intelligence applied to your data. Anthropic has leaned into enterprise while OpenAI is newer there after its consumer success. What buyers actually want, Nate stresses, is optionality, because every business leader is nervous about lock-in. That is why Claude tying up with Salesforce makes sense as a sale: pick Claude, pick Koa, pick another model through the control plane, and the buyer keeps the ability to change their mind.

DoorDash closes the loop from the startup side. Alongside text ordering it announced a limited beta of an MCP server, plugging itself into other people’s agents. DoorDash wants the order whether you type it into their assistant, someone else’s assistant, or a command line. Losing the moment in the interface does not mean losing the transaction, and an agent might even place orders a human would not have bothered to organize. But Nate is careful: shipping an MCP server and an agent is not by itself a strategy. If two providers offer equally good information and equally dependable execution, connecting them makes them easier to compare and replace. Being reachable by agents in 2026 and 2027 gets you considered; it is table stakes. It does not establish why anyone should keep paying you.

Two smaller stories show where the value survives. Crust Data published a case study of a two-person recruiting firm that built a custom Claude skill on Crust Data’s APIs instead of buying another packaged recruiting tool: the firm supplied its own selection criteria, the vendor supplied structured data agents can read, and the workflow was built together on top. The buying decision moved from a point solution with an interface to a data supplier helping construct the workflow. Payroll cuts the other way: getting an AI to explain a paycheck is a very different proposition from replacing the system that gets people paid. Workday’s payroll agent works because of configured records, rules, and approvals underneath, and no startup wants to hand-code payroll rules given the compliance risk. Agents make such systems more valuable, because they help people cut through complexity rather than removing the need for the rails.

The framework he lands on has three layers: the data layer, the application layer, and the agentic layer, and everyone now has to be able to talk about their work in those terms. Individuals should be able to say which data they use, which workflows they still open by hand, and how their agent accelerates them. Buyers have to understand the fingerprint-like pattern of actual AI use across their teams before simplifying, or they will cut productivity they never knew existed. Sellers have to state their answer for 2027, not just today, because buyers are purchasing a belief that the vendor will still be relevant when the next model lands tomorrow. And the application layer is not dead: applications decompose into interfaces and workflows, compliant workflows like Workday’s and ADP’s are not going anywhere, and interfaces will absorb more AI rather than vanish into a command line most knowledge workers fear.

His advice splits by seat. If you are an individual, talk about how you use AI instead of guarding your tricks as a private productivity edge, because a CFO’s vendor decision can quietly strand everything you built. If you lead, do not sit at board level and slap AI over the top; dig in with teams. If you sell software, from Salesforce down to Crust Data, sell the future state and honest outcome acceleration, because businesses are increasingly buying outcomes, and adopting AI is now merely the baseline assumption. The recruiting story returns at the end as the new normal: the recruiter had to explain to her manager that switching AI providers would lose weeks of progress, and Nate’s point is that she was, effectively, a decision maker in how AI is used at her company. That is now true everywhere.

  1. When an employee’s trained agent holds the workflow knowledge, who actually owns that asset, the employee, the team, or the company, and what happens to it when the person leaves or the vendor changes?
  2. If being reachable by agents is only table stakes, what concretely separates the data suppliers who keep getting paid from the ones agents route around, and how would a buyer test that before signing?
  3. Nate is optimistic that software gets reinvented rather than killed, but which parts of today’s SaaS stack fail his own test, the muddy bundles of finding, arranging, and acting that agents can already do, and how quickly do those vendors feel it?

Founder profiles · writing craft · Meta · the American dream

How Mark Zuckerberg, Palmer Luckey, Josh Kushner & Scott Wu Think | Jeremy Stern

David Senra opens his conversation with Jeremy Stern, the editor-in-chief of Colossus, by calling him the best in the world at writing long-form profiles of top investors and entrepreneurs. The episode is a behind-the-scenes tour of four of those profiles, on Mark Zuckerberg, Palmer Luckey, Josh Kushner, and Scott Wu, and of the craft that produced them.

The Zuckerberg profile grew out of an unlikely approach. Stern had earlier written a profile of Palmer Luckey in which Zuckerberg was the villain, the man who had fired or failed to protect Luckey after Luckey’s small contribution to a pro-Trump group in 2016. Zuckerberg gave a comment for that piece, which may have helped reconcile the two men, and afterward his team reached out to Stern. Over five or six months Stern spoke with Zuckerberg repeatedly and interviewed almost 40 other people. He expected an overexposed subject. He found a paradox instead: perhaps the most powerful founder alive, and still, in Senra’s phrase, not fully understood.

Much of the conversation circles Zuckerberg’s lifelong fascination with Augustus, the first Roman emperor. It goes back to his mid-teens. He can still recite Virgil verses memorized in high school Latin at 42. He once ended meetings by declaring that Carthage must be destroyed, and used to shout “domination” until Sean Parker told him to stop because it could be quoted in an antitrust suit. He recently named his personal agent Agrippa, after Augustus’s loyal lieutenant. Stern reads the fascination as partly self-portrait: Zuckerberg describes Augustus as turning a pillage-based economy into a trade-based one, while glossing over the standing army and the end of political liberty that made it possible.

Two things, people around Zuckerberg told Stern, are consistently underestimated. The first is how ruthless his competitive drive is. A former researcher who now works at a rival lab called him the Terminator, always discontent, always moving forward. The second is that he is, by most accounts including his critics’ and rivals’, a decent person to work for. His own account of his edge is unglamorous: sitting with a team and methodically improving one product over decades, with no need to raise money or ask anyone’s permission. Stern argues that this makes Meta quietly well positioned in AI, since roughly half of humanity already uses WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, or Messenger, and two decades of unsexy infrastructure work sit underneath. And he doubts Zuckerberg will ever step back. When Yahoo offered a billion dollars in 2006, Zuckerberg, then 21 or 22 and owning most of the company, said no immediately, and his management team walked away. In Stern’s telling, he is addicted to building and will simply never quit.

The Luckey profile came first, and it was the first profile of a person Stern had ever written. It began with a question, not a person: after October 7, when Israel’s sophisticated defenses were breached by hang gliders and construction equipment, is defense technology real, and how far away is it? Luckey, founder of Oculus and then Anduril, was the way in. A planned short piece became a 15,000-word cradle-to-grave portrait. What struck Stern was character: Luckey had backed Trump when doing so cost him his company, not when it became fashionable in Silicon Valley.

Scott Wu of Cognition drew Stern for almost opposite reasons. With public frustration at the major AI figures rising, he went looking for a major AI figure who is a humanist, someone you might actually trust with the technology, and Wu’s name came up again and again. Among Olympiad gold medalists in math, physics, and programming, Wu is regarded as the greatest competitor and coach of his generation, a natural leader not yet 30. He is also fiercely competitive at everything, the kind of person who will turn five idle minutes into a game. Wu has refused large acquisition offers and comes across, in Stern’s reading, as the same person in every setting, unstage-managed and introspective.

The Josh Kushner profile is where the episode turns personal. Stern had been interested in the Kushner family for years: grandparents who survived the Holocaust with the Bielski partisans, a father who built a New Jersey real estate empire, and within one grandmother’s lifetime, presidents and prime ministers coming to dinner. The profile opens with Kushner visiting Rick Rubin to ask, in effect, what do I do if I lose the magic? Stern heard in that question a specifically Holocaust-family fear, that everything built can be taken away overnight. For Stern, the grandson of survivors himself, the piece became a check on whether the American dream is still alive, and Thrive Capital’s founder became evidence that it is.

Running underneath all four portraits is an argument about writing itself. Stern came to Colossus to bring literary ambition, drawn from fiction and poetry, to business and technology, the figures he sees as the most culturally central in American life right now. He cites David Samuels calling the long-form magazine profile America’s only indigenous form of writing, heir to the New Yorker of the 1940s and Esquire of the 1960s. He does not mine his subjects for life lessons and claims he forgets them once a piece ships. Senra presses him repeatedly on what the work has done to him, and Stern mostly declines, quoting David Foster Wallace on writers as natural-born watchers who dread the tables being turned. The one debt he does name is to his college Russian professor, Natalya, who taught him that having a gift creates an obligation to use it, and that wasting it is a kind of sin. He named his daughter after her.

Open questions

  1. Can the long-form profile, rebuilt inside a business publication, actually restore the literary tradition Stern admires, or does the format depend on the magazine world that no longer exists?
  2. Is Zuckerberg’s Augustus self-image a useful lens on how he runs Meta, or a flattering story that obscures the costs of one-man rule?
  3. As public frustration with AI’s leading figures grows, will builders in Scott Wu’s mold, technical, humanist, and unbought, end up holding real power in the industry?
