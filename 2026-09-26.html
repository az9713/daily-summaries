How I'm Preparing For The Ai Revolution

Chamath Palihapitiya on how he’s preparing for the AI revolution — doomerism, his personal AI toolkit, taste as the scarce skill, and what kids should study.

Chamath Palihapitiya's latest video on his Social Capital channel opens with a framing he clearly wants people to hold onto: an AI tsunami is coming, and the useful question isn't whether jobs will change but how each of us adopts the tools to come out ahead.

He pushes back hard on the doomerism. Yes, some jobs will disappear — but he argues that in the fullness of time, AI will have created far more jobs than it took away. The people here today will simply be doing something different and new.

The middle of the video is the most concrete part: his personal AI toolkit. He describes what amounts to a "life model" — a set of curated agents he maintains across the foundational models. There's a Nash equilibrium agent for high-stakes negotiations, built to surface multi-party dynamics, trade-offs, and hidden players; it's layered with a psychological filter drawn from business and psychology books (Robert Greene, Dale Carnegie, the Four Agreements) that checks his own weaknesses before a big decision.

Then there's the practical machinery: a burn-management agent running portfolio optimization and Monte Carlo simulations so his interest income covers his lifestyle net of taxes — rooted in his rule of never living in debt, which he says gives him complete optionality. He and his wife use a health stack around blood testing and nutrition. He's even asked it questions about parenting.

His core point about all of this: the models themselves are excellent and interchangeable — he names them casually — but the real work is curating the microservices on top. You have to write the guidelines, refine them, judge the output against your own taste. Without that management, he jokes, the AI would just tell him to sauna like every cold-plunging goofball. The skill is guiding the AI on how to think about what's next.

From there he zooms out to what this means for everyone else. His thesis is taste and judgment. Find where you genuinely enjoy spending your time, and you'll have infinite hours to pour into it — AI handles the drudgery. A loan officer whose paperwork is automated still has to say yes or no; the judgment call remains human. The practical advice: build these tools for yourself, apply them to your life as a test, and learn what the technology actually is — and isn't. It's not a super-gun.

On what kids should study, he's honest that he doesn't know. If it's about taste and judgment, engineering and physics teach you how the world works (the judgment side); incentives and psychology — something like PPE, philosophy, politics, economics — cover the taste side.

The closing chapter is the most ambitious. He admits we know almost nothing: we know the models are good; whether they destroy jobs or create abundance is conjecture, and everyone has incentives to be right on one side. What we do know is that we live in a hyper-amplified social media environment optimized for clicks, so misinformation around AI gets supercharged. He draws the parallel to the industrial revolution — net-net, incredibly positive for humanity, despite the mess.

His call to action: today's capitalism is atomized, every company for itself. At the start of the industrial revolution, figures like Carnegie, J.P. Morgan, Jay Gould, and Rockefeller realized industrialization's benefits accrued to them and marshaled resources to pay it forward — libraries, schools, infrastructure — so everyone could ride the upward trajectory. AI's leaders need to do the same as a group, and establish the trust to be believed. Without that, he warns, we get stuck in a messy middle of mistrust — and the framing of AI remains poorly explained, which he calls an enormous failure of the AI community right now.

Open questions

  1. If AI leaders won't coordinate to "pay it forward" the way industrial titans funded libraries and schools, what institution actually could — or does the messy middle just arrive?
  2. Chamath's life model depends on obsessive curation — is taste itself the new scarce skill, or can it be taught at scale?
  3. He bets engineering and physics for judgment, incentives and psychology for taste — but what does schooling look like if both turn out to be wrong?
