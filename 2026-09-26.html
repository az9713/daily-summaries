How I'm Preparing For The Ai Revolution

Chamath Palihapitiya on how he’s preparing for the AI revolution — doomerism, his personal AI toolkit, taste as the scarce skill, and what kids should study.

Chamath Palihapitiya's latest video on his Social Capital channel opens with a framing he clearly wants people to hold onto: an AI tsunami is coming, and the useful question isn't whether jobs will change but how each of us adopts the tools to come out ahead.

He pushes back hard on the doomerism. Yes, some jobs will disappear — but he argues that in the fullness of time, AI will have created far more jobs than it took away. The people here today will simply be doing something different and new.

The middle of the video is the most concrete part: his personal AI toolkit. He describes what amounts to a "life model" — a set of curated agents he maintains across the foundational models. There's a Nash equilibrium agent for high-stakes negotiations, built to surface multi-party dynamics, trade-offs, and hidden players; it's layered with a psychological filter drawn from business and psychology books (Robert Greene, Dale Carnegie, the Four Agreements) that checks his own weaknesses before a big decision.

Then there's the practical machinery: a burn-management agent running portfolio optimization and Monte Carlo simulations so his interest income covers his lifestyle net of taxes — rooted in his rule of never living in debt, which he says gives him complete optionality. He and his wife use a health stack around blood testing and nutrition. He's even asked it questions about parenting.

His core point about all of this: the models themselves are excellent and interchangeable — he names them casually — but the real work is curating the microservices on top. You have to write the guidelines, refine them, judge the output against your own taste. Without that management, he jokes, the AI would just tell him to sauna like every cold-plunging goofball. The skill is guiding the AI on how to think about what's next.

From there he zooms out to what this means for everyone else. His thesis is taste and judgment. Find where you genuinely enjoy spending your time, and you'll have infinite hours to pour into it — AI handles the drudgery. A loan officer whose paperwork is automated still has to say yes or no; the judgment call remains human. The practical advice: build these tools for yourself, apply them to your life as a test, and learn what the technology actually is — and isn't. It's not a super-gun.

On what kids should study, he's honest that he doesn't know. If it's about taste and judgment, engineering and physics teach you how the world works (the judgment side); incentives and psychology — something like PPE, philosophy, politics, economics — cover the taste side.

The closing chapter is the most ambitious. He admits we know almost nothing: we know the models are good; whether they destroy jobs or create abundance is conjecture, and everyone has incentives to be right on one side. What we do know is that we live in a hyper-amplified social media environment optimized for clicks, so misinformation around AI gets supercharged. He draws the parallel to the industrial revolution — net-net, incredibly positive for humanity, despite the mess.

His call to action: today's capitalism is atomized, every company for itself. At the start of the industrial revolution, figures like Carnegie, J.P. Morgan, Jay Gould, and Rockefeller realized industrialization's benefits accrued to them and marshaled resources to pay it forward — libraries, schools, infrastructure — so everyone could ride the upward trajectory. AI's leaders need to do the same as a group, and establish the trust to be believed. Without that, he warns, we get stuck in a messy middle of mistrust — and the framing of AI remains poorly explained, which he calls an enormous failure of the AI community right now.

Open questions

  1. If AI leaders won't coordinate to "pay it forward" the way industrial titans funded libraries and schools, what institution actually could — or does the messy middle just arrive?
  2. Chamath's life model depends on obsessive curation — is taste itself the new scarce skill, or can it be taught at scale?
  3. He bets engineering and physics for judgment, incentives and psychology for taste — but what does schooling look like if both turn out to be wrong?

What Happens When Intelligence Becomes Almost Free

Salim Ismail on what happens when intelligence becomes almost free — the Jev model, System 1 vs System 2, confidence-based governance, and the Jevons paradox.

Salim Ismail's latest video is about a new model called Jev, and he thinks it may be one of the most important developments in AI since large language models arrived. Jev is not another giant chatbot trying to out-reason GPT or Claude. It is built for something smaller and, in his view, far bigger: making decisions really fast.

His core point is that most real decisions are not essay questions. Inside a company, the typical decision is "is this customer likely to churn," "should this invoice be approved," "which sales lead should I call first." That is judgment, not poetry, and today's software cannot do it. Software is deterministic — humans write the rules — while reality is fuzzy. So companies insert humans everywhere to absorb the fuzziness: managers, analysts, approvers, coordinators. Ismail puts a striking number on it: roughly three-quarters of all activity inside a company is that fuzzy judgment and coordination layer.

Jev's founders, the Typesafe team, call it a System 1 model, borrowing Daniel Kahneman's famous distinction from Thinking, Fast and Slow. System 2 is slow, deliberate reasoning — the neocortex weighing whether to offer a guest a meal or just coffee. System 1 is the fast reflex, like hitting the brakes.

Until now, AI has been almost entirely System 2: you don't want a giant reasoning model burning tokens every time your app must decide whether an email is urgent. Ismail's image is that it's like calling the CEO every time someone needs to decide which elevator to take. You want cheap, fast, reflexive intelligence at the bottom, agents above it, deep reasoning models above that, and humans where appropriate — something closer to a nervous system, where your autonomic functions handle enormous complexity without conscious thought.

That lands squarely on Ismail's long-running thesis, the organizational singularity: what happens when coordination becomes nearly free. Here it's judgment becoming nearly free. Organizations grow bureaucratic precisely because people need coordinators, and coordinators need coordinators. If software can make probabilistic judgments at every decision point — sensing, classifying, routing, approving, escalating — management activity that today lives in meeting rooms and spreadsheets becomes executable in code, without anyone writing a million rules.

The name itself is a nod to the economist William Stanley Jevons, and Ismail leans hard on Jevons paradox. When steam engines got more efficient, coal consumption exploded, because cheaper energy made energy viable in far more places. The London M25 was built without accounting for it and filled up in months instead of a decade.

Intelligence, he argues, will follow the same curve: make it ten times cheaper and you won't use a tenth as much — you'll use far more, in places where it would previously have been absurdly expensive. When photography became essentially free, we didn't take the same twenty-four frames per roll; we took billions, and the whole problem shifted from composition to organization. When intelligence becomes nearly free, the question stops being "where should we use AI" and the answer becomes "everywhere."

Jev's design choice matters here too: it does not generate arbitrary text but returns constrained probabilities. Ismail flags this as the fix for one of generative AI's biggest enterprise headaches — an LLM that occasionally writes you a sonnet instead of the JSON you asked for is charming in chat and dangerous in a payments system. He is careful about the vendor's "zero hallucinations" claim: a model can return a perfectly formatted answer and still make the wrong decision. What he thinks is genuinely solved is the failure surface — it's bounded and constrained.

That boundedness enables his most concrete idea: confidence-based governance. At 99.9 percent confidence, execute. At 95 percent, execute but audit. At 75 percent, send it to a different model. At 55 percent, send it to a human. Instead of the crude question of whether AI or a person should decide, you build a ladder: what level of confidence requires what level of governance. When judgment calls are nearly free, that ladder becomes the architecture.

He is honest about the hype boundary. Jev may succeed or fail; some other architecture may beat it, and its strongest benchmark numbers come from Typesafe's own evaluations, with the company itself acknowledging bias and urging private testing.

The point, he says, isn't the winner — it's that a whole new category exists. We spent years asking how intelligent one giant model can get. The better question now is how cheaply intelligence can be distributed everywhere. The future organization may not have a giant AI on top; it may have millions of tiny intelligences making continuous judgments, escalating to humans when needed — not an org chart anymore, but a nervous system. And once judgment is cheap enough to put everywhere, you don't get better software. You start redesigning the organization around it.

Open questions

  1. who owns the accountability when a confidence ladder fails — when the 99.9 percent judgment is the wrong one, is it the model vendor, the deployer, or the manager who set the threshold?
  2. if Jevons paradox holds and organizations consume vastly more intelligence than before, do total AI energy and compute costs explode even as the per-decision price collapses — and who plans for that?
  3. Ismail sketches a ladder where 55 percent confidence routes to a human, but humans reviewing thousands of machine-flagged edge cases suffer alert fatigue; what actually keeps the human in the loop engaged and competent when judgment is otherwise free?

Boz to the Future Episode 28: The Future According to Aparna Chennapragada

Meta CTO Andrew “Boz” Bosworth and Aparna Chennapragada on the future of wearables — the Meta charm, agents, private processing, and attention.

This episode of Boz to the Future, Meta CTO Andrew "Boz" Bosworth's podcast, was recorded at Meta Connect 2026. His guest is Aparna Chennapragada, Meta's VP of Product for Wearables, who joined the company in June and was on the Connect stage twelve weeks later.

Boz opens with a long recap of the Connect announcements. The headline hardware: Meta VR glasses at one hundred grams with split-compute architecture, cinema-grade video, typing on any surface, and VR gaming. Then audio-only glasses that drop the camera to reach a thinner form factor. Then hearing enhancement for Ray-Ban Meta — an FDA-certified feature for mild-to-moderate hearing loss, set up with a test in the app, far cheaper than traditional hearing aids. New styles everywhere, including a Lisa collaboration, aviators and Zenas frames from Ray-Ban's parent, and over a hundred total styles.

Plus a small handheld device Meta calls the charm: pocketable, worn on a bag or wrist, that you whisper notes to and get a haptic confirmation back. Day two covered developer surfaces and a new AI-based Horizon platform. The thread through all of it, Boz says, is Muse — the personal agent at the operating system level of glasses and headset alike, with agent connectors announced for commerce, travel, and more.

Aparna's own story is the episode's spine. Her through line, she says, is taking brutally hard computer science problems and turning them into products ordinary people can use — Google Search, Google Now before assistants were a thing, Google Lens, then agents.

She joined Meta because AR and the new generation of AI clicked together like jigsaw pieces: computing that's with you rather than in front of you. On the personal side, she grew up as the only non-doctor in a family full of them, with a mother who ran one of the biggest hospitals and a father who sculpted by night — art and technology fused early. Her seventeen-year-old son, a music producer and tech nerd, is her toughest critic. And her mother, eighty-five with hearing loss, makes the hearing enhancement deeply personal for her.

The hearing feature gets real emotional weight. Both of them describe hearing loss not as a technical problem but as something that changes a personality — the life of the party starts hanging back, others tiptoe around repeating themselves. It's the difference between isolation and connection. Aparna cites research tying untreated sensory loss to downstream Alzheimer's and dementia risk, and both note the stigma that keeps older people — especially men — away from hearing aids. A pair of glasses that also helps you hear carries no such stigma.

From there the conversation moves to what agents actually change. Aparna's thesis: voice is the interface, no menus, no settings — and the semantic leap means you no longer have to be painstakingly precise. She discovered she could mumble near-unintelligibly and the agent would just do the right thing, and that collapse in required precision makes you ask for far more. Boz's analogy: when phone cameras removed the "is this photo worth it?" friction, photo counts didn't rise twenty or thirty times — they exploded by orders of magnitude. Agents do the same to requests.

The examples make it concrete. A teammate with a chipped windshield told his glasses agent one sentence — where he'd park — and came out at the end of the day to a repaired car, the agent having booked Safelite and handled the logistics.

Aparna spotted a pop-art piece at a friend's house and asked her agent to fuse that style with Indian-mythology art from a Pixar animator she admires: ten generated images, Instagram profiles of similar artists surfaced, and she's now collaborating with one of them — a project she says she simply never would have started without the agent. And Boz had Muse build, in one shot, a video site for ten-year-old family VHS footage he'd digitized but never published — a decade-long to-do item done in an afternoon. Her summary: imagine a to-do list that does things.

They sketch two arcs for wearables' future. The tech arc is the model getting better — not just smarter, but better at personality, presence, conversationality.

Boz says nobody needs a Fields medalist for daily life; they need help with an annoying customer-support agent. Both insist consumer AI's benchmark is nothing like academic evals: nobody goes through their day wishing they could solve an Olympiad problem, so the industry needs new evals for everyday life — Aparna calls them the new PRDs, the new APIs. The fashion arc is weight, battery, comfort, and style diversity: glasses have to be independently desirable to wear, because humans are exquisitely sensitive to what sits on their face. Early 150-gram glasses were dead on arrival.

Then there's the bystander loop: Meta announced private processing, sending glasses queries end-to-end encrypted to a hardware-assisted trusted execution environment, like WhatsApp's model. The pitch is that both the wearer and the people around them need to feel comfortable — a single-player experience and a multiplayer one.

Attention gets its own chapter. Boz's story: his son's soccer practice was canceled by email, the family re-planned the whole carpool orchestration, then a follow-up email un-canceled it — and his Muse assistant pushed exactly one notification, "soccer is un-canceled, undo the plan change," ignoring everything else. That's the difference between managing the device and the device managing you.

His principle for notifications is the ratio of delightful moments to terrible ones, and AI finally has a chance at getting it right because it understands context phones never could. Aparna adds that you have to coach your agent like a human assistant — she told hers she wants short, tight answers — and it converges on your taste fast.

The philosophy section is quietly a rebuke of the AI industry's dominant discourse. Boz argues you don't have to worry about alignment in consumer tools the way labs worry about it, because misalignment is self-correcting: a drill that sometimes refuses to drill is a drill nobody buys. Consumers are the toughest crowd on the planet — they'd rather have one thing work essentially every time than everything work most of the time.

Their focus is deliberately "normie": not eating jobs or paperclips, but saving you money on car insurance, making life a little better. Aparna points to the custom "Jolly" avatar — a fuzzy little guy who puts on headphones to shop for you — as a disarming view of the technology that trivializes nothing.

On Meta's culture, Aparna is candid: the ambition is pointed and opinionated — "plan and build for success" — not a sprawling index fund of bets. The flip side is an utter disregard for sunk cost that can make people queasy, plus a carrying cost for keeping option value open.

Boz's illustration: the charm was conceived in March and ships in December only because several in-flight projects could pivot into it. His own conviction story: after the first-generation Ray-Ban Stories, he told Mark they shouldn't ship the second generation — the first hadn't lit the world on fire. Mark overruled him, and that bet is why Meta now leads the category.

They close on optimism. Both want more optimistic science fiction — Bosworth notes tech products have always been sequels to a sixties sci-fi novel, and Star Trek remains his pinnacle. Aparna's advice to users: stop taking the flying car to the grocery store; push these models and devices hard, and the mental offload repays you. To builders: the future isn't model-generated and synthetic, it's model-generated and tastemaker-driven — deeply personal, and everyone is a developer now.

Open questions

  1. the numbers Boz cites — consumer products needing to work essentially every time — is that standard actually achievable for agents that browse, pay, and act in the open world, or does the physics of probabilistic models make a different social contract inevitable?
  2. private processing is presented as WhatsApp-style end-to-end encryption for glasses queries — but how far can on-device trust really extend when the queries carry video of everyone around the wearer, and will the bystander loop accept the explanation?
  3. Meta's culture bets on keeping option value alive until the last moment — the charm, the second-generation glasses. At what scale does that ambient option tax become a liability, and is "conviction in public" a strategy only a company with Meta's resources can afford?

Why AI's Next Breakthroughs Could Come from Outside the Big Labs

a16z partners on why AI’s next breakthroughs could come from outside the big labs — pacing, regulation, agent swarms, and security.

This is a fifty-five minute panel of a16z partners chewing through the state of AI safety discourse, recorded with the loose energy of colleagues who've been arguing about this for years. The center of gravity is Dario Amodei's recent "pacing the frontier" post — the proposal that frontier labs deliberately slow or stage their releases around better security and testing — and the political fallout that followed it.

The panelists say they broadly agreed with the substance of the post. Nobody disputes that frontier models need better security, sandboxing, and testing; enterprise diffusion, they argue, is impossible without trustworthy systems. But they think the framing is broken.

"Pacing" is a fuzzy non-word, one of them says — you can't pace against a schedule that was never published, and labs running at a dead run with record fundraising can't claim to be pacing while they're at it. The message tries to split the difference between the pause-the-work faction inside the labs and the regulators outside them, and it ends up making both sides unhappy. Worse, the atmospherics are off: an employee claiming a ten percent chance of species extinction one day, and the CEO nodding along on television while publishing a measured technical proposal the next, reads as incoherent.

That incoherence, the panel argues, is a political disaster. The labs have never taken a clear position on extinction risk — one says they should state outright whether they believe their work could wipe out the species — and as long as the official-adjacent rhetoric stays at "maybe ten percent," any regulator would be negligent not to act.

The panelists' fear is regulatory capture and worse: a demand for "pacing" becomes a license for politicians to set the velocity themselves, and the only guaranteed outcome of talking to government, they say, is that nobody gets what they asked for. The two thousand twenty-eight election, one predicts, will be a referendum on AI, and the pro-AI case is losing the vocabulary battle — every word in the debate, from "pause" to "rogue swarms," was chosen by the skeptics.

The conversation gets much warmer when it turns to something concrete: cybersecurity. The panelists trade war stories about old-school side channels — monitors leaking electromagnetic signals, keyboards betraying which keys were worn clean — and argue that the first productive meeting between the safety community and the security community is finally happening.

Agent swarms, they say, completely flip the internal threat model. Corporate systems were built on the assumption that people do the right thing almost all the time; swarms are ten thousand roaming software drones that will happily mistake a bad task for a good one. The access models are too coarse and too slow, and a renaissance in secure-by-design systems — maybe even a return to the probabilistic-programming ideas of nineteen-sixties computer science — may be needed.

The title's thesis lands in the final stretch. The panel discusses a new class of models that skip text generation entirely: instead of writing out long responses, the model reads text and picks the best option from a set, which is faster, cheaper, and more accurate for embedding AI into traditional software.

That innovation, the panel notes, didn't come from a frontier lab — it came from outside. The labs, one says, are trying to build beings; beings speak natural language. The people trying to wire AI into actual software don't need a being, they need a decision engine. The center of innovation, the panel concludes, has moved: the platform layer has hit critical mass, and the breakthroughs are coming from the outside in.

Open questions

  1. will any frontier lab ever take a clean, public position on extinction risk — either "we believe this is a real nonzero risk and here is what follows" or "we don't believe our work causes this" — and if none does, is heavy-handed regulation truly inevitable?
  2. can enterprise security actually be rebuilt for a world of agent swarms — granular, high-performance access control across every internal API — before a serious incident forces the issue?
  3. is Europe's GDPR instinct — a consent prompt on every agent action touching a third-party product — the likely middle ground for regulation, and would that make AI products unusable in the way consent banners made the web numb?

The collie and the pug | HackMIT keynote 2026

3Blue1Brown’s Grant Sanderson on the collie and the pug — what happens to building and understanding when AI decouples them.

Grant Sanderson, the creator of 3Blue1Brown, opened his HackMIT 2026 keynote with a story about two dogs. A border collie and a pug, both adopted as puppies. On the way home the collie is giddy — all the running, all the chasing, all the catching ahead of them. The pug just shrugs: you are living in the past. The owners will hand you every meal. You will never once need to hunt.

They grow old living exactly that life. The collie sprints after frisbees at the park every single day; the pug curls up in a lap. At the end, the pug says, see? I was right. You never once needed to run. And the collie answers: I didn't run to live. I was living to run.

Sanderson tells this story because the AI moment has decoupled two things that used to be joined: understanding something and building something. Not long ago, the rate limit on what you could create was how deeply you understood the technically complicated parts underneath. That limit is dissolving. From an abundance standpoint, that's wonderful — the only constraint left is your ability to add value to other people's lives.

But from an educational standpoint, he calls it a real shame. One of the best ways to learn has always been to pick a project and build around it. He has watched friends who never engaged with math in school fall in love with linear algebra and trigonometry later, simply because they wanted to make a video game and the matrices were the rate limiter. Learning to code reshapes how you think, and that reshaping came bundled with the building.

Nobody knows this more intimately than he does. The most fun coding of his life, he says, went into Manim, the animation library that now powers 3Blue1Brown. Its earliest seeds were planted at a Stanford hackathon, where he wanted a personal project to understand how computer graphics work. Over the years he kept tuning it — implementing more than he needed to, understanding how things worked down to why each pixel is the color it is — like an old car he kept in the garage, never taken to the mechanic.

Then the vibe shift. For a long while, LLM coding tools were most useful where he wasn't an expert; where he was expert, hand-writing was still faster. In recent months that flipped. Agentic tools got good at the expert-level stuff too, and suddenly amplifying what he could build meant he could keep extending Manim without understanding the new pieces the way he understood the old ones. That triggered a genuine introspective moment: he could feel the decoupling, and part of him was willing to let go of the deep understanding, let the tool be more robust that way.

But he also felt like the collie. He just wants to understand how things work. The abstraction argument doesn't settle it for him: yes, you don't need C to write Python, and nobody debugging at one layer needs the layers below — but he senses something qualitatively different about this latest jump, even if the rigor of the old layers carried benefits you could feel.

So he leaves the room without a clean answer. The parable doesn't even fully hold, he admits: the pug isn't lazy, and there's real value in shipping things that make people's lives better. The question he poses instead is about intention. Are you learning things so you can build, or is the building itself the joy? This weekend, do you want to be the pug, or do you want to be the collie?

Open questions

  1. if understanding is no longer the rate limiter on building, what becomes the new training ground for the kind of thinking that used to arrive bundled with the struggle — and can anything replace it?
  2. Sanderson senses a qualitative difference in the AI layer of abstraction versus earlier ones. What exactly is different this time — is it that the layer hides not just mechanics but the reasoning itself?
  3. he admits the parable falls short: the pug ships value while the collie runs for joy. Is there a version of the story where the two dogs are the same person at different moments — and how do you know which one the moment calls for?
