Baseten · continual learning · catastrophic forgetting

AI researchers talk continual learning over a game of HORSE

On Baseten, "AI researchers talk continual learning over a game of HORSE" puts Charlie O'Neill, co-head of model training at Baseten, on a basketball court with Jack Morris, co-founder and head of research at Engram. The format is simple: whoever makes a shot gets to ask the next question. The conversation that follows is a tour of continual learning, covering where new knowledge should live in a model, whether forgetting is really a defect, the state of research publishing, what standard evaluations fail to measure, and whether distillation can take over from reinforcement learning.

The opening question is when learning belongs in the weights and when it belongs in the activations, meaning the KV cache a model builds up at inference time. O'Neill says frontier models now spend the large majority of their tokens searching, gathering the right information into context through harnesses and subagents. Re-deriving the same knowledge on every run is wasteful, he argues, like a basketball player who still has to think about every dribble. Used knowledge should become automatic by moving into the weights. The problem, from Engram's recent research, is that writing knowledge into weights reliably breaks something. Across thousands of continual learning episodes, every method they tried damaged at least one of three things: the base model's existing knowledge near the task, which is the familiar catastrophic forgetting; knowledge the model had itself picked up in earlier continual learning; or general capabilities such as instruction following and reasoning. His conclusion is that models need a middle layer, a compressed parametric memory that is denser than a raw KV cache but safer to edit than the weights themselves, though nobody yet knows how to move information from such a cache into the weights without breaking things.

Morris adds a distinction borrowed from mechanistic interpretability: having information stored in a model is not the same as having the circuits that retrieve and compose it. Engram's work shows that after a model appears to forget something, the information is often still there. Feed the answer back in and the loss on those tokens stays low, well below where it was before training, yet the model will not produce the answer on its own, because the retrieval circuitry has been overwritten by newer facts. Forgetting, in other words, can be a wiring failure rather than an erasure.

Asked whether future memory search will happen in latent space rather than over text files, Morris is permissive: training KV caches, updating weights, LoRA adapters, steering vectors, and mixture-of-experts updates all look valid to him. He pictures a single model that updates continuously, forgets some things, and can simply be reminded, much like a person. Learned KV caches are attractive mostly because the systems side is easy, with mature software for loading caches and running inference, not because learning fundamentally has to happen there.

On forgetting itself, Morris is blunt: it is overblown, and a soft form of it is desirable. Forcing a model to erase something completely, the goal of unlearning research, is probably impossible anyway. Specialization changes the calculus, since a model fine-tuned for one startup's coding work loses nothing important when it forgets restaurants in Kazakhstan; it can be told again when travel planning comes up. The big labs face the harder version, folding six more months of the internet into a general model without disturbing what is already there. Both men are drawn to adding capacity instead, whether through effectively endless trainable caches, extra experts, or reserved layers that stay empty during pre-training and grow later, along the lines of nested learning research.

The middle of the game turns to research publishing, which O'Neill, fresh from reviewing for summer conferences, calls a mess: papers written by AI, reviews written by AI, and rebuttals that arrive implausibly fast. Morris names two pressures. Noise has exploded and now reads as polished as real work, with submission volumes at venues like NeurIPS up sharply in a few years. At the same time, the frontier labs have normalized releasing little, so nobody has settled what good sharing looks like anymore. A paper alone cannot reproduce a modern model, because results depend on a long tail of decisions behind the scenes. What he wants is a modern successor to Distill, the interactive journal Chris Olah ran before Anthropic: fewer, deeper, more explorable publications. The risk is gatekeeping, since any threshold produces false negatives for newcomers. His proposed reallocation is to spend fewer tokens generating and more verifying, with agent swarms reproducing results from paper and code. Baseten already works this way internally, forcing experiment plans through a rigorous LLM-checked process where every assumption is stated and ticked off, then using agents to review each step afterward, a routine he says has caught many errors. In a lighter moment, the two agree that the LeBron of AI researchers is Noam Shazeer, and the Michael Jordan is Michael I. Jordan of Berkeley.

Morris also argues that open research inside a company like Baseten currently offers a rare best of both worlds. The old trade, academic freedom against industry resources, has softened because businesses built on open models need answers to broad questions about memory and continual learning, and greedy selection of the most obvious next problem does not compound. A small industry lab can fund a multi-year blue-sky program and still ship.

His candidate for the field's most neglected problem is understanding what open weights models leak and what they can actually do beyond headline evaluations. Two leading coding models post nearly identical, highly correlated scores, yet feel completely different in use, and their errors are uncorrelated, which is exactly why people ensemble them, planning with one and reviewing with the other. Evals flatten these very different artifacts into a single number. Leakage compounds the puzzle: closed models shed text all over the internet, so open models end up inadvertently distilled from them, some even believing they are Claude, and future pre-training runs will read accounts of events like the Mythos episode and absorb beliefs from them. Removing material from pre-training data barely helps, since a model can reconstruct a famous book from the quotes and summaries around it, and even a suspicious absence tells a smart model that something happened.

The final topic is distillation versus RL for continual learning. The conventional split says distillation is weak at long-horizon skills and RL is weak at teaching knowledge, since a fact the model never samples has no chance of being reinforced. Morris pushes back on the first half. On-policy self-distillation, where the teacher is a copy of the student given privileged information, delivers feedback on every token rather than a single reward bit at the end, and could be far more sample efficient than RL if it worked. It does not yet: nobody has made it stable, which he takes to be why it has not displaced RL and fine-tuning. The fix he is excited about is pedagogical RL, training the teacher to produce only trajectories that are both correct and reachable by the student, so supervision stays on policy. He expects large post-training runs built mostly or entirely on distillation within five years, pointing to multi-teacher on-policy distillation already used to merge expert models, and to a possible future where one enormous teacher warm-starts the training of whole model families beneath it. The game itself ends in a tiebreaker, still undecided when they walk off, which both seem to consider a fair result.

Open questions

  1. What mechanism can write knowledge into a model across thousands of continual learning episodes without damaging prior knowledge or general capability, and will it look like a compressed trainable cache, added capacity, or something else entirely?
  2. Can on-policy self-distillation be made stable, perhaps by constraining the teacher to student-reachable trajectories, well enough to carry large-scale post-training on its own within the next few years?
  3. What replaces the conference paper as the field's unit of shared knowledge, and can agent-driven verification scale to check the long tail of hidden decisions that determine whether a published result is real?

TechTechPotato · Meta MTIA · custom AI silicon

The Next Generation of Facebook Is Here

On TechTechPotato, "The Next Generation of Facebook Is Here" has Dr. Ian Cutress walking through Meta's Hot Chips 2026 presentation on the next two generations of its in-house AI silicon, the MTIA 300 and MTIA 400. Cutress opens with a bit of show and tell: he owns physical samples of the first two MTIA generations, the small accelerator cards Meta has been deploying internally by the tens or hundreds of thousands, built with help from Broadcom and, in the first generation, around an Andes RISC-V core. The video then works through Meta's slides on why the company designs its own chips at all, and what the third and fourth generations change.

The core argument is that Meta's dominant workload is not chatbot token generation but recommendation: the ranking systems that decide which post, product, or video each user sees next. Those models lean on enormous embedding tables rather than next-token prediction, and Meta says its recommendation compute demand triples every year. On general-purpose GPUs, these memory-hungry workloads leave much of the silicon idle waiting on data; Cutress cites utilization figures in the industry ranging from roughly a tenth to two fifths of peak arithmetic. Because a small accuracy slip in ranking can cost billions over a year, and because owning the chip means owning the toolchain and the total cost of ownership, Meta designs silicon with a much higher ratio of memory bandwidth to compute than a merchant GPU would carry.

The MTIA 300, which Meta labels a 2025 chip and detailed in a paper earlier this year, is a dedicated training part. It pairs a 3-nanometer compute die with five 5-nanometer I/O chiplets and six HBM3 stacks for 216 gigabytes of memory, draws on the order of 700 to 800 watts depending on the numeric precision in use, and needs liquid cooling. A grid of processing elements and messaging elements handles decoupled collective communication, with about 1.2 terabytes per second of custom on-package remote direct memory access. Meta's reported results emphasize embedding operations: large speedups over GPUs on a 150-billion-parameter model, higher point-to-point and scale-up bandwidth, and a modest gain in performance per unit of total cost, around nine percent, alongside support for larger batches and full-precision communication where quantized arithmetic would lose accuracy the workload cannot spare.

The MTIA 400, due next year, carries a dual mandate: generative AI and recommendation together, covering both the deep learning ranking models and the token generation and training behind Meta's Llama family and its growing fleet of internal agent models. Its design shifts to chiplets for yield and flexibility. Each package combines two compute chiplets with an SoC die and I/O dies, all of the main logic on 3 nanometers, and eight HBM3E stacks delivering about 9.4 terabytes per second of peak bandwidth and 288 gigabytes of memory per package. Relative to the 300, Meta increased the processing element count by roughly a third while cutting memory by roughly a third, and claims about four times the compute density and around five times the 16-bit performance across the generations. The chips run wide and slow, at deliberately low clock frequencies, because these problems span many packages and efficiency beats latency. Systems scale from four chips in a tray to 72 in a rack.

Two architectural details get extended treatment. Each processing element in the 8-by-6 grid, which carries a redundant row purely to rescue yield, holds two RISC-V scalar and vector cores plus a command processor that orchestrates the arithmetic and the fabric interface. Notably, Meta declined to say whose RISC-V cores it licenses for these generations, a change from the openness around the first chip's Andes core, and the presenters also had no answer for Cutress when he asked how an outsider could get software for the chips he owns. The second detail is a number format Meta calls MX8s: where existing microscaling formats share one exponent across a vector of low-precision values, Meta shares a single exponent across a whole 16-by-16 matrix block of FP8 numbers, saving data movement whenever the data arrives in tiles of that shape. Cutress believes it is the first such format publicly announced. A host embedding cache rounds out the memory story: since even 288 gigabytes of HBM cannot hold embedding tables that run to terabytes, host memory backs the tables while dedicated hardware manages a cache of them inside the HBM, keeping the hottest data next to the compute.

The roadmap does not stop at the 400. Meta plans a 450 as an enhanced step and then a 500 series aimed more squarely at inference scaling, closer in spirit to the first two generations. Cutress closes by noting how unusual it is for a hyperscaler to present internal silicon this openly, since nothing obliges Meta to disclose any of it; his read is that the engineers are proud of the work and that the presentation doubles as recruiting. He invites anyone running these chips internally to tell him how they behave in practice and how hard they are to program.

Open questions

  1. Will Meta's matrix-wide shared-exponent format stay a private advantage, or does its usefulness for tiled training push it toward wider adoption the way earlier microscaling formats spread?
  2. As Meta splits its roadmap between dedicated training parts, dual-mandate parts, and a future inference-focused 500 series, how much of its fleet actually migrates off merchant GPUs, and where do NVIDIA and AMD keep the workloads Meta's own silicon does not fit?
  3. Why has Meta stopped naming its RISC-V core supplier after disclosing it for the first generation, and what does that silence signal about how the design's control points are shifting as the MTIA program matures?
