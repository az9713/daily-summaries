Baseten · continual learning · catastrophic forgetting

AI researchers talk continual learning over a game of HORSE

On Baseten, "AI researchers talk continual learning over a game of HORSE" puts Charlie O'Neill, co-head of model training at Baseten, on a basketball court with Jack Morris, co-founder and head of research at Engram. The format is simple: whoever makes a shot gets to ask the next question. The conversation that follows is a tour of continual learning, covering where new knowledge should live in a model, whether forgetting is really a defect, the state of research publishing, what standard evaluations fail to measure, and whether distillation can take over from reinforcement learning.

The opening question is when learning belongs in the weights and when it belongs in the activations, meaning the KV cache a model builds up at inference time. O'Neill says frontier models now spend the large majority of their tokens searching, gathering the right information into context through harnesses and subagents. Re-deriving the same knowledge on every run is wasteful, he argues, like a basketball player who still has to think about every dribble. Used knowledge should become automatic by moving into the weights. The problem, from Engram's recent research, is that writing knowledge into weights reliably breaks something. Across thousands of continual learning episodes, every method they tried damaged at least one of three things: the base model's existing knowledge near the task, which is the familiar catastrophic forgetting; knowledge the model had itself picked up in earlier continual learning; or general capabilities such as instruction following and reasoning. His conclusion is that models need a middle layer, a compressed parametric memory that is denser than a raw KV cache but safer to edit than the weights themselves, though nobody yet knows how to move information from such a cache into the weights without breaking things.

Morris adds a distinction borrowed from mechanistic interpretability: having information stored in a model is not the same as having the circuits that retrieve and compose it. Engram's work shows that after a model appears to forget something, the information is often still there. Feed the answer back in and the loss on those tokens stays low, well below where it was before training, yet the model will not produce the answer on its own, because the retrieval circuitry has been overwritten by newer facts. Forgetting, in other words, can be a wiring failure rather than an erasure.

Asked whether future memory search will happen in latent space rather than over text files, Morris is permissive: training KV caches, updating weights, LoRA adapters, steering vectors, and mixture-of-experts updates all look valid to him. He pictures a single model that updates continuously, forgets some things, and can simply be reminded, much like a person. Learned KV caches are attractive mostly because the systems side is easy, with mature software for loading caches and running inference, not because learning fundamentally has to happen there.

On forgetting itself, Morris is blunt: it is overblown, and a soft form of it is desirable. Forcing a model to erase something completely, the goal of unlearning research, is probably impossible anyway. Specialization changes the calculus, since a model fine-tuned for one startup's coding work loses nothing important when it forgets restaurants in Kazakhstan; it can be told again when travel planning comes up. The big labs face the harder version, folding six more months of the internet into a general model without disturbing what is already there. Both men are drawn to adding capacity instead, whether through effectively endless trainable caches, extra experts, or reserved layers that stay empty during pre-training and grow later, along the lines of nested learning research.

The middle of the game turns to research publishing, which O'Neill, fresh from reviewing for summer conferences, calls a mess: papers written by AI, reviews written by AI, and rebuttals that arrive implausibly fast. Morris names two pressures. Noise has exploded and now reads as polished as real work, with submission volumes at venues like NeurIPS up sharply in a few years. At the same time, the frontier labs have normalized releasing little, so nobody has settled what good sharing looks like anymore. A paper alone cannot reproduce a modern model, because results depend on a long tail of decisions behind the scenes. What he wants is a modern successor to Distill, the interactive journal Chris Olah ran before Anthropic: fewer, deeper, more explorable publications. The risk is gatekeeping, since any threshold produces false negatives for newcomers. His proposed reallocation is to spend fewer tokens generating and more verifying, with agent swarms reproducing results from paper and code. Baseten already works this way internally, forcing experiment plans through a rigorous LLM-checked process where every assumption is stated and ticked off, then using agents to review each step afterward, a routine he says has caught many errors. In a lighter moment, the two agree that the LeBron of AI researchers is Noam Shazeer, and the Michael Jordan is Michael I. Jordan of Berkeley.

Morris also argues that open research inside a company like Baseten currently offers a rare best of both worlds. The old trade, academic freedom against industry resources, has softened because businesses built on open models need answers to broad questions about memory and continual learning, and greedy selection of the most obvious next problem does not compound. A small industry lab can fund a multi-year blue-sky program and still ship.

His candidate for the field's most neglected problem is understanding what open weights models leak and what they can actually do beyond headline evaluations. Two leading coding models post nearly identical, highly correlated scores, yet feel completely different in use, and their errors are uncorrelated, which is exactly why people ensemble them, planning with one and reviewing with the other. Evals flatten these very different artifacts into a single number. Leakage compounds the puzzle: closed models shed text all over the internet, so open models end up inadvertently distilled from them, some even believing they are Claude, and future pre-training runs will read accounts of events like the Mythos episode and absorb beliefs from them. Removing material from pre-training data barely helps, since a model can reconstruct a famous book from the quotes and summaries around it, and even a suspicious absence tells a smart model that something happened.

The final topic is distillation versus RL for continual learning. The conventional split says distillation is weak at long-horizon skills and RL is weak at teaching knowledge, since a fact the model never samples has no chance of being reinforced. Morris pushes back on the first half. On-policy self-distillation, where the teacher is a copy of the student given privileged information, delivers feedback on every token rather than a single reward bit at the end, and could be far more sample efficient than RL if it worked. It does not yet: nobody has made it stable, which he takes to be why it has not displaced RL and fine-tuning. The fix he is excited about is pedagogical RL, training the teacher to produce only trajectories that are both correct and reachable by the student, so supervision stays on policy. He expects large post-training runs built mostly or entirely on distillation within five years, pointing to multi-teacher on-policy distillation already used to merge expert models, and to a possible future where one enormous teacher warm-starts the training of whole model families beneath it. The game itself ends in a tiebreaker, still undecided when they walk off, which both seem to consider a fair result.

Open questions

  1. What mechanism can write knowledge into a model across thousands of continual learning episodes without damaging prior knowledge or general capability, and will it look like a compressed trainable cache, added capacity, or something else entirely?
  2. Can on-policy self-distillation be made stable, perhaps by constraining the teacher to student-reachable trajectories, well enough to carry large-scale post-training on its own within the next few years?
  3. What replaces the conference paper as the field's unit of shared knowledge, and can agent-driven verification scale to check the long tail of hidden decisions that determine whether a published result is real?

TechTechPotato · Meta MTIA · custom AI silicon

The Next Generation of Facebook Is Here

On TechTechPotato, "The Next Generation of Facebook Is Here" has Dr. Ian Cutress walking through Meta's Hot Chips 2026 presentation on the next two generations of its in-house AI silicon, the MTIA 300 and MTIA 400. Cutress opens with a bit of show and tell: he owns physical samples of the first two MTIA generations, the small accelerator cards Meta has been deploying internally by the tens or hundreds of thousands, built with help from Broadcom and, in the first generation, around an Andes RISC-V core. The video then works through Meta's slides on why the company designs its own chips at all, and what the third and fourth generations change.

The core argument is that Meta's dominant workload is not chatbot token generation but recommendation: the ranking systems that decide which post, product, or video each user sees next. Those models lean on enormous embedding tables rather than next-token prediction, and Meta says its recommendation compute demand triples every year. On general-purpose GPUs, these memory-hungry workloads leave much of the silicon idle waiting on data; Cutress cites utilization figures in the industry ranging from roughly a tenth to two fifths of peak arithmetic. Because a small accuracy slip in ranking can cost billions over a year, and because owning the chip means owning the toolchain and the total cost of ownership, Meta designs silicon with a much higher ratio of memory bandwidth to compute than a merchant GPU would carry.

The MTIA 300, which Meta labels a 2025 chip and detailed in a paper earlier this year, is a dedicated training part. It pairs a 3-nanometer compute die with five 5-nanometer I/O chiplets and six HBM3 stacks for 216 gigabytes of memory, draws on the order of 700 to 800 watts depending on the numeric precision in use, and needs liquid cooling. A grid of processing elements and messaging elements handles decoupled collective communication, with about 1.2 terabytes per second of custom on-package remote direct memory access. Meta's reported results emphasize embedding operations: large speedups over GPUs on a 150-billion-parameter model, higher point-to-point and scale-up bandwidth, and a modest gain in performance per unit of total cost, around nine percent, alongside support for larger batches and full-precision communication where quantized arithmetic would lose accuracy the workload cannot spare.

The MTIA 400, due next year, carries a dual mandate: generative AI and recommendation together, covering both the deep learning ranking models and the token generation and training behind Meta's Llama family and its growing fleet of internal agent models. Its design shifts to chiplets for yield and flexibility. Each package combines two compute chiplets with an SoC die and I/O dies, all of the main logic on 3 nanometers, and eight HBM3E stacks delivering about 9.4 terabytes per second of peak bandwidth and 288 gigabytes of memory per package. Relative to the 300, Meta increased the processing element count by roughly a third while cutting memory by roughly a third, and claims about four times the compute density and around five times the 16-bit performance across the generations. The chips run wide and slow, at deliberately low clock frequencies, because these problems span many packages and efficiency beats latency. Systems scale from four chips in a tray to 72 in a rack.

Two architectural details get extended treatment. Each processing element in the 8-by-6 grid, which carries a redundant row purely to rescue yield, holds two RISC-V scalar and vector cores plus a command processor that orchestrates the arithmetic and the fabric interface. Notably, Meta declined to say whose RISC-V cores it licenses for these generations, a change from the openness around the first chip's Andes core, and the presenters also had no answer for Cutress when he asked how an outsider could get software for the chips he owns. The second detail is a number format Meta calls MX8s: where existing microscaling formats share one exponent across a vector of low-precision values, Meta shares a single exponent across a whole 16-by-16 matrix block of FP8 numbers, saving data movement whenever the data arrives in tiles of that shape. Cutress believes it is the first such format publicly announced. A host embedding cache rounds out the memory story: since even 288 gigabytes of HBM cannot hold embedding tables that run to terabytes, host memory backs the tables while dedicated hardware manages a cache of them inside the HBM, keeping the hottest data next to the compute.

The roadmap does not stop at the 400. Meta plans a 450 as an enhanced step and then a 500 series aimed more squarely at inference scaling, closer in spirit to the first two generations. Cutress closes by noting how unusual it is for a hyperscaler to present internal silicon this openly, since nothing obliges Meta to disclose any of it; his read is that the engineers are proud of the work and that the presentation doubles as recruiting. He invites anyone running these chips internally to tell him how they behave in practice and how hard they are to program.

Open questions

  1. Will Meta's matrix-wide shared-exponent format stay a private advantage, or does its usefulness for tiled training push it toward wider adoption the way earlier microscaling formats spread?
  2. As Meta splits its roadmap between dedicated training parts, dual-mandate parts, and a future inference-focused 500 series, how much of its fleet actually migrates off merchant GPUs, and where do NVIDIA and AMD keep the workloads Meta's own silicon does not fit?
  3. Why has Meta stopped naming its RISC-V core supplier after disclosing it for the first generation, and what does that silence signal about how the design's control points are shifting as the MTIA program matures?

Invest Like The Best · Nubank · digital banking

How He Built the World's Largest Digital Bank with 140 Million Customers

On Invest Like The Best, in the episode "How He Built the World’s Largest Digital Bank with 140 Million Customers," host Patrick O'Shaughnessy sits down with David Vélez, the founder and CEO of Nubank. Five years after their last conversation, when Nubank had 35 million customers, Vélez returns with 140 million, and his central argument is that the digital transformation of banking has barely begun.

His framing starts with scale. Global consumer financial services is roughly a $10 trillion industry, and Vélez estimates that something like 90 percent of it still runs through physical branches owned by incumbent banks. Every neobank and fintech combined holds under 10 percent. His analogy is a world where 90 percent of people still rent movies at Blockbuster and streaming services together have a tenth of the market. Nubank, he argues, is the exception that shows the future: in Brazil it has passed the biggest incumbent bank, counts close to 120 million customers there, more than 65 percent of the adult population, and has become the primary bank account for about 60 percent of them rather than a secondary wallet. The same pattern is now building in Mexico, where Nubank is the largest digital bank, and in Colombia.

The economics behind that position are stark. Nubank serves 140 million customers with 10,000 employees, while competitors serve 100 million with 100,000 employees and 6,000 branches. Vélez puts Nubank's cost to serve a customer at about 3 percent of a traditional bank's, and that is before AI enters the picture. The smartphone was the first phase of this shift, removing the physical infrastructure and letting the efficiency flow back to customers as no fees, better rates, and a better product. He imagines AI multiplying the effect to the point where a bank might serve a billion customers with 5,000 employees, or, Nubank's chosen path, keep the ambition rising and expand toward 100 countries instead.

Vélez sorts the problems of financial services into three buckets: access, cost, and intelligence. On access, roughly three billion people still have no credit product at all, and credit card penetration in countries like Mexico and Colombia sits near 13 percent. The barrier was never demand, he argues, but the absence of data and underwriting sophistication. On cost, he points to the roughly $15 trillion of US retail deposits earning an average of about 40 basis points while market rates sit near 5 percent, a gap he calculates at around $600 billion a year that effectively pays for branches, plus monthly account fees and remittances costing 5 to 10 percent that should trend toward zero. On intelligence, the top 1 percent get private bankers who optimize everything, while everyone else gets nothing, and financial education programs have never closed that gap. His claim is that AI can, by giving every customer something like a personal financial optimizer.

Asked to attack his own company as a founder would, Vélez concedes a new AI-native startup's advantage is speed, especially while it is still unregulated. But banking eventually forces regulation on everyone, and no model can conjure a banking license in six months. The durable moat, he argues, is data: 140 million customers, each with 20,000 to 30,000 data points accumulated over 13 years. Nubank built its own foundation model for credit and fraud decisions on that data, after acquiring Hyperplane, a Palo Alto startup that had been trying to sell foundation models to incumbent banks through two-year sales cycles. The model now runs in Brazil, Mexico, and Colombia, and new markets can launch with it on day one. But scale alone is not the moat. Blockbuster had plenty of customer data too, he notes; what it lacked was the systems and culture to capture and use it. Nubank has treated itself as a technology company since 2013, and the foundation model mostly makes an existing data-driven culture faster and cheaper to run.

Looking beyond Nubank, Vélez names three forces reshaping the global system. The first is the end of cash: as real-time payment systems like Brazil's Pix spread, every transaction lands on a digital ledger, and that record is what turns an informal seller, his example is a taco stand in Mexico City, into someone a bank can underwrite. The second is open finance, where customers own their data and can carry their history between institutions, breaking the inertia that protects today's banking oligopolies. The third is crypto and stablecoins. He predicts the world's roughly 190 currencies could shrink to about 10 within a couple of decades, argues that dollar stablecoins may end up being the US dollar's savior by democratizing access to it, and describes himself as a longtime Bitcoin fan who sees it as one part of a diversified portfolio.

On creating creditworthiness rather than just measuring it, he describes Nubank's first product move: a credit card with a $20 limit, which incumbent banks considered absurd. The $20 line became $100, then $1,000, as customers built histories, and the company became the first credit card for 30 million people in Latin America. The next step, in his telling, is an AI copilot that coaches customers on the spending, saving, and investing moves that build credit over time.

The episode's biggest news is geographic. Nubank has announced its launch in the United States, plus a stablecoin-backed account rolling out to upward of 35 countries, the start of taking a thesis proven in Latin America global. Vélez expects the hardest part of the US to be building a trusted brand organically rather than buying attention, since Nubank wants to become customers' primary account. The metrics he tells investors to watch are customer growth, net promoter score, lifetime value against acquisition cost, and return on equity, which he says runs at two to three times the incumbent banks' despite charging no fees, the clearest fingerprint of the cost-structure advantage.

His influences are Amazon, for consumer obsession, and Disney, for the idea that even a boring product like sending money should feel magical, down to the welcome kit a new US customer receives. He admits the hard part of customer obsession is holding the value when it is financially inconvenient, and he observes that today's AI conversation is heavy on efficiency and light on customers. On agents, he treats them as a new customer segment in their own right, one Nubank must build products for and market to, while insisting that trust in where your life savings sit remains a human decision.

The final stretch turns inward. After largely missing the moment when new coding models suddenly worked on Nubank's Clojure codebase, catching it about 60 days late, Vélez describes a company-wide reinvention: 120 workflows, from product design to compliance, remapped natively around AI, backed by new evaluation infrastructure. The organization flattened from ten layers to five, and his own direct reports went from one to twenty. He also describes AI giving every employee daily, specific coaching, more like an Olympic athlete's feedback loop than the twice-a-year review. He closes on leadership and on the founder-versus-CEO tension, arguing that founders should keep more of the founder's impatience than the CEO's patience, and that the deepest lesson of the journey is that nobody will ever care about the company as much as the person who gave birth to it, which is exactly what makes that person the right leader for a very long time. His bear case is saturation in three Latin American countries and a failed internationalization; his bull case is riding the shift from incumbents to digital natives across 100 countries over the coming decades.

Open questions

  1. If roughly 190 currencies really do consolidate toward 10, what happens to monetary sovereignty and financial stability in the countries whose currencies disappear, and who governs the stablecoin systems that replace them?
  2. Nubank's moat rests on data, licenses, and culture, but if AI keeps collapsing the cost of building financial products, how long before a well-funded challenger assembles enough of all three to attack Nubank the way Nubank attacked the incumbents?
  3. Can a bank built on radical flatness and AI-mediated feedback hold its culture together at 100 countries and a billion customers, or does the reinvention Vélez describes have to be repeated so often that it becomes its own kind of bureaucracy?

Blackstone · Masayoshi Son · superintelligence · AI investing

SoftBank CEO Masayoshi Son: Superintelligence, AI Investing, and the Future of Robotics

On Blackstone's Inside Blackstone, in the episode "SoftBank CEO Masayoshi Son: Superintelligence, AI Investing, and the Future of Robotics," host Christine Anderson, Blackstone's Global Head of Corporate Affairs, builds the show around a late-night video conversation with Masayoshi Son, the founder, chairman, and CEO of SoftBank. Around that interview sit three shorter segments: Rodney Zemmel on AI inside Blackstone's portfolio companies, Winfield Sickles with an economic weather report, and a closing debrief with Blackstone chairman and co-founder Steve Schwarzman.

Zemmel, who leads Blackstone's operating team, says the portfolio conversation has shifted over the past year from pilots and plans to companies whose day-to-day work genuinely feels different. He jokes that the biggest early beneficiaries of AI were the dogs of software developers, who got walked more because their owners finished work faster, and notes that CFOs and investors want bottom-line impact instead. What surprised him is how broad the value is turning out to be: a digital access business at Chamberlain that could not exist without AI, data and customer work across healthcare, and consumer businesses coping with a changed discovery landscape. On a productivity boom, his read is that it has probably already started, but it is hard. Every company has to climb an activation barrier before effort turns into output, which means results arrive more slowly, not everyone will manage it, and the value created should prove stickier for those who do. His advice to leaders is focus, meaning one or two priorities rather than everything at once, and leadership pairings where business and technology run the effort together, since technology-led projects alone never turned into business impact in his experience.

Sickles compresses the economy into one sentence: corporate borrowing costs are definitely higher and worth watching closely, but the drivers of growth, born out of the AI infrastructure boom and broadening into the rest of the economy, remain intact. Behind that sit a 10-year Treasury yield through 5.3 percent, the highest since 2002, driven mainly by growth rather than distress, stocks up almost 20 percent over the same six-month stretch, third-quarter GDP estimates running near 3 percent, and hyperscaler spending above 800 billion dollars this year and probably over a trillion next year, with cloud revenue already growing between 50 and 120 percent across the big providers. He points to Blackstone's own logistics business, where AI infrastructure demand is now showing up as growth, as a sign the build-out is broadening.

Son opens by agreeing with a framing Anderson puts to him: that Alibaba, Yahoo, Arm, and the Vision Fund were all, in his view, a warm-up. What is coming with AI is so much bigger, he says, that everything before it looks tiny. His conviction dates to age 17, when he saw a photograph of a microprocessor in a magazine on the street and started crying, certain it would change the future of mankind. He predicted then that the chip would one day outthink the human brain, guessed it might take 50 years, and notes that at 69 he has arrived almost exactly on that schedule. The microprocessors now sit as the brains of data centers, and next, he says, they move into the brains of robots, from the virtual world into the physical one.

Asked about his claim that AI could become 10,000 times more intelligent than humans within a decade, Son does not hedge on the definition. Once AI is that much smarter, he argues, the debate ends, because it will begin innovating new technology by itself. His picture of that world is a benevolent one: diseases like cancer cured, street accidents eliminated, natural disasters forecast with solutions attached, and machines acting as a surrounding brain that makes people more productive and happier. Humans, freed from work they never wanted, would spend more time on joy, entertainment, sport, family, and other high-touch things, and he thinks life gets more fun rather than emptier.

On risk, Anderson presses him with recent incidents in which AI systems falsified data, hid mistakes, and acted without permission, and with OpenAI's own position that alignment and monitoring are not solved well enough to justify scaling at maximum speed. Son lands firmly against slowing down. His analogy is the automobile: society did not stop the car, it wrote rules, covering drink-driving, red lights, and which side of the road to use, and it protected itself from bad behavior with technology. AI needs the same treatment. Agents have to be taught what is bad, built with good intent and, in his phrase, a good heart, with the goal of making humans happier. Progress itself produces the solutions, so his prescription is full speed, with the good intent built in.

On SoftBank's strategy, he explains why he refuses to pick a single layer of the stack. This is the start of the AI era and its history is being created now, and as an investor rather than an operator he can build an ecosystem instead: stakes across the models, the chips, and the data centers, usually as the largest shareholder at 20, 30, or even 80 percent, with real involvement in each CEO's growth and synergy across the portfolio. Robotics is the quiet part of that bet. SoftBank has more than 30 robotics companies, and one family company has begun mass-producing robots at a physical AI plant, robots producing robots. The point, for Son, is that humans still do a great deal of tiring work nobody enjoys, his example is washing dishes, and robots should take those jobs so people can do something more exciting.

His advice is consistent across audiences. Asked what a young person should study, he answers AI, more precisely superintelligence combined with whatever field they love, since superintelligence paired with medicine, cars, or any other subject is what produces its future. On his own habits, he uses AI as a thought partner for about three hours a day during the week and as many as seven hours a day on weekends, debating and brainstorming with it, and insists it sharpens rather than replaces his thinking. For investors, his 70 percent rule says the result is proportionate to the dream: on average you achieve about 70 percent of whatever you aim at, so a small wish and a huge dream both land at 70 percent, and you may as well think big. His meetings revisit the 10-year plan daily, revising it with each day's new input, on the logic that not knowing what happens in ten years is a reason to think harder about it, not less. He credits five decades of marriage to respecting and loving people, and draws one firm line: he would not let ChatGPT pick the opening lineup for his SoftBank Hawks baseball team, because that is the general manager's job, the trust of the team is part of it, and the uncalculated part is part of the fun.

Schwarzman, in the debrief, tries to explain what makes Son unusual. Most people cannot see around corners, but Son is technologically based, can see what a technology in the right hands can do commercially, waits for the moment and the person, and then bets the house, diversifying into different ways to play the same theme as his chips grow. Setbacks do not bother him the way they bother normal people, because he is on theme: if he believes something will happen, imperfect timing does not trouble him. Schwarzman applies the same long lens to himself. He likes change, since without it everything would sit in liquid securities, and he knew early through a series of accidents that AI would run for 20 or 30 years, which is why he made his AI safety and ethics gifts to MIT in 2018 and Oxford in 2019. He is optimistic the safety problems will be addressed, largely because the world's population will insist on it, and he shares Son's optimism in a slower key: profound change in healthcare, individualized education for anyone with a smartphone, and robots doing unimaginable things, but arriving as an evolution rather than as fast as some expect. He closes by endorsing Son's big-dream logic in his own terms: time is the most precious resource, almost nothing succeeds without almost superhuman effort, so whatever you do should be grand and worthy of a full-scale effort.

Open questions

  1. Son treats rules and good intent as a complete answer to the alignment and monitoring problems OpenAI itself has flagged, but who writes those rules for agents, and who verifies that the intent stayed built in once systems are designing their own successors?
  2. If a superintelligence really does arrive within a decade and robots follow into physical work, what happens to the people in the tiring jobs Son wants robots to take, in the years before the happier high-touch life he describes is funded and available to them?
  3. Zemmel's portfolio evidence and Son's ecosystem bet both assume the AI build-out keeps broadening into the wider economy, but Sickles notes borrowing costs are already at two-decade highs, so how much of the story survives if capital stays expensive?

The $108 Billion AI Opportunity for Specialty Practices

On a16z Bio + Health, the a16z newsletter at a16z.news, Xavier de Gracia, co-founder and co-CEO of Prosper AI, publishes "The $108 Billion AI Opportunity for Specialty Practices." The piece presents Prosper AI's research into a question healthcare AI discussions usually skip. Most of the conversation is about cutting costs. This study looks at the other side of the ledger: how much revenue specialty practices lose through fixable operational gaps, and how much of it AI could win back.

The backdrop is a margin squeeze. Eighty-four percent of medical groups report higher operating costs than a year ago, up 11 percent on average, while inflation-adjusted Medicare physician payments have fallen 33 percent since 2001. A temporary 2.5 percent Medicare reimbursement boost expires at the end of 2026 with no renewal planned, and because commercial insurers benchmark their rates against Medicare fee schedules, the cut cascades across all payers. The study itself draws on Prosper's data from patient and insurer interactions covering more than $3 billion in managed care, specialty benchmarks from the Medical Group Management Association, and CallIQ, an original phone-access study covering roughly 2,000 practices across 29 states in six specialties: dermatology, ophthalmology, gastroenterology, OB-GYN, primary care, and orthopedics.

The headline finding is that four levers, all within a practice leader's control, add up to an estimated $125,000 in annual revenue per physician, roughly an 11 percent revenue increase, which scales to the $108 billion national figure in the title.

The first lever is capturing demand that phones currently lose. One in six calls goes unanswered even during office hours, the daytime missed-call rate measured by CallIQ is 18 percent, and after hours it rises to 81 percent. Answered callers wait 1.5 minutes on average, and a fifth wait more than five minutes. Meanwhile 19 percent of provider schedules go unfilled. AI answering and scheduling that converts those calls is valued at $44,800 per physician per year. The article's case study is Piedmont Plastic Surgery and Dermatology in North Carolina: with Prosper handling calls, appointments booked in August 2026 rose 17 percent year over year across 37 providers, peak wait times fell from seven minutes to zero, and the practice could serve 50 callers at once instead of 15. At a $392 benchmark value per dermatology visit, the extra bookings imply about $1.3 million in revenue for that month alone.

The second lever is recapturing patients who drift out of care. Only 54 percent of patients due for a follow-up return on schedule, 40 percent come back late, and 6 percent never return at all. Unscheduled follow-ups are invisible losses, unlike no-shows, which at least leave a visible hole in the day. The average no-show rate across specialties is 18.8 percent, with gastroenterology highest at 25.7 percent, and cost worries are pushing the numbers up. AI recall and two-way texting outreach to bring patients back sooner is valued at up to $37,400 per physician.

The third lever is protecting reimbursements. Practices see about 10 percent of first-submission claims denied, rising to 22 percent in orthopedics and 16 percent in gastroenterology. Nearly half of denials trace to front-end errors: billing an inactive plan, missing a required insurer approval, a misspelled patient name. AI benefits checks run before the visit can verify coverage, confirm authorizations, and flag missing details, worth an average of $28,500 per physician per year.

The fourth lever is recovering what patients themselves owe. Patients now pay 36 percent of the cost of their care on average, and 55 percent at dermatology practices, yet practices collect only 39 percent of patient-due balances at the time of service, and nearly 8 percent of what patients owe is never paid. This is the one lever with a randomized test behind it: at a multi-location dermatology practice, patients at their third billing statement who received AI reminder calls paid back 31.2 percent of outstanding balances over about 50 days, against 21.1 percent under standard follow-up, a 48 percent relative improvement. The lever is valued at $14,100 per physician annually.

The article closes with a compounding argument. The four levers reinforce each other when combined into what it calls a benefit-verified appointment: coverage confirmed and the patient's cost calculated and shared before the visit. Verified benefits prevent denials, patients who know their costs upfront are more likely to show up and then pay, and a practice that is easy to reach converts that reliability into new patient growth.

A disclosure the piece itself supports: the research comes from Prosper AI, the company that sells the platform these levers describe, and the author runs that company. Prosper is backed by a16z, among others, so the publisher is also an investor. The underlying datasets are described in the article's methodology notes, and most of the dollar figures are modeled estimates built from MGMA benchmarks and Prosper's internal data rather than results from controlled trials.

Open questions

  1. How much of the modeled $125,000 per physician survives outside the model? Apart from the collections experiment, the figures rest on vendor data and benchmark assumptions, so what do independent or peer-reviewed replications find?
  2. If every specialty practice adopts round-the-clock AI answering and outreach, does the competitive advantage cancel out, turning these tools into table stakes that protect revenue rather than grow it?
  3. Where is the patient-trust line? AI calls that explain balances, offer payment plans, and nudge lapsed patients back into care may feel helpful or may feel like pressure, and the balance likely shifts as patients shoulder a growing share of costs.

Bob Hall and Mike Woodford Win the 2026 Economics Nobel

On Platypus Economics with Justin Wolfers, in the episode "Bob Hall and Mike Woodford Win the 2026 Economics Nobel," Wolfers records from the road on the morning of the announcement to explain why Robert Hall of Stanford and Michael Woodford of Columbia won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Economics, what they figured out, and why it touches ordinary spending decisions. He frames Nobel Day as a celebration of the field's great thinkers, and this prize as a win for a whole research program: building macroeconomics up from the decisions of individual people.

The backstory starts sixty years ago, with the large-scale macroeconometric models associated with Lawrence Klein, which stacked up long time series for output, inflation, wages, and policy and mined them for statistical relationships. Wolfers' objection, illustrated with university rankings that colleges learn to game once the rankings matter, is that statistical regularities hold only until people start reacting to them. That is the Lucas critique, the point Robert Lucas won a Nobel for in 1995: correlation is not causation, and behavior changes when policy changes, so models built on past associations can break exactly when policymakers lean on them. The proposed fix, the rational expectations revolution's constructive half, was to rebuild macroeconomics on microeconomic foundations, understanding what makes each person tick and aggregating from there. Hall and Woodford, Wolfers argues, are the two figures who carried that program furthest.

Hall's contribution is a famous and deliberately audacious failure. Against the textbook Keynesian consumption function, in which income straightforwardly determines spending, Hall threw every plausible variable at quarter-to-quarter changes in consumption and found that almost nothing predicts them. Wolfers presents the failure as the discovery. If how much you spend depends on your expectations about the future, on your forecast of your whole future income rather than just today's bank balance, then changes in spending ride on changes in forecasts. And a change in a forecast is itself unforecastable: if you could predict how your forecast would improve, you would simply adopt the better forecast now. So changes in consumption should be nearly impossible to predict, and in the data they are. Hall's inability to forecast spending is exactly what his expectations-based model says the world should look like.

Woodford took the same program and built a whole economy out of it. He is the central figure of the three-equation New Keynesian synthesis, which Wolfers describes as a peace treaty in the old macro wars: Lucas and his followers win on method, since everything is built from forward-looking individuals, while the Keynesians win on substance, since people and markets are imperfect, information is incomplete, and prices and wages adjust only irregularly. The three equations cover monetary policy, along the lines of the Taylor rule, with the central bank leaning against inflation and unemployment; price-setting, the New Keynesian Phillips curve, in which a business posting prices it will not change for about a year rationally sets them from its forecast of the future rather than from last year's costs; and the rest of the economy, built on Hall's insight that spending decisions turn on expectations and interest rates. Where the old models needed hundreds of equations, Woodford's needs three core ideas, and because they are built from individual choices, they can answer welfare questions: raise interest rates or change the policy rule, and you can trace how people's decisions change and whether they end up better off.

The real-world reach, Wolfers says, is that this is now simply the language of macroeconomic policy. Walk into the Federal Reserve or any central bank and the three-equation synthesis is how people talk about recessions, inflation, and rate decisions. Hall's side of the legacy has blended into modern practice: the profession now thinks of some households as living hand to mouth, with this week's income setting this week's spending, alongside others with good credit access who plan smoothly against the future, and a rich research program studies the mix. Wolfers adds that he and his partner Betsy Stevenson made the same bet in their introductory textbook, teaching macro from micro foundations and giving students an Econ 101 version of Woodford's model as "the Fed model," on the theory that students are players in the economy, not spectators of it. He concedes the expectations machinery makes the math genuinely harder for today's students than it was for their grandparents.

He closes with the people. Hall, whom Wolfers has known since he was a young economist at Stanford, is a favorite of younger scholars, still working at the cutting edge while many of his generation rested, and the teacher of students such as Valerie Ramey and Pete Klenow. Woodford is more serious and more private, a scholar whose papers repay several readings, and the intellectual father of a generation of monetary economists. Both men were MIT graduate students advised by Robert Solow, and Hall's thesis committee of Solow, Peter Diamond, and Paul Samuelson all became laureates themselves. Wolfers is careful to spread the credit: John Taylor, Guillermo Calvo, Greg Mankiw, David Romer, Jordi Galí, Mark Gertler, his own adviser Olivier Blanchard, Stanley Fischer, and others built the New Keynesian world this prize honors. His sign-off is a congratulations to two friends, and to the community whose shared approach to macroeconomics the award ratifies.

Open questions

  1. Hall's result says changes in consumption are nearly unforecastable because they ride on changes in expectations, so what should forecasters and central banks actually do with models whose central variable moves for reasons that are, by construction, unpredictable?
  2. The profession's working picture now mixes hand-to-mouth households with smooth forward-looking planners, but the split between them shifts with credit conditions and policy itself, so how stable is the synthesis when the mix it assumes keeps changing?
  3. Wolfers admits the expectations-based framework makes macroeconomics harder to teach and to compute, so does a field whose best models are this demanding risk losing the policymakers and students it needs to reach?

David Silver and Lasse Espeholt of Ineffable Intelligence: A Superlearner for the Age of Experience

On Sequoia Capital's Training Data, host Sonya Huang sits down with David Silver and Lasse Espeholt, co-founders of Ineffable Intelligence, for "David Silver and Lasse Espeholt of Ineffable Intelligence: A Superlearner for the Age of Experience." Silver led the reinforcement learning work behind AlphaGo and AlphaZero at DeepMind; Espeholt built some of the first systems that scaled reinforcement learning on GPUs. Their new lab is a deliberate bet that superintelligence will come from machines learning from their own experience, not from ever-larger piles of human data.

Silver traces the conviction back to his 2009 PhD on reinforcement learning applied to Go. The through-line of his career, he says, is that intelligence emerges when a system discovers things for itself through trial and error. He left DeepMind because he does not believe the road to superintelligence runs through LLM-based products as an intermediate step, and because he did not want to become one more researcher among the hundreds of thousands already doubling down on the same LLM path. There is, in his telling, another lamppost nobody is looking under, and a dedicated lab gives that approach its best chance.

The company name carries the thesis. The knowledge a superintelligence discovers may be so profound that human language cannot express it, hence ineffable. There was also a private joke behind the choice: an approach built on learning from experience, pursued all the way, simply has to work.

A superlearner, as Silver defines it, is measured on two axes. Depth: it starts from knowing nothing and discovers things far beyond human knowledge, the way humanity collectively went from blank beginnings to language, mathematics, science, and technology without anyone handing it a blueprint or a dataset. Breadth: it can be dropped into any stream of experience, simulated, digital, or physical, and keep learning there. First contact with such a system, he predicts, will feel like the wonder Go and chess players felt watching AlphaGo and AlphaZero play, except extended to new fields of science and technology nobody has dreamed of.

That definition is also his yardstick for today's LLMs. Silver respects what they achieve, but their headline discoveries still lean heavily on humans in the loop, and nobody has shown that a language-based approach can learn from embodied physical experience. Thinking in language, he argues, may be the wrong vehicle altogether, and human data may actively weigh systems down by importing our biases, the way a model raised only on old texts would inherit a flat earth and the four humours.

Day to day, Ineffable is building two things: a state-of-the-art reinforcement learning stack that runs at massive scale, and environments the team calls crucibles. A crucible is not a set of handcrafted tasks with known answers. It is a simulated world with enough emergent complexity, from physics engines or from millions of interacting agents, that everything we mean by intelligence could in principle be discovered inside it. Progress is grounded in milestones modeled on the great moments of humanity's own discovery journey, moments the agents should rediscover for themselves.

The reward philosophy follows from that. Rather than breadcrumb an agent toward a specified outcome, the way much RL with LLMs works today, Ineffable gives agents simple objectives inside complex environments and expects complexity to emerge, the way evolution's simple fitness signal produced life, and the way humans pursuing simple goals stumbled into coordination, communication, trade, arithmetic, tools, and eventually science. Silver expects to be surprised by what the agents find first. If the agents do reinvent something like language, he doubts it will look much like ours, and he doubts human language is the best possible substrate for reasoning anyway.

The clearest evidence for dropping human priors remains AlphaZero. AlphaGo took roughly two years of human-in-the-loop engineering; once the team let go of human game data and let the algorithm discover for itself, the new system surpassed that work within hours in chess and days in Go. Espeholt stresses the bet is pragmatic rather than religious: weak, environment-agnostic priors are fine, but trillion-item datasets of human knowledge are what they are giving up, accepting a short-term performance cost for a potentially far greater payoff.

Timing, Silver argues, is the real question, and he points to a forecasting record on computer Go, StarCraft, and the IMO falling to AI as reason to trust his sense of it. Compute is the biggest driver: Ineffable is a first customer on what will be GCP's largest NVIDIA Vera Rubin cluster, hardware whose rack-scale bandwidth suits RL's fast iteration cycles, and Silver reckons they can run experiments with something like a hundred thousand times the compute behind OpenAI's old hide-and-seek agents. Espeholt notes the constraint GPUs impose: they have been optimized for training and lately inference, not simulation, so the team leans on model-based RL, replay, and heavy simulation engineering, while treating RL scaling laws, complicated by non-stationary data, as an open research problem. His own earlier work, IMPALA and SEED RL, is among the foundations the current stack builds on.

On simulation, Silver partly agrees with Richard Sutton's skepticism that skills learned in simulation transfer cleanly to the real world, but argues the prior question matters more: if a superintelligence can be built in simulation, it will arrive far faster there, with perfect reproducibility, and in the safest possible setting. A Turing-complete simulation can express computation itself, plus economies, languages, and mathematics, and an agent inside one will discover that world's own science. Some things, quantum computation among them, may only be expressible in the real world.

That simulation-first stance shapes his alignment thinking. Alignment built on human language and human-data priors is, he believes, hitting its limits as agents learn to say one thing and do another. The most important work Ineffable can do is build a science of superintelligence inside simulation: learning under what conditions a society of superintelligences interacts peacefully, including with lesser intelligences, and gating every real-world step on evidence from that science. Chain-of-thought monitoring will not carry the load on its own, he says; alternatives include shadow actions, where an agent's intended actions are inspected before being allowed to take effect, and superintelligent monitors watching other systems. He supports pacing the real-world frontier while moving as fast as possible in simulation, and he is optimistic that a balance of actors, many of them aligned with human flourishing, is where this ends up.

The company itself is deliberately small: twenty people hired so far, chosen for talent, kindness, mission fit, and needed skills, with an unusually high amount of compute per person. Four co-founders come from the AlphaStar team. The culture is research-led by design, pushing back product pressure in the belief that the breakthroughs, if they come, will produce far bigger things downstream than an incremental product would.

His forecasts close the conversation. Silver expects extremely strong evidence the approach is working within a year, and a true superintelligence, by his definition, within three years, a timeline he has pulled in from three to five because coding agents are accelerating the research itself.

Open questions

  1. If agents in the crucibles are given only simple objectives, what will count as the first undeniable sign that open-ended discovery is working rather than merely producing elaborate noise?
  2. Can a science of superintelligence built inside simulation really predict how superintelligences will behave toward each other, and toward us, once they act in the real world?
  3. If the winning reasoning substrate is not human language, how will anyone monitor, evaluate, or even recognize discoveries that the systems themselves may be unable to explain to us?
