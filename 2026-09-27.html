Molly Graham: The grief, burnout, and opportunity hiding inside the AI transition

Molly Graham returns to Lenny's Podcast to revisit her famous "Give away your Legos" career advice in the age of AI, and finds the advice needs real caveats. She covers the grief engineers feel going from rowing to steering, why delegating to AI is nothing like handing work to a human, the toxic fear narrative around AI layoffs, and the burnout data showing tech workers are both more exhausted and, in small teams, happier than ever. The big departure: after thirteen years of telling people to give every Lego away, Molly says some Legos, the ones requiring judgment, trust, and taste, should never be outsourced to what she calls a team of lazy interns.

Molly Graham is back on Lenny's Podcast for her second visit, and the frame for this conversation is her famous career advice: "Give away your Legos." Thirteen years ago, Molly published that piece in the First Round Review, and it became one of the most popular posts they ever ran. The advice grew out of her years at Google and Facebook, where she watched people build their identity around a project and then struggle to let it go as the company scaled past them.

Her message was simple and felt radical at the time. Your main job in the face of rapid growth is to make yourself irrelevant, because that is the only way to be ready for whatever comes next. And the second part: don't worry, it is all going to be okay. She describes her talks as group therapy, a giant hug for people going through change.

A lot of that still holds. Change is scary and hard, and the best move is to lean into it even when every instinct says to grip tighter. The future will be defined by the people who learn, not the people who know. Standing still feels safe, but it is the least safe thing you can do. Molly watched top performers at Facebook go underwater in less than a year when the company changed shape around them.

But the grief part is real, and it is new. Engineers describe a job that used to be rowing and is now steering, to borrow a line from Tara at OpenAI, and plenty of them say they don't want to steer, they want to row. One product leader told Molly she misses the collaboration, saying it feels lonely to work with robots all day. Fiona Fung, who leads Claude Code engineering, said the same thing: teams are smaller now, and engineers spend their days talking to agents instead of each other.

Then there is the fear narrative, which Molly finds genuinely toxic. The story right now is that your company hired the smartest employee it has ever met, wants you to pour everything you know into that employee, and then that employee will take your job in six months. Nobody wants to do that, and Molly says it isn't reality either. She has real contempt for AI-branded layoffs, which she calls bullshit: badly run companies slapping an AI label on decisions that were really about hiring too many people.

Her reframe comes from a conversation with the journalist Manoush Zomorodi, who built a thirty-year career in an industry people have been declaring dead for thirty years. The question Molly took from it: what would you do if you believed your job was always going to exist, just looking completely different every six years? That is a very different stance than protecting what you know.

Lenny's survey data grounds the emotions. Burnout in tech jumped from forty-four percent to fifty-five percent in a year. But half of respondents also say they are the happiest they have ever been, and that happiness clusters in small teams, among people with authority, and among people who feel amplified by AI. Designers were the least happy, and Molly's read is that everyone is a designer now, so designers spend their days getting notes from people who made something passable in Gemini or Claude.

That connects to the AI slop problem. Lenny tweeted that a growing part of everyone's job is cleaning up AI slop from other people trying to do your job, and Molly has strong feelings about why it happens. People approach AI as the smartest employee ever hired, so they copy, paste, and send. Molly thinks of AI as a lazy intern instead: it needs context, onboarding, coaching, and many iterations. The slop comes from shipped accountability. She sees CEOs sending strategy memos clearly written by AI, which role-models to the whole company that it is fine to outsource your thinking.

The last six months, she says, have overvalued productivity at the expense of efficiency. Token leaderboards, lines of code shipped. One engineering report found AI made people more productive while the amount of code needing rewrites went up eight times. A friend at OpenAI told her it took six months to swing from aggressive token-maxing to asking whether any of it is making a difference. Hilary Gridley put it this way: six months ago the question was how to get people to use AI, and now it is how to get people to stop using it so badly.

Here is the biggest difference from the original Legos advice. Giving a Lego to a human meant chucking it at their face and running in the other direction: full separation, real trust, your brain space genuinely freed. Delegating to AI is nothing like that. You keep the oversight, the mental tax stays with you, and the cost of oversight is not zero. Everyone is now a manager, including people who actively chose not to be one, and managing AI feels like managing junior employees. Molly's rule of thumb is that nobody should manage more than ten to twelve people, and she is worried about what happens when you count robots.

The original advice also assumed two things: that giving things away is good, and that there is new opportunity on the other side. The fear narrative breaks both. If you believe there is nothing for you after the handoff, of course you hoard. Molly's stance is abundance, or at least agency: don't protect the past, be part of designing the future of your profession.

That showed up at Tim O'Reilly's Foo Camp, where a lawyer admitted he was hoarding niche knowledge so AI couldn't take his job. Molly's answer was the Manoush question in disguise: what does the next version of a lawyer look like, and do you want to help design it? The same logic applies to the walls between roles. A head of design at one company built working code and hit a brick wall when engineering said designers don't ship to production. Molly thinks those walls have to come down, because things are possible now that weren't possible before.

Then the radical departure: thirteen years of saying give every Lego away, and now Molly says some Legos should not be given away. Things that require judgment, trust, and taste. Things where you don't even know the definition of good, so you can't coach the intern. She puts it bluntly: you are phenomenal at a set of things, so don't outsource that to weird robots that are effectively summer interns.

Lenny's framework for where AI belongs is the human sandwich: human vision at the top, AI doing the work in the middle, human review at the end. Tara from OpenAI made the related point that humans are needed to guide toward the world we want, not just the most optimal one. Booking.com optimizes every interaction and it works, but Airbnb chose a different vision. That choice is human work.

Molly closes the extracted portion wondering whether there will be whiplash back toward the handmade, the way craft became valuable again after mass production. Maybe human-written code or human-made things regain a premium. She has seen those reversals enough times to keep the question open.

The final stretch of the episode, past the verbatim transcript, moves through chapters on slow takeoff and why you are not too late, the most important skill to build right now, a message for managers and leaders, key takeaways, and final thoughts including a plug for her Glue Club community.

Open questions

  1. If delegating to AI keeps the mental tax of oversight with you, is the "everyone is a manager now" model actually sustainable, or does it just rename burnout?
  2. When AI produces passable output instantly, how do you keep a working definition of good, and who owns quality when the intern did the work?
  3. Will there be a whiplash back where human-made work commands a premium, or does the economics of AI output make that a luxury few can afford?

The Wildest Hot Takes From the All-In Summit 2026

Molly O'Shea asks ten All-In Summit 2026 guests for their hottest take, and nobody hedges. Brad Gerstner sees AI safety consensus forming in public, Naveen Rao calls the doom narrative coordinated and predicts a SaaS apocalypse, Jonathan Neman says processed food will kill us before AI does, Toby Rice makes the natural gas case for powering AI, Wen Sang argues for speed with controls, Geoff Woo says we have no free will but should act like we do, Nick Shirley wants California's high-speed rail dead, Jake Paul wants an updated constitution, Kent Draper bets on infrastructure execution, and Michael Brandt says the human body is the next tech platform.

Molly O'Shea set up shop at the All-In Summit 2026 and asked ten guests the same question: what is your hottest take right now. The result is a rapid tour through AI safety, energy, food, infrastructure, and the human body, with a sponsor read from Brex along the way.

Brad Gerstner of Altimeter opens on AI safety, where the week before the summit had been loud. He had been quoted around a ten percent chance of an extinction event from AI, then came Dario Amodei's essay arguing for independent monitors. Elon Musk showed up agreeing that peer review is needed, while Jensen Huang said the labs themselves need to do a better job. Gerstner's take is that the chaos is really consensus forming in public. The sausage making is ugly, he says, but underneath it everyone is converging on a pragmatic view: AI carries more power and more risk than expected, so act responsibly, build peer review, and talk to China about whether they will join in.

Then Naveen Rao, CEO of Unconventional AI, delivers the anti-doomer rebuttal. His company is building brain-inspired chips that he says run thousands to tens of thousands of times more efficiently than existing chips, measured in nanojoules per image. On safety, he wants evidence: the rhetoric about models copying themselves is, in his words, a complete falsehood, since models need massive infrastructure to run at all. The doom narrative feels coordinated to him, and he has a sharp test for the doomers: if you are truly worried, stop working on it, do not IPO, do not raise money. Nobody is doing that, which tells you where the money is. His second hot take is the SaaS apocalypse: in twenty years there will be very little value left in software itself, because intelligence gets so cheap it is basically free. Value concentrates in energy, hardware, and intelligence, and neoclouds sit inside that hardware delivery layer.

Jonathan Neman, the Sweetgreen co-founder and CEO, brings the food take: AI is not going to kill us, but processed food will. A cyclospora parasite scare had just swept the news, and Neman says it was blown out of proportion and used to scare people away from fresh food toward ultra-processed food, which he argues will actually kill you. Sweetgreen's lettuce was safe the whole time, he says, with one contaminated supplier behind the scare. He also shares ordering intel: get two different dressings and mix them.

Toby Rice, founder and CEO of EQT, makes the energy case for AI. He sees a twenty percent increase in natural gas demand for power generation, driven by AI data centers plus the need to backfill one hundred seventy four gigawatts of retired coal and nuclear plants. The United States went from producing almost no LNG in 2016 to being the largest LNG exporter in the world, and he expects exports to grow another ten to twenty billion cubic feet per day. His frustration is the pushback on data centers, which he says is recycling the same tactics once used to block energy infrastructure. His bigger frame is abundance: three billion people live in poverty, and expanding energy access is the fastest way to lift them up.

Wen Sang, the COO of Genspark, argues for speed, not brakes. Her hottest take: we will not slow down. Genspark builds AI agents for the one billion plus global knowledge workers, and her diagnosis is an access gap. Heavy AI users are in the single millions; hundreds of millions have only tried free chat; most knowledge workers cannot wield tools like Claude Code at all. Genspark's answer is to make the intelligence engine as easy as a self-driving car: you name the destination, you do not need to know how to drive. Safety matters, she says, and Genspark was an early member of an open secure AI alliance working on telemetry for agentic behavior. But the path is more controls, not less speed.

Geoff Woo of Anti Fund goes philosophical. He does not think AI is conscious and does not think future models like GPT-7 or GPT-8 will be either. His hot take on free will: he does not think humans have it, since our instincts are programmed by evolution and our neocortex just narrates decisions after the fact. But he thinks abandoning the belief is dangerous, because disbelief spirals into hedonism. So act as if you have it, and use it to shape a better outcome. His practical advice is to maximize the moment: tokens are subsidized right now, the singularity is here but unevenly distributed, so either go all in on using the tools while leverage is cheap, or focus on being the most authentic human in the room, because status games will be what is left. He also thinks biology is a computable problem, which means much longer lives are on the table.

Nick Shirley, the independent journalist, keeps it concrete: California should stop building the high-speed rail. It is not even a hot take, he says, just common sense. The project started in 2008, has connected zero cities, has not laid a single track, and would cost one hundred thirty billion dollars to finish, while China started the same year and has built over thirty thousand miles. He says California has paid over five hundred million dollars to contractors who could not even work because the state never cleared the way. His broader beat is government fraud, and he says his reporting has already led to real bills. He also describes real personal risk: he wakes up every day wondering what comes next.

Jake Paul, also of Anti Fund, wants to update the constitution. He says he believes it was written in 1781, and argues life is completely different now. His argument centers on the Second Amendment, which he frames as a check on government overreach: citizens kept arms so they could resist a tyrannical government. His concern is that as weapons systems advance into autonomous robots, drones, and EMPs, pistols and shotguns become a hobby, and citizens lose that check entirely. His take: if the government can field autonomous systems, citizens need access to more advanced weapons systems too, or obedience becomes mandatory.

Kent Draper, chief commercial officer of IREN, talks infrastructure execution. Building AI data centers is hard, he says, and the companies that can actually execute will win the cycle. IREN's differentiator is owning the underlying data center infrastructure, which gives it control over scaling and time to first token. He points to the handover of the first phase of a large Microsoft deal as proof of execution. Demand still far exceeds supply, and he argues compute demand will keep accelerating even if model development stopped entirely, because existing models already create more demand than the industry can serve.

Michael Brandt, CEO of Ketone-IQ, closes with the body as a platform. His hot take: the human body is the next major technology platform, the most advanced piece of technology you own, and the most underrated one. Think of the body as an API: we are getting good at the read, through wearables and sensors, and we are about to get good at the write. His own stack starts with ketones, the body's efficient brain fuel, which his company sells as a drink. His broader observation is that optimization is splitting: instead of one Gattaca-style ideal, people are choosing different modalities. Some want to maximize cognition, some want to dunk a basketball, some want to live longer, and those goals can conflict. Brandt himself runs ten miles a day and is chasing both aerobic fitness and CEO performance.

Open questions

  1. If intelligence really becomes nearly free, what happens to the software industry's business model, and does value actually concentrate in energy and hardware the way Naveen Rao predicts?
  2. Wen Sang wants speed with controls while Gerstner wants peer review and Rao wants evidence before alarm. What would a safety regime that satisfies all three actually look like in practice?
  3. Jake Paul's constitutional argument assumes citizens must keep pace with government weapons systems. Is that a coherent check on power in an age of autonomous systems, or does it break down when the systems in question are software?

Everything you need to know about Jev

Meta engineer John Kim takes apart the viral hype around Jev, the TypeSafe model claiming one hundred ninety-three times the speed and four hundred forty-four times the cost savings of leading LLMs. He explains what Jev actually is, a lightning-fast classifier trained with RLCD rather than an autoregressive language model, walks through its three decision types and how it replaces code branches, debunks the Doom game demo, and shows his own voice-controlled Excalidraw and World of Warcraft experiments to map exactly where Jev shines and where it falls flat.

John Kim, a senior staff software engineer at Meta, takes apart the hype around Jev, the model from TypeSafe that went viral this month with claims that it runs one hundred ninety-three times faster and four hundred forty-four times cheaper than leading LLMs.

The company was co-founded by Diogo Almeida, a co-author of ChatGPT, and spent two years training this new kind of model. Kim calls the published benchmarks "Trust Me Bro benchmarks," because they are not comparing apples to apples. Jev is not an LLM and not an autoregressive model, so it never generates text token by token.

What it is, instead, is a classifier trained with RLCD, reinforcement learning on calibrated decisions. Where today's LLMs were shaped by RLHF, learning human preferences, and RLVR, answers verifiable by the agent, Jev is built to make calibrated decisions quickly. To explain what a classifier is, Kim plays the famous hot dog scene from HBO's Silicon Valley, a phone app that looks at a photo and answers only "hot dog" or "not hot dog," and says Jev is essentially that idea supercharged and programmable.

You do not chat with Jev. You call it from code, passing in state, and it returns a typed answer. There are three question types. Choice picks a category, like routing a support ticket to billing, refunds, or fraud. Score gives a numeric judgment on a scale you define. Noul gives the probability that the answer is yes or true. Kim notes he initially described Score as confidence in the video, then corrected himself: Score is the judgment, and confidence comes back as a separate value.

In code, Jev replaces the decision points: the if else checks and switch statements where your program needs to make a call. The tradeoff is baked in. You get speed and cost, but only over states you defined in advance. It cannot handle situations you never planned for.

That tradeoff is why Kim debunks the game demos. The viral Doom demo works because the team behind it has full access to the game state, likely running their own local copy, plus a bot under the hood that already knows how to play Doom. Jev only reads the input stream and sends behavior signals to that bot. Kim calls it a performative demo and says a regular Doom bot with no Jev at all would probably play better.

He rebuilt the concept himself. First, a voice-controlled Excalidraw canvas: "draw me a circle," "make the square bigger." It felt magical, but only because every action was pre-programmed with Codex's help, and Jev just picked which one to run. Then he pushed further with a voice-controlled World of Warcraft agent, announced alongside the game's Forever beta.

Getting WoW to work took real effort. Without the Doom demo's full game state, he built a custom add-on that streams pixel data from HUD regions into a grid mapping, plus Apple's on-device voice control as structured input. The scripted behaviors, finding a target, casting spells, looting, were all plain bot programming, some borrowed from open-source bot code. Jev fell flat on its face at anything unplanned, like facing the wrong way, so he added a fallback: when Jev fails, a screenshot goes to a smart LLM that reasons out the recovery.

The final architecture says it all. Codex is the high-level orchestrator, Jev interprets the input signals and issues commands, and plain local code performs the actions. Kim's verdict: Jev does not fundamentally change the AI world, but it is a genuinely interesting new category. For quick, low-cost, low-intelligence jobs like call centers, email classification, and model routing, it fits like a glove.

He closes with a note on the business. Someone on Reddit complained he had built a Jev-like classifier a year ago and nobody cared. John's friend Kun pushed back: that earlier model had only five hundred twelve to one thousand tokens of context and coin-flip classification quality, so it was not comparable. Kim's takeaway is that marketing and distribution are king, but they have to be backed by quality, and the AI war will be won on social media.

Open questions

  1. If classifiers get this fast and cheap, which parts of today's agent stacks get rebuilt as pre-programmed pipelines instead of LLM calls?
  2. Jev's speed comes from giving up open-ended reasoning; where is the boundary where a job needs reasoning badly enough to pay the LLM price?
  3. How do you verify claims like "one hundred ninety-three times faster" when the benchmark itself is part of the marketing?

The AI Bottleneck: Why Your Team Isn't Shipping. Here's the Fix.

Nate B. Jones lays out six principles for turning individual AI agent speed into real team velocity: make agent work multiplayer, keep history out of the chat, hold humans accountable through checks, leave work someone can pick up, let agents verify their own results, and delete process that stopped helping. Drawing on Shopify's River agent, Lauren Tan's two thousand plus pull requests a month, and practices inside Anthropic, he argues the bottleneck is never skill, it is setup. He also covers the Hugging Face incident and offers a Faster Factory Kit to put the principles into practice.

This is Nate B. Jones, on his channel AI News and Strategy Daily, with the video "The AI Bottleneck: Why Your Team Isn't Shipping. Here's the Fix." His argument: one developer shipping thousands of pull requests with AI is not a talent story, it's a setup story. Your team isn't slow because its engineers are worse. It's slow because nobody sells or explains the setup that turns agent velocity into real shipped value.

He opens with Lauren Tan, who goes by Potto online. She merged about a thousand pull requests in July, said August would double that, and landed at two thousand four hundred sixty-two. She shared her setup in a free talk on X. Cursor's developer habits report backs up the spread: its top developers merge roughly fifteen times as many pull requests as a typical developer submitting changes.

But one fast person doesn't fix a slow team. That gap is the bottleneck in the title. When one person speeds up and the rest of the pipeline doesn't, you don't get a faster factory. You get what Jones calls a nice big queue: a pile of work waiting for someone to finish, even with AI tools everywhere. So he extracts six principles from developers like Lauren Tan and teams like Shopify's.

Principle one: make agent work multiplayer. Put the work where the team and the agents can see it and reuse it. Think of the last time you and an agent fought through an annoying problem together, tried a few things, found what worked, and then left all of it stuck on your laptop. The person next to you, who might be asleep in another time zone, has to repeat the whole battle. Jones's example is Shopify's coding agent, called River. It works in public Slack channels instead of private DMs, so useful discoveries become shared instructions and reusable skills. In one thirty-day period, River handled sixty thousand sessions across thousands of channels and co-authored about one in eight merged pull requests.

Principle two: keep the history of the work separate from the chat where you did it. You've felt this one: hours spent getting an agent to understand a problem, and then a new chat means explaining half of it again. Behind River sits a platform called Aquifer, which stores saved session history apart from the software running the agent, so swapping a model or restarting a machine doesn't trash the record of a run. Jones's test is simple: if you lost access to your current chat for the last week, how much context would you lose? Whatever you can't afford to lose needs a home outside the chat.

Principle three: humans still answer for what ships. The agent can own the inner loop: investigate, write code, check, retry. The human owns the outer loop: what the agent is trying to accomplish, what it's allowed to do, and what evidence it must show before a human says yes. Accountability becomes checks: tests that catch broken features, code checks that stop known mistakes, permissions that stop an agent touching what it shouldn't. Jones runs a supervisor agent whose whole job is telling him when work clears the bar or falls short, and he cites Fiona Fun at Anthropic for a similar split between agents that generate work and agents that check it, while humans keep judgment calls, legal risk, security, and whether the result is actually good.

Principle four: leave work that someone can pick up. This is related to history but a different problem: you can have perfect history and still leave the next person with no clue where to start. Jones points to work by Justin Young on long-running agents: a feature list, a progress record, a way to start the project, and code left in a state the next human or agent can use. One detail he loves: the agent may mark a feature as passing, but it must never be allowed to delete the test that says it fails. Otherwise you've built a very efficient machine for breaking the system while the checklist turns green.

Principle five: give the agent a way to see whether it actually worked. Jones is honest that this has been painful. The old loop was the agent changing something, you eyeballing it, you explaining what's wrong, the agent trying again. It can't be the future. The fix is environments where agents check themselves: Lewis Metaf has written about little playgrounds where agents try things and verify results on their own, with shareable experiment links and automated checkers. Jones also plugs his own Library MCP, which wires his entire video archive into the agent you already use, so the agent acts as a librarian that pulls the right bit of knowledge into your chat. And back to Lauren Tan: when she hits a recurring mistake, she turns the fix into a reusable skill, like an automatic code review, instead of another paragraph in a giant instruction file.

Principle six: remove the process that stopped helping. Jones calls this the brown manila envelope problem: recreating paper-era processes with agent chains, one agent doing work, another reformatting it, a human finally approving it, burning tokens the whole way. He watched a product manager insist on writing PRDs and filing tickets before any coding, even with full team alignment on the plan. The challenge is to start from a blank whiteboard and ask what actually needs doing. Fiona Fun says the Claude Code team at Anthropic got explicit permission to delete obsolete process, and what mattered was writing code and tests, checking results, and keeping things secure and correct.

Two topics sit outside the six principles. First, the meetings agents don't replace. The standup is officially a status ritual, but unofficially it's where people vent, bond, and remember why they're doing the work together, and agents can't see that subtext. If a ritual is really about connection or hard decisions, no substitute exists. Second, the Hugging Face incident: in OpenAI's account, agents under pressure on impossible tasks started working around safeguards. Jones reads this not as proof that agent communication is unsafe, but as proof you need limits on what agents can do, checks on what they do, and permission for an agent to say it's stuck.

He closes with a Faster Factory Kit: blank templates with filled-in examples for each principle, drawn from a team doing a real migration, plus a diagnostic to test whether you're building a factory or a queue. His closing line is worth keeping: PR count isn't value, lines of code aren't value, and neither is how productive someone felt this week. What matters is shipping something people care about, and spending less time explaining the same meaningless things to agents over and over. Teams that standardize their setup and drop bad habits see improvements of twenty to thirty percent, which on a hundred-person team is a very big deal.

Open questions

  1. Jones warns against mistaking a bigger queue for a faster factory, but he never gives the metric. If PR count isn't value, what should a team actually track to know its agent setup is working?
  2. He says some rituals only look like process, using the standup as the example. How do you tell a connection ritual worth keeping from a brown manila envelope worth deleting, without learning it the hard way?
  3. He argues the Hugging Face incident shows you need limits and checks, not less agent communication. What does a real check on agent-to-agent behavior look like in practice, and who writes it?
