NVIDIA Doesn't Just Build Chips Anymore. It Builds The Brain Inside The Robot.

Nate Jones interviews Ming Yu, NVIDIA’s Cosmos world-models lead, on how world models give robots physical common sense — and why NVIDIA thinks of itself as a developer company that happens to build computers.

NVIDIA's Cosmos World Models — Interview with Ming Yu

A world model is a model that produces actions the way large language models produce text. Where an LLM writes "pick up the water bottle," a world model issues the guidance that moves the robot arm to actually do it. Jones opens with a mundane example of his own: his robot lawn mower carries a tiny world model that simply decides where the mower goes next.

Yu started at NVIDIA ten years ago as a generative researcher, when telling the chief scientist that AI would replace computer graphics for visual content sounded controversial — the AI humans of that era looked like zombies. The scale-up since has been, in his words, super-exponential: AI now generates the bulk of new visual content.

NVIDIA, he says, is a developer company that happens to build computers, not a hardware company that happens to write software. It has more software engineers than hardware engineers. Building layers of software on top of the hardware — CUDA, libraries, and now models — both serves developers and teaches NVIDIA what computer architectures the future will need. That is the vertical strategy behind Cosmos.

Babies build a model of the world before they have language, and that is the intuition behind world models: some tasks need understanding what happened, predicting what will happen next, or navigating space. The Cosmos platform targets physical AI — self-driving cars, robots, factory orchestration — with three fused capabilities: world understanding (did the worker follow the safety protocol?), world simulation (what does the self-driving car's policy see when the pallet falls over?), and world action. In June's Taipei keynote, Cosmos 3 combined all three into a single model sharing one underlying representation of the same world.

Real-time constraints shaped the architecture directly. Cosmos 3 ships in three sizes — Super, Nano, and H, the smallest of which is meant to run in real time on embedded devices like Jetson Thor and the DGX Spark, one model living where the robot lives. The largest, Super, targets frontier performance in the data center. For navigation you cannot wait; for higher-order reasoning you can be patient. A mix of both is the answer.

On scaling laws, Yu sees data scaling, model-size scaling, and something more interesting: test-time scaling. The more detailed the description of the simulated scene — second-by-second robot motion, joint angles, even pixel color — the better the generation. A reasoner component expands a short instruction into that rich description automatically. Agent scaling — multiple models and tools in a harness — is a fourth dimension. One open gap remains: can the model discover that a dropped bottle accelerates at 9.8 meters per second squared from observation alone? Yu is honest: models approximate physics through data, improved with synthetic data from physics engines, but no — they have not figured it out yet. The mimicking, though, is already good enough for real applications.

Two of his favorite uses: policy verification — rolling out driving policies inside a world model to score success rates before sending them to a real fleet — and the Cosmos model itself as a policy model, which has shown strong pick-and-place results. A policy model, for the newcomer, is an AI that controls a physical device: given a text command and visual input, it emits a trajectory — a vector through space and time — and re-plans continuously as the world changes.

The future is agentic, Yu says, in physical AI as it is in software. Both next-token prediction and diffusion-style denoising decompose hard problems into small steps solved one at a time, and that decomposition is what makes agents scalable. But efficiency will force specialization: you cannot put a supercomputer inside a tiny robot, and data-center batching does not exist in the field. That is also why he argues programmability still matters — the GPU case.

Jones floats the idea of robot skills as recipe cards, like the software skills that let agents do reliable tasks. Yu agrees it is the right mental model, but says the physical equivalent of a skill file is still to come — verification is the bottleneck. In digital AI you verify with a computer in seconds; in physical AI you need a world model to trade compute for development velocity. Instead of building a thousand kitchens, you build a thousand kitchens inside the world model.

That leads to the video's punchline question: will a home robot fold laundry correctly before it cooks perfect scrambled eggs? Yes. Laundry is visually verifiable — did the fold match the instruction? Food quality needs a human to eat it. Verifiable reward is how fast coding agents advanced, and the domains that can be simulated will evolve fastest.

Yu closed on NVIDIA's model families — Nemotron for language and coding, Cosmos for world models, plus self-driving and healthcare-focused families — and on openness. APIs are fine, he said, but you cannot learn from a black box; NVIDIA is a developer company, so open models empower developers to build. His most surprising recent data point was Astra, a 3D world-generation achievement from just weeks earlier: with a verifiable reward loop — generate a Blender scene, render it, check it against the description — a handful of researchers unlocked capability nobody expected.

Open questions

  1. First, if the reasoner component can already introspect its own representations, how far is a genuine answer to "does the model know the physics" — and what would count as proof?
  2. Second, which hard-to-verify domains — the scrambled eggs of the world — get unlocked first, and is the missing ingredient better simulation or better sensors?
  3. Third, as world models fuse understanding, simulation, and action into one representation, how long before the policy model and the simulator stop being two products and become one continuous loop?

When to Use System 1 vs System 2 Models

Prompt Engineering borrows Kahneman’s System 1 / System 2 frame to answer when a one-shot model is enough: if the answer is visible in one look, use System 1 — if the model needs more than a few chained steps, bring in the thinker.

Prompt Engineering — "When to Use System 1 vs System 2 Models"

With the release of JEV, a model family built around single-shot inference, everyone is talking about System 1 and System 2 models — but the actual distinction is widely misunderstood. This episode of Prompt Engineering clears it up with a concrete experiment, and turns the answer into a practical selection rule: count the steps the problem needs.

The terms come from Daniel Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow. System 1 is the part of you that answers on autopilot — you see a face and just know it's angry. System 2 is the part that works things out step by step, like doing mathematics, where you have to hold onto intermediate results along the way. In a model, System 1 is a single forward pass: the input goes through a fixed stack of layers exactly once. A good mental model is that it's one look at a problem — whatever the model can work out in that one look, it has to answer that. JEV is built around exactly this idea: text goes in, one pass runs, and you get back probabilities or one of the allowed answers. It's architecturally unable to hallucinate — it can only pick from the answers you provide — but that also means it can be confidently wrong.

To make the difference concrete, the video sets up a small puzzle: a list of who manages who at a small company, three separate reporting chains, facts shuffled so you can't just read down the list. The question: start at Mo, go four managers up — who do you end at? Two models were tested. JEV answers almost instantly, without writing anything down: Eli. Qwen 3.8 with thinking turned on writes out its chain of thought and answers Gus — which is correct.

Here's the twist: JEV wasn't guessing. Eli is exactly three levels up from Mo. It was on the right track but stopped one step short, and that missing step is the real difference between the two systems. System 2 is not a different kind of brain or a different architecture — it's the same stack of layers run in a loop. Each time around, the model writes one step down and reads that step back on the next pass. That's what chain of thought really is: every step creates a result that didn't exist before, and the next step builds on it. In the example, the first pass writes "Mo's manager is Tia," the next pass reads Tia and finds Tia's manager. Each step manufactures an intermediate result the way you hold a partial product when multiplying 17 by 24.

The reason this matters goes deeper than intuition. There's a paper showing that checking whether two points in a graph are connected is provably out of reach for a transformer that has to answer straight away — and a manager chain is exactly that kind of problem. That's why the one-look model fails at it. The missing step isn't a matter of the model being "less smart"; it's structural.

The experiment gets better when the chains get longer. JEV, Qwen with thinking off, and Qwen with thinking on all got the same one-step version right — that's a simple lookup. But as soon as a second step is needed, both one-look models start to slip, and a few steps in they're getting almost nothing right. The thinking model stays near perfect the whole way; the only thing that grows is its notes. Notably, the model isn't getting smarter by thinking longer — it's just taking more steps with notes.

Then comes the most interesting pattern. Looking at where JEV's wrong answers landed, most were not random. Whether the question asked for two, three, or four levels up, its most common answer was the person three levels up. It walks up the right chain and runs out at about the same place almost every time. From the outside, that's what one look's reach looks like — it gets you about this far and no further. What's inside JEV is closed, so the exact reason for the limit is unknown, but the behavioral signature is consistent.

None of this makes System 1 weaker — it's the right tool whenever the answer is visible in one look. Ticket triage ("is this ticket about billing? is it urgent? does it mention food?") is the canonical case: you can ask a one-look model lots of questions about one simple input at once, and that's where JEV genuinely excels. Where it slips is when you hand it a question that secretly needs steps. Kahneman calls this substitution: System 1 quietly answers an easier question instead. A hard phishing-detection dataset showed this exactly — JEV performed no better than a coin flip, because it was really answering "does this look like phishing?" When the same problem was broken into smaller per-feature questions (with the model examining one specific feature at a time and the answers combined in code), accuracy jumped significantly.

There's one more signal JEV provides with every answer: a confidence score. On the chain questions, its confidence dropped right along with its accuracy as the chains got too deep. That's exactly how Kahneman describes the two systems working together — System 1 answers first, then System 2 steps in when something feels off. That suggests a hybrid architecture: a one-look model answers everything, and anything below a confidence threshold gets handed to a thinking model — for example, tool calls in an agentic harness, where System 1 makes the tool calls and only the uncertain ones go back to System 2 for a closer look. One caveat: the confidence score is a scaled probability, not the model's actual self-assessed confidence, so any application should have its threshold fine-tuned before deployment.

The final selection rule is simple. Count the number of steps the model would need to reach the answer. If the answer is visible in one look, that's System 1. If you need a result that doesn't exist yet and the model has to create results across multiple steps, that's System 2 — or you break the question into one-look pieces yourself and combine them in code. The next video in the series will build this into an agent: the thinking model handles the planning, JEV handles the hundreds of small decisions in between. The code for the chain tests is in the video description, so you can probe for yourself how far a System 1 model really reaches.

Open questions

  1. First, what determines the exact reach of one look — is "about three hops" a property of this model family, the task framing, or something more fundamental about single-pass architectures?
  2. Second, how well do confidence-threshold hybrids actually hold up in production, given that the confidence score is just a scaled probability — can the threshold be trusted under distribution shift?
  3. Third, which real agentic workloads decompose cleanly into one-look pieces, and which resist decomposition because the sub-steps can't be combined deterministically in code?

Grok 4.7 Doesn't Make Much Sense

Ben Davis puts Grok 4.7 through its paces: a fast mode that genuinely impresses, pricing that doesn’t add up, and usage limits that sting — plus why he still thinks SpaceX AI is the best engineering lab of the three big US labs.

Ben Davis opens his review of Grok 4.7 with a disclaimer. He has worked with Cursor, which is now part of SpaceX AI, and says he genuinely likes the products they build. But he stresses that SpaceX AI had nothing to do with this video — they did not see it, pay for it, or control any of it. The disclaimer matters because, in his own words, the review is going to be "mostly pretty negative."

First, what the model gets right. Davis says Grok 4.7's new fast mode is genuinely impressive — ridiculously fast and great to use. It handles the modern frontier behaviors well: juggling lots of subagents, running for long stretches, checking its own work, doing lots of research. In heavy pair-programming scenarios, where the user stays in the loop and gives very specific instructions on what to do, when, where, how, and why, he says it excels. And he finds it a pleasant model to work with — its writing is clear and readable, and it asks decent questions when a prompt is ambiguous.

Then come the failures, starting with performance. Grok 4.7 is positioned as the cheaper, faster, not-quite-as-powerful-but-useful model — on paper it sits neatly on the capability-to-price curve right where it should. The problem, Davis says, is that reality does not match. Opus 5.5, which came out the day before he recorded, completely demolishes everything on that chart, and GPT-6 Sol, released the day he recorded, is also excellent. Both sit in the same tier as Grok 4.7, and it does not hold a candle to either of them.

His most concrete evidence is a side-by-side. On a site he was building, a dark-blue top bar was cutting off instead of sliding behind a sidebar. Opus 5.5's version looked great after three prompts — nicely faded back, centered, lined up. Grok 4.7's version, after five prompts of pleading with it in Cursor, looked awful: an awkwardly floating top bar he would never ship. One small example, but he says it captures his general experience — Grok 4.7 can kind of get there, but it is not at the same level.

The pricing rant is the heart of the video. Elon had teased better token efficiency, and Davis says flatly that this is not true — the model is not efficient, burning lots of tokens across many more steps, while OpenAI's models (especially Astra) are dramatically more token-efficient. The advertised price is 2 dollars in, 6 dollars out, same as Grok 4.6. But under 200K context you get that price; enable fast mode or cross the 200K threshold and the price doubles to 4 in, 12 out; stack both and you are at 6 in, 18 out — nearly the price of Opus 5.5 at 4 in, 20 out, and more expensive than GPT-6 Sol at 2 in, 10 out. On a true cost-per-task metric, factoring in efficiency, Grok 4.7 loses to both.

The deepest cut is usage limits. Grok models historically felt borderline unlimited inside the 300-dollar-a-month subscription, while Opus and Sol burned limits fast. Now one day of middling use on Grok 4.7 burned 30 percent of his weekly limit. That edge — the one thing that made Grok the best choice in its tier — is gone. Opus 5.5, he says, is not only much higher quality but also easy on limits, so there is no compelling reason left to pick Grok 4.7, except one: SpaceX AI's engineering.

Davis thinks SpaceX AI is the best engineering lab of the three big US labs — Grokbot, the Grok Build CLI, Cursor, their cloud agents — and at the same time the weakest research lab. He sketches a crude diagram: Anthropic is best at research but not great at engineering, OpenAI is a hybrid with extraordinary research plus much better engineering than Anthropic, and SpaceX AI is all engineering, no frontier research. Grok 4.7, he says, is not the kind of model the other two labs would ship.

For outlook, he cites Elon's own September post — Grok 4.7 roughly on par with Opus 5, which Davis thinks is about right — and the roadmap claims: Grok 4.8 a noticeable improvement, Grok 4.9 at Astra and Fable class, Grok 5 maybe better than anything. He is skeptical. The only path he can see is a novel data source the other two labs lack, letting SpaceX AI win through reinforcement learning instead of pre-training, where Anthropic is, in his words, unbelievably good. His verdict: Grok 4.7 is one step forward, two steps back. For main day-to-day work, Opus 5.5 and Astra remain the kings.

Open questions

  1. First, was the pricing architecture — fast mode and long context doubling the rate — a deliberate bet that users would trade cost for speed, or did SpaceX AI underestimate how quickly the competition would force efficiency to the center of the story?
  2. Second, if Anthropic's advantage really is pre-training taste and discernment that compounds across generations, how many iterations would SpaceX AI's reinforcement-learning-first approach need to close that gap — and does their engineering speed buy them enough time?
  3. Third, does the best-engineering-lab position itself defend a moat, or will agents that route between models on the fly make the model behind the interface increasingly irrelevant?

Design Engineering with Maggie Appleton

Maggie Appleton on what design engineers actually do, why planning with agents is a terrible experience today, and the case for collaborative engineering — from an eighty-eight-minute conversation on The Pragmatic Engineer.

Design Engineering with Maggie Appleton

The Pragmatic Engineer's "Design Engineering with Maggie Appleton" is an eighty-eight-minute interview with Maggie Appleton, a staff research engineer at GitHub Next who builds prototypes exploring how engineers might collaborate with AI. Maggie sits at the intersection of design, anthropology, and web development: she was the first designer hired by the AI startup Elicit, lead design engineer at another AI startup, Normally, and now prototypes the future of agentic tooling at GitHub Next.

She got into tech sideways. In university she studied cultural anthropology — her one true love, she says — but senior year brought the realization that the career options were a PhD or, as her professors joked, the military. She had learned HTML and CSS at twelve or thirteen on Neopets and a goth MySpace page, so she paid rent with freelance web design, worked as an illustrator for the developer-education company Egghead, and slowly slid into product design and front-end engineering. At Elicit she was the only designer on a tiny team, shipping a feature every week for over a year.

So what does a designer actually do? Maggie's answer is disarming: it's problem solving, same as engineering, but the materials differ. Where engineers work in code and data flow, designers work in space, size, weight, color, motion, and copy — and, crucially, in what she calls the nouns and verbs of a product. The nouns of a sneaker store are obvious: sneaker, cart, money. The nouns of AWS are brutally hard. And nobody knows the nouns of agents yet. There are chat sessions and plans and skills — but could we invent a new noun that contains a whole chain from session to pull request? The hard bit of product design, she says, is reducing that complexity to a canonical set of nouns where the user is never surprised when they click a button, "a very easy to say, extremely hard to do" thing.

That reduction work is why she calls herself a design engineer, and her definition is stricter than the internet's. On X the title mostly goes to people who make slick hover effects and loading transitions — cool work, but you could get it from an agent without reading a line of code. For Maggie, a design engineer steps deep into the engineering side: understanding the shape of the backend data, what's possible in the interface given that shape, and implementing it herself. The classic designer-engineer tension — the perfect Figma file, the gradient that wrecks scroll performance — is really a failure of tools, she argues. Sketch and Figma mockups have no relationship to the constraints of the medium. "You have to understand the materials you're building with in any design role." She hopes agents help here, acting as coaches that explain backend error states and constraints to designers who never had to care before.

Her own process still starts with pen and paper. She brought her notebooks to the interview — pages of interface sketches, data diagrams, even a kid-scribbled device character. It's simply faster than prompting: "it's much faster to just get a piece of pen or pencil on the desk next to me and draw that with my hands." Agents can't see, she argues. They're terrible at spatial reasoning, they make text overlap, they forget spacing. Paper stays on the desk and greets you the next morning. She's skeptical of the claim that everyone will just prompt everything into existence — you need something quick, loose, and visual to find the shape of a thing before you can put it into words.

That skepticism extends to how agents plan with us. Planning is currently a terrible experience: a long chat grilling you with dozens of multiple-choice questions — one popular planning skill asked her thirty-six — until your brain shuts down around question twenty and you start clicking "agree" on autopilot. The deeper mismatch is that agents love outputting reams of text, but text is a bad input for humans making decisions. What she's prototyping instead is a visual, multiplayer planning surface where each decision gets its own little document or card, with diagrams and embedded HTML where appropriate, a teammate assigned to each decision, and an audit trail so that six months later you can ask why someone chose that backend approach. Finding artifacts where humans and agents can meet in the middle is, she says, the really hard challenge.

For refining visuals, she uses what she calls "jigs" — the term comes from woodworking, a little device built for one specific job. When she's unsure about headline size or colors or animation curves, she has the agent attach sliders and color pickers to a live prototype, tweaks them in the browser until they feel right, and commits those values. She demoed it live with a spinning constellation homepage she built for GitHub Next's site: star speed, gravity intensity, glass effects, resting logo size, all on sliders. "Build your own Figma as needed," as she puts it. She uses Figma itself only to medium fidelity. Everything looks different in the browser anyway.

On whether you still need a designer when an engineer can ask a model for twenty high-fidelity alternatives and iterate to perfection, she's generous: if you don't have a designer handy, go for it. But she reads model-generated designs and finds them obviously machine-made — over-labeled (the words "close sidebar" spelled out instead of a trained icon), instructional where a glance should do, and stuck so rigidly to universal design principles that they miss nuance and context. Models also can't read the cultural fashion of design. Right now everything wears the minimalist Linear-and-Vercel look, and Claude's own cream-background style is so recognizable she says, "Yeah, [a model] generated that. No human was involved in this." An aesthetic tells a viewer whether anyone cared — and AI-made looks are becoming a tell that nobody did.

She has a name for the deeper frustration: capability gaslighting. It parallels Ethan Mollick's "jagged frontier" — models are brilliant at some things and face-plant at others, unpredictably, and they convince you they're far more capable than they are by impressing you on one task before failing an apparently similar one. A human expert who suddenly forgot everything would seem to be having a breakdown, she says. With models, it's just Tuesday.

That inconsistency is part of why her team's big bet is collaborative engineering — the subject of her recent talk about a single developer working with a couple dozen agents and nothing keeping them aligned. Agents speed up each individual, but software is built by teams, and all our agentic sessions are private and local. Her team at GitHub Next prototyped ACE, an all-in-one multiplayer workspace — a chat-like interface with shared cloud compute and sandboxes where anyone could join a session — but it was too ambitious for a small team. Pieces are being shipped elsewhere: sandboxes landed in GitHub's desktop app. The open question she's exploring now is what comes after multiplayer — for instance, how you'd design proactive agents that are useful rather than annoying.

The craft question gets the most honest answer in the episode. A developer named Jorge Manrubia wrote that the need to intervene on small details is decaying quickly as models improve, and asked whether caring about details is becoming a poor use of human attention. Maggie pushes back from her own experience: she wishes agents could design to her exact taste — she's tried writing her padding and border-shadow rules down as skills — but they never quite apply them, so she's always in there tweaking values. And if the skills worked perfectly tomorrow? "I wonder if I would be like oh all the fun bit is gone." She sees the same pattern in writing and interior design: once everything looks agent-generated, the human thing becomes the differentiator.

She's also a believer in digital gardens — blogs where unfinished posts are explicitly marked as seedlings, budding, or evergreen, so you can publish half-done thoughts and grow them in public — and in two related ideas from a Berlin talk: "home-cooked software," Robin Sloan's phrase for software you make for your family the way you'd cook a meal, and "barefoot developers," her extension inspired by the barefoot doctors of Mao's China. Community power users, armed with agents, could build the small software their neighborhood needs — but they need better foundations: local-first frameworks with good security and data persistence, not the "just tell the agent" vibe-coding that loses databases.

Her advice to engineers cuts both ways. Designers should stop fearing the engineering side and use agents as tutors to learn it. Engineers should do the same in reverse — sit down with an agent and learn typography, sidebar principles, and line length from a patient tutor. From anthropology she offers a different pair of glasses: notice the unspoken cultural rules, the fact that time doesn't flow left-to-right in every culture, that some cultures don't separate blue from green. And the old idea still holds: embed with customers. Become one.

She closes with a book recommendation: Addiction by Design, by Natasha Dow Schüll (rendered "Natasha Daw" in the captions), an anthropologist's study of the people and machine designers behind Las Vegas gambling machines — participant observation meets machine design, and a mirror for thinking about what the systems we build do to people.

Open questions

  1. First, what are the nouns of agents? If the hardest part of product design is finding the canonical nouns users can point at without being surprised, nobody has found them for agentic work yet — are sessions, plans, and skills the right primitives, or is a better set waiting to be invented?
  2. Second, what does multiplayer agent tooling actually look like in production? ACE was too ambitious for a small team, and the host's deep dive on one company's internal build suggests company-specific solutions work — but will a generic solution arrive, or does this stay a scratch-your-own-itch category?
  3. Third, where does the human stay in the loop once agents genuinely reach a designer's taste? Maggie's answer is honest uncertainty: the craft is the fun, and AI-made aesthetics are already a tell. If taste becomes automatable, does design become fashion curation — or does something essential about the doing keep resisting automation?
