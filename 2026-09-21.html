The AI Boom’s Biggest Winners May Not Last

A panel from The Next Endeavor 2026 on who funds the AI boom and whether the winners will last — a Reuters moderator, a UBS chief investment officer, a Qualcomm Ventures director, and a seed-stage founder debate capital, compute, and where the value accrues.

Imagination in Action's "The AI Boom's Biggest Winners May Not Last" is a panel from the Next Endeavor 2026 event — and it is really about money, not technology. The moderator, Crystal, a Reuters reporter who runs its tech practice, frames the session around what really powers the AI race: capital. Her guests span the scale: a chief investment officer from UBS; Albert Wang, a senior investment director at Qualcomm Ventures, the chip giant's $2 billion fund; and Dave, founder of seed-stage Link Ventures, who backs university founders in Boston. Dave's opening joke sets the tone: he shares a stage with someone managing trillions; his own funds, about a billion.

The private investors' opening theme is speed. Wang says Qualcomm Ventures has invested for 25 years and never seen a wave like this: companies that raised every 12 to 18 months now come back every four to six. He watched a company go from seed to $100 million in annual revenue in six months — real revenue, unlike the dot-com era's $2 million on $30 million of burn. Dave says the old SaaS rule of "triple-triple-double-double" used to span years; now companies triple every six months, and his latest fund holds four 100x winners with not a single loss — unheard of in venture.

The UBS investor keeps asking the title's question: how durable are these revenue streams? AI is super-exponential and extraordinarily disruptive, she says, but the panel underappreciates how disruptive it will be to today's winners too. Dave answers with one of the session's best analogies: Google and AltaVista were nearly identical; AltaVista is gone. The microprocessor may be the greatest invention in human history, and it was nearly commoditized — while the era's money went to the search layer on top. Building the most useful thing does not decide who keeps the value.

The panel then builds a theory of where the moat sits right now. Wang argues the classic moat — capturing the customer when supply outstrips demand — has inverted: today demand outstrips supply, so whoever has access to supply has the moat. Dave makes it physical: two-thirds of all AI compute runs through TSMC, one company outside the US. Buyers offer two or three times the price and get turned away: "you are begging people to take your money." His firm likes things directly above and below the transformer layer — accelerators like Etched or Groq, data-center investments, retraining data layers — while distrusting the crowded agent-orchestration field.

The panel also bridges to the week's biggest AI story: the UBS investor calls OpenAI's Navier–Stokes proof another watershed, and Dave supplies the inside story — ten thousand agents, 88 hours, OpenAI redirecting compute from the other Millennium problems to leapfrog Anthropic. His punchline is about capital, not math: finishing first or second was a function of compute, "and that's probably not a lasting competitive moat." She sees the same in the $20 trillion-plus of public-market gains since ChatGPT — most of it in the enabling layer. UBS's three structural themes say where the puck goes: AI as a disruptor, power and resources for the energy demand, and AI applied into industries — pharma and biotech most exciting, where AI could lower drug costs and surface new molecules.

On commoditization, the public investor worries perhaps $5 trillion of private-market AI value could prove fragile if general frontier models keep improving and purpose-built apps become mere features. Dave counters that China already answered: open-source models trained for a tenth to a hundredth of the cost destroyed the comfortable story that three or four labs will control all of AI. He sides with Musk's 100x-gain claim for task-specific models, predicting multiple $10 billion to trillion-dollar companies from vertical models in biotech, materials and finance. Her counterweight is quieter: the moat may come down to proprietary data, and in pharma, cell and tissue datasets are genuinely defensible.

On safety, Dave is bluntest: calls to slow model improvement are "utterly insane" because better models cure cancer and create energy — the real threat is small open-source models in bad hands: hacking, viruses, chemical attacks, "already percolated across every terrorist organization in the world." His line: it is not AI versus humans, it is humans with bad intent using AI as a weapon. He favors restricting release of models past a capability threshold — his example is a model that designs a deadlier coronavirus from a single prompt. Wang, the Hugging Face investor, is the pragmatist: enterprises race to the top because they will not take on open-source liability. The UBS investor agrees a global consensus is needed and reaches for the nuclear non-proliferation treaty as the model.

The closing round yields the final idea: "universal basic compute." OpenAI and Anthropic are buying up essentially all the compute and spending it on recursive self-improvement, generating enough revenue to keep paying — so equitable distribution will not happen naturally. What about the entrepreneur with a great idea but no compute? The universities' answer is already: "I literally can't get the compute. It's all been locked up." Asked whether the constraint looks the same in a year, Dave says efficiency will rise a thousand to a million times in two years — and compute will still be sold out, because use cases are near infinite. Wang agrees demand is infinite, citing Jevons' paradox — but adds the one caveat: the bottlenecks may be human. Data-center permitting has become a bipartisan political fight, and without enough permits, transformers and turbines, we could see temporary gluts of exactly the wrong kind.

Three questions the video leaves open:

How durable are today's AI revenue streams, and will the moat go to whoever holds the compute, the data, or the application layer — or to none of them, the way the microprocessor's value leaked away?

If compute efficiency rises a thousandfold and demand still outruns supply, does "universal basic compute" become a real policy question, or do the labs that locked up the capacity keep it?

Will frontier models commoditize under Chinese open-source competition, or will vertical models — biotech, materials, finance — prove the bigger, more durable investment theme?
