The AI Boom’s Biggest Winners May Not Last

A panel from The Next Endeavor 2026 on who funds the AI boom and whether the winners will last — a Reuters moderator, a UBS chief investment officer, a Qualcomm Ventures director, and a seed-stage founder debate capital, compute, and where the value accrues.

Imagination in Action's "The AI Boom's Biggest Winners May Not Last" is a panel from the Next Endeavor 2026 event — and it is really about money, not technology. The moderator, Crystal, a Reuters reporter who runs its tech practice, frames the session around what really powers the AI race: capital. Her guests span the scale: a chief investment officer from UBS; Albert Wang, a senior investment director at Qualcomm Ventures, the chip giant's $2 billion fund; and Dave, founder of seed-stage Link Ventures, who backs university founders in Boston. Dave's opening joke sets the tone: he shares a stage with someone managing trillions; his own funds, about a billion.

The private investors' opening theme is speed. Wang says Qualcomm Ventures has invested for 25 years and never seen a wave like this: companies that raised every 12 to 18 months now come back every four to six. He watched a company go from seed to $100 million in annual revenue in six months — real revenue, unlike the dot-com era's $2 million on $30 million of burn. Dave says the old SaaS rule of "triple-triple-double-double" used to span years; now companies triple every six months, and his latest fund holds four 100x winners with not a single loss — unheard of in venture.

The UBS investor keeps asking the title's question: how durable are these revenue streams? AI is super-exponential and extraordinarily disruptive, she says, but the panel underappreciates how disruptive it will be to today's winners too. Dave answers with one of the session's best analogies: Google and AltaVista were nearly identical; AltaVista is gone. The microprocessor may be the greatest invention in human history, and it was nearly commoditized — while the era's money went to the search layer on top. Building the most useful thing does not decide who keeps the value.

The panel then builds a theory of where the moat sits right now. Wang argues the classic moat — capturing the customer when supply outstrips demand — has inverted: today demand outstrips supply, so whoever has access to supply has the moat. Dave makes it physical: two-thirds of all AI compute runs through TSMC, one company outside the US. Buyers offer two or three times the price and get turned away: "you are begging people to take your money." His firm likes things directly above and below the transformer layer — accelerators like Etched or Groq, data-center investments, retraining data layers — while distrusting the crowded agent-orchestration field.

The panel also bridges to the week's biggest AI story: the UBS investor calls OpenAI's Navier–Stokes proof another watershed, and Dave supplies the inside story — ten thousand agents, 88 hours, OpenAI redirecting compute from the other Millennium problems to leapfrog Anthropic. His punchline is about capital, not math: finishing first or second was a function of compute, "and that's probably not a lasting competitive moat." She sees the same in the $20 trillion-plus of public-market gains since ChatGPT — most of it in the enabling layer. UBS's three structural themes say where the puck goes: AI as a disruptor, power and resources for the energy demand, and AI applied into industries — pharma and biotech most exciting, where AI could lower drug costs and surface new molecules.

On commoditization, the public investor worries perhaps $5 trillion of private-market AI value could prove fragile if general frontier models keep improving and purpose-built apps become mere features. Dave counters that China already answered: open-source models trained for a tenth to a hundredth of the cost destroyed the comfortable story that three or four labs will control all of AI. He sides with Musk's 100x-gain claim for task-specific models, predicting multiple $10 billion to trillion-dollar companies from vertical models in biotech, materials and finance. Her counterweight is quieter: the moat may come down to proprietary data, and in pharma, cell and tissue datasets are genuinely defensible.

On safety, Dave is bluntest: calls to slow model improvement are "utterly insane" because better models cure cancer and create energy — the real threat is small open-source models in bad hands: hacking, viruses, chemical attacks, "already percolated across every terrorist organization in the world." His line: it is not AI versus humans, it is humans with bad intent using AI as a weapon. He favors restricting release of models past a capability threshold — his example is a model that designs a deadlier coronavirus from a single prompt. Wang, the Hugging Face investor, is the pragmatist: enterprises race to the top because they will not take on open-source liability. The UBS investor agrees a global consensus is needed and reaches for the nuclear non-proliferation treaty as the model.

The closing round yields the final idea: "universal basic compute." OpenAI and Anthropic are buying up essentially all the compute and spending it on recursive self-improvement, generating enough revenue to keep paying — so equitable distribution will not happen naturally. What about the entrepreneur with a great idea but no compute? The universities' answer is already: "I literally can't get the compute. It's all been locked up." Asked whether the constraint looks the same in a year, Dave says efficiency will rise a thousand to a million times in two years — and compute will still be sold out, because use cases are near infinite. Wang agrees demand is infinite, citing Jevons' paradox — but adds the one caveat: the bottlenecks may be human. Data-center permitting has become a bipartisan political fight, and without enough permits, transformers and turbines, we could see temporary gluts of exactly the wrong kind.

Three questions the video leaves open:

How durable are today's AI revenue streams, and will the moat go to whoever holds the compute, the data, or the application layer — or to none of them, the way the microprocessor's value leaked away?

If compute efficiency rises a thousandfold and demand still outruns supply, does "universal basic compute" become a real policy question, or do the labs that locked up the capacity keep it?

Will frontier models commoditize under Chinese open-source competition, or will vertical models — biotech, materials, finance — prove the bigger, more durable investment theme?

Jev is incredible

Theo Browne reacts to Jev, TypeSafe AI’s classification model from ChatGPT co-inventor Diogo Almeida — typed, probabilistic decisions in milliseconds rather than autoregressive generation.

The headline numbers are genuinely startling. Jev is aimed at the unglamorous middle of every software pipeline: routing, ranking, tagging, verifying, deciding. Traditional LLMs can take three to over three hundred seconds to do the kind of classification work Jev does in milliseconds, which puts the claimed speedup around forty to two hundred times — a claim Browne says is real and true, no hype. The cost side is arguably crazier: about four cents per million input tokens, against roughly ten dollars per million for a model like Claude Fable, and output tokens that are effectively free — "too cheap to meter." TypeSafe's homepage advertises up to 193.6 times faster and 444.6 times cheaper than frontier models, though the company admits those sit at the higher end of real-world gains.

The centerpiece demo is the race. Side by side: on the left, a parallel TypeSafe API given twenty-seven questions about a pile of data, one request each, started together; on the right, an autoregressive OpenAI API, gpt-5.6-terra. Jev answered all twenty-seven with properly formatted JSON — ranked answers, types, and a confidence estimate on every decision — in about a tenth of a second, for a cost the on-screen readout put at $0.000081, which Theo narrates as an amount that simply rounds to zero. The LLM took nine seconds and cost a dollar thirty. By TypeSafe's accounting that is on the order of one hundred and seventy times cheaper and seventy-something times faster.

What makes this possible is a deliberately lobotomized design. Jev gives up string generation entirely. The shape of its output is deterministic — give it a JSON format and it honors it, always — while the content stays probabilistic but, unusually, calibrated: every output ships with confidence and uncertainty estimates, so you can build thresholds around accuracy instead of flying blind. That determinism of shape underpins the no-hallucination claim: because the contract for what the model must output is fixed, so it cannot hallucinate the format. TypeSafe's benchmark shows Jev at zero percent structured-output errors while even frontier models stumble — Browne notes Claude Haiku at a 45.5 percent error rate, which he says means Haiku should be treated as dead for this work. The design is meant to be called as reliably as code, and it can decide on a whole batch of inputs at once in parallel.

He is equally emphatic about what Jev is not. It has no vision — every demo feeds it structured state as data — and a tiny 32k-token context window. So the demos have quirks: in the Doom demo the agent's brain is effectively wiped every frame, so it spins wildly, never remembering it just turned. The grounded examples are email classification — a hundred exported emails at two hundred milliseconds apiece — and his own coding threads: he classified 32,311 messages across 1,118 of his coding-agent threads for thirty-seven cents, discovering nearly half his work was bug fixing and PR review. His heuristic for the whole model: if a task takes a human under ten seconds once the information is perceived — the color of a shirt — Jev is probably good at it; if it needs real thought, it is not.

The second half is a sustained rant against the hype cycle. He takes aim at a Braintrust tweet proposing Jev as a judge scorer for LLM outputs — a non-reasoning classifier cannot evaluate reasoning it was never given — and at the viral idea of using Jev for context compaction, which he dismantles: compaction is synthesis, not filtering; Jev never sees the reasoning traces the labs keep to themselves; and rewriting history breaks the cache. People have benched it, he says, and it does not perform well at all. His summary line is blunt on purpose: the model is roughly as intelligent as a switch statement. That is not a criticism. A switch statement is the right tool for choosing a path between states, and his fear is that viewers will mistake a reflex for a brain. The value, he argues, is as a new library you install and call inside the tools you build — not an inference farm, not a replacement for Fable or Astra. "The only way this would replace an LLM," one chat commenter wrote, "is if you're using them badly." He mostly agrees.

Three questions the video leaves open. First, if the Jevons paradox framing is right and classification gets so cheap that teams leave it always on — ten decisions a second, forty emails a second — which product architectures actually get redesigned around an ambient decision layer, and which just get a faster version of the old bottleneck? Second, how load-bearing can the calibrated confidence become: will teams genuinely trust Jev's uncertainty estimates enough to auto-route low-confidence cases up to a reasoning model, and does that hybrid hold up in production, or does the calibration drift the moment the data does? Third, what breaks first on the roadmap — with no vision and a 32k context window, is TypeSafe's promised image support and context growth fast enough to keep the magic from wearing off?

The current balance of power in open models

Nathan Lambert’s expanded Congressional-briefing remarks on open-weight models: China now leads clearly in open weights and the lead is not narrowing — while America still leads in truly open-source models, a distinction that matters.

Nathan Lambert writes that he was recently invited to brief members of Congress and their staff on open-weight models through the lens of U.S.-China competition, and this piece is the expanded form of those remarks — his state of the union on open models. Drawing on download data, benchmark scores, usage figures, and academic publication trends, his argument is that China has become the clear leader in open-weight models and the lead is not narrowing — while America still leads in truly open-source models, a distinction that matters.

He begins with definitions. Open language models publish their weights for inspection and downstream use, unlike closed models like GPT-4 or Claude, reachable only through an API or a product. The common category is open-weight: weights, a license, and inference code, as with Meta's Llama, Alibaba's Qwen, Google's Gemma, or DeepSeek's models. True open-source adds everything needed to reproduce the model — the training code and data — and its most prominent examples were built in America, led by the Allen Institute for AI's Olmo family alongside OpenAthena's Marin and EleutherAI's Pythia, all from American nonprofits.

America was the early leader through Meta's Llama. China took the Hugging Face download lead in July 2025 on Qwen's strength; since Lambert first published his American Truly Open Models tracker that August, China's lead has grown to about 1.6 billion, with 3.2 billion total downloads — twice America's.

Benchmarks tell the same story. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, as of his September 14 writing date, the top Chinese models — Z.ai's GLM-5.3 (45), Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 (44), GLM-5.3-Flash (42) — dwarfed the leading American models, Thinking Machines' Inkling (26) and Nvidia's Nemotron 3 Ultra (23). The top American open models trail fifteen Chinese-made ones. The newest Chinese models, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K3, cross the agentic capability threshold that Anthropic's Claude Code crossed in December 2025, meaning open models can now do real agentic work.

The gap to the closed frontier is what makes this urgent. Lambert estimates Chinese open-weight models sit roughly two to five months behind the closed American frontier, with American open-weight models six to nine months behind the likes of OpenAI and Anthropic. That gap has been shrinking for three years, unlocking real markets in 2026. He rejects the dismissal that China is merely distilling American models: distillation matters most in new domains, and fully preventing it would widen the gap by only one to two months. Chinese labs also release faster and focus on a slightly narrower set of tasks, which flatters them on benchmarks. Still, he says, their models are genuinely strong. In 2026 the Chinese labs also shifted to buying cutting-edge training data — challenging reinforcement-learning environments for agentic tasks — from American companies and new Chinese startups.

The piece's distinctive note is the regulatory paradox. As open-weight models approach frontier capabilities, they enable real risks, especially in cybersecurity, and Washington's attention is growing. But restricting the strongest open models would mostly hurt Americans. His example: Hugging Face used a Chinese open-weight model to understand a recent cyberattack, because the closed models refused to answer its questions. Open software can't be kept from bad actors anyway, so restrictions would set back American businesses and researchers. His answer is ecosystem preparation and investment in American open models: owning open models allows better coordination of genuinely global risks while accelerating AI diffusion at home.

The adoption data is striking. OpenRouter, the inference platform popular for trying open-weight models, grew from about one trillion weekly tokens in September 2025 to about eighty trillion today, and the Chinese share rose from roughly seventy percent to over eighty percent. American companies building on Chinese open models include Harvey, Cursor, DoorDash on Kimi models, Airbnb on Qwen, Perplexity on DeepSeek — plus a large swath of younger Silicon Valley startups, increasingly signing enterprise agreements with Chinese labs for permission to use the models, a new form of cross-border collaboration he says he has never witnessed. On arXiv, mentions of any open model rose from two percent in January 2023 to fifty percent in September 2026, with leadership shifting from America to China. Today Qwen appears in thirty percent of papers against Llama's twenty-one, and any Chinese open-weight model in over forty percent against America's thirty — the Chinese share still growing.

He sees signs of hope. American models of comparable capability and size get adopted at disproportionate rates when they exist: OpenAI's gpt-oss became one of the most adopted open-weight models of all time, Google's Gemma 4 rivaled Qwen's most popular small models, and Nvidia's Nemotron has modest adoption. His six takeaways: the open-to-closed gap keeps shrinking toward a two-to-five-month lag; usage is exploding in high-value industries like software engineering and legal services, forming an alternative ecosystem to the best closed models; Chinese companies are the clear leaders; distillation explains only a month or two of their lead; Chinese models, especially Qwen, are now the foundational layer of research; and open models are reaching capability levels where cybersecurity risks demand ecosystem-level preparation. He closes on soft power: open models are becoming the substrate on which everyone outside the few frontier labs will harness accelerating software engineering, and the organizations enabling that access gain influence with it. His final note is humility — we lack good data on open-model use outside the U.S. and China, and with dozens of organizations within a year of the frontier, it is a matter of when, not if, open models cross the thresholds that unlock new workflows.

Three questions hang over the piece. First, can the United States retake open-weight leadership without Meta re-committing to the race Llama started — gpt-oss and Gemma 4 suggest American models get adopted when they exist, but someone has to build them. Second, how do you regulate an ecosystem where restricting the strongest open models hurts your own businesses, when your own defenders rely on the same software. Third, what does it mean for the frontier labs if open models hold a two-to-five-month gap — at what point does selling intelligence behind an API collide with a public substrate nearly as capable.

The AI factory playbook for engineering teams

Clarvo interviews Warp CEO Zach Lloyd on the ‘factory’ — a centralized, code-defined system of agents that turns Slack requests into triaged tickets, implementations, PRs, and QA — with live demos of the dashboards and workflows that power it.

The episode opens with the provocation that humans, not models, are now the bottleneck in software production. Lloyd's team ships more than two thousand pull requests a month through its factory, and the numbers tell the story: the average time from kicking off a task to an open PR is thirty-five minutes, while the wait for the first human review averages three and a half hours. The factory is Warp's answer to that imbalance and to the broader problem of scaling agentic coding beyond individual developers working in private local setups.

A software factory, in Lloyd's definition, is a noun, not a metaphor: a collection of repositories, MCP servers, configuration, and agents that encode the team's entire software development life cycle. The builder-facing side of Warp's factory, which they named Wilson, lives mostly in a public Slack channel. Anyone, including non-engineers, can tag Wilson with a request, and instead of just generating code, the factory treats the request as an input to be processed through a full pipeline: it triages the request, opens an issue in Linear for tracking, implements the change, opens a GitHub PR, and then performs QA, including computer-use verification where the agent records a video of the completed feature in action. Everything happens in public, so anyone on the team can watch the work unfold, jump into the thread, or learn from how a colleague prompted the system. Work can also be initiated automatically, such as crash reports from Sentry feeding directly into the factory, or started from Linear or GitHub rather than Slack.

The manager-facing side is what Lloyd calls an engineering leader's dream: because every run is centralized in the cloud rather than scattered across individual laptops, the factory produces team-wide observability. The dashboard tracks velocity, how automated the factory is, and a metric Clarvo had not seen before, human interactions per PR, which counts every Slack reprompt, Linear comment, and code-review correction as a proxy for how much hand-holding a task required. The instinct is that humans are both the creative force and the bottleneck, and fewer interventions per PR means a more automated, higher-throughput operation. Cost is measured too, as agent spend per PR, and Lloyd notes that model choice has been the biggest lever for driving that cost down, with context management a secondary factor.

Beyond measurement, the factory adds two loops that distinguish it from ordinary coding agents. The first is scoring: because every agent run is recorded, the team can score all runs retroactively along dimensions like redundant tests, using an LLM as a judge with a sampling rate to keep the judging costs down. The second is self-improvement, where an observer agent studies a sample of failed runs, twenty or more to avoid overcorrecting on a single example, and proposes updates to the factory's own code-defined configuration to prevent that failure mode from recurring. Because the factory is defined in code, its state can be frozen at a point in time, replayed, and modified by coding agents themselves.

Lloyd also demonstrates how the same playbook extends to non-technical CEO work. He redesigns a slide deck through the Figma MCP inside a coding agent, talking to the model by voice rather than typing; mines four weeks of sales-call transcripts through the Granola MCP to surface the most frequently asked questions about Warp software factories, anonymized for the recording; and searches his email and calendar with a command-line tool to rediscover cold enterprise leads, asking the agent to assemble the results in a Google Sheet without printing names or addresses in the thread. His meta-point is that he now thinks in parallel tasks, kicking off multiple agent threads at once, one per outcome.

On risk and process, Lloyd says Warp still requires human code review on every PR, though they no longer require the reviewer to be someone other than the person who prompted the agent. Code review, he and Clarvo agree, is becoming an exercise in risk management, with low-risk PRs eventually earning automatic approval so human attention concentrates where it matters. The company still runs a traditional product process underneath the automation, with user interviews, product observation, and design jams before building, because shipping faster is meaningless if the software does not solve a real user problem.

Another powerful extension of the recorded data is model routing through a cost-quality Pareto chart: by replaying real past factory tasks under different model configurations and scoring the outcomes with the same LLM-as-a-judge infrastructure, the team can build a benchmark on its own data rather than relying on generic public benchmarks. That tells them, for example, whether a curated set of front-end tasks can safely run on a cheaper, faster model instead of a flagship reasoning model, accepting whatever quality trade-off the evidence shows, and the findings feed directly into routing strategy.

A few questions this leaves open: how much of this factory approach transfers to teams that do not run their work in public Slack channels, and whether the public-by-default model survives real enterprise secrecy requirements; what happens to the LLM-as-a-judge scoring loop when the judge model and the builder model share the same blind spots, and whether anyone will audit the judges rather than just the builders; and whether driving human interactions per PR toward zero is actually the right optimization, or whether the interactions being eliminated are sometimes the creative friction that produces better software.

5 'Boring' Claude AI Businesses That Can Make You Rich In 2026

Sean Dollwet argues the most profitable AI opportunities are the least exciting ones — five boring, unsexy, quietly lucrative Claude-powered businesses.

Business one is local business SEO blog writing. Every plumber, dentist, HVAC contractor, roofer, and landscaper knows ranking on Google brings customers — yet none will write a blog post about drain repair. That gap is the opening: use Claude to draft batches of local SEO posts, clean them up so they do not read like generic AI output, and sell them as a monthly package. The presenter cites a writer who landed two clients in his first month, worth eight hundred dollars in recurring monthly income. The playbook: write three free samples with Claude, then email twenty to thirty prospects offering four posts a month for four hundred dollars. The downside: you are selling your time, and a lost client must be replaced. That leads to business two — no client-chasing there.

Business two is writing niche nonfiction ebooks and publishing them on Amazon KDP — Amazon's free Kindle Direct Publishing program. This is his own business: he made millions selling books and sold his first catalog through Empire Flippers for eight hundred and twenty thousand dollars. The key difference is distribution. With blog writing you must find your own clients; with KDP, Amazon does the finding — you target a real search term inside Amazon, and its algorithm shows your book to buyers already there with a credit card on file. The best niches are boring, specific, and evergreen: particular cookbooks, hobbies, how-to books, topics people need help with right now. Claude does the heavy writing — research, outlines, manuscripts — though you must still edit, humanize, and pick a profitable niche. The reality check: no one sells one book and quits their job. You need a portfolio, since some books make hundreds a month and others make tens; those small streams add up, and every book keeps paying long after it was written. He pitches his mentorship program, Road to Hero.

Business three is building onboarding documents and SOPs for small businesses with Claude. Companies burn days rewriting the same onboarding materials, and most keep process knowledge inside employees' heads — a problem people gladly pay to make disappear. The work is a thirty to forty-five minute owner interview, recorded and dropped into a Claude project, prompted into a clean structured SOP, then delivered as a polished document days later. Pricing is strong: solo operators charge one thousand to five thousand dollars per project, and even five SOPs can clear five hundred to a thousand dollars per client — for a few hours of labor once templates are dialed in. The startup recipe: pick one business type, build one free example, and email twenty similar businesses with the sample. The catch: once a client's SOP library is done they need you no more — so you are always feeding the pipeline.

Business four is AI chatbots for local businesses, using Claude. Every local business gets the same ten questions from website visitors — hours, insurance, service areas, prices — and most visitors bounce because nobody answers at nine on a Sunday evening. The solution: a chatbot on the business's website, trained on its FAQs and pricing, with Claude reasoning behind a drag-and-drop interface — no coding required. The model that works is setup plus retainer: a one-time setup fee of roughly five hundred to five thousand dollars, then a monthly retainer of five hundred to twenty-five hundred dollars per client to keep the bot updated. The playbook: pick one industry, build one working demo, then email thirty to fifty businesses with a screen-recording of the demo and a price. You are not selling technology; you are selling the end of losing customers on a Sunday night. The downside: chatbots are not set and forget — clients expect you to work whenever they ask.

Business five is building AI voice agents for small businesses. If chatbots handle website text, voice agents answer the actual phone — where local businesses bleed the most money: around seventy-three percent miss a big chunk of their calls daily, and every missed call is a customer calling someone else. A voice agent picks up on the first ring, sounds close to human, and handles bookings and common questions — literally recovering lost revenue. The setup is harder: designing conversation flows, testing edge cases, and calendar and CRM integration. But the numbers reflect that: setup fees of five hundred to a few thousand dollars, monthly retainers of three hundred to fifteen hundred per client — so ten to fifteen clients can push past ten thousand a month. You start by focusing on one industry, demoing a first agent to owners, and closing a few clients cheaply for case studies before raising prices. The tricky part: the highest technical bar, and you must fix things fast when they break. It is also the newest opportunity — competition is thin, but not for long.

Stepping back, four of the five are client services: find clients, deliver, get paid, repeat. That works fast if you do it right, but you own no asset — when you stop working, you stop getting paid. The KDP business is the odd one out: every book is an asset that keeps selling while you sleep and years after you wrote it, because Amazon brings the buyers and the portfolio compounds. His philosophy is to focus only on things that compound — his YouTube videos earn for years the same way his books do. For beginners, he concedes a service business is a reasonable way to get money fast: start there, save, then invest into something that compounds. He closes by asking viewers which of the five they would build first.

A few questions this leaves open: which of the five would you actually start with? For the service businesses, how would you keep clients long enough to reach stable income instead of a replacement treadmill? And for the KDP route, how do you find an underserved niche and keep quality high enough that your books keep selling for years?

Industry · manufacturing · tech hubs

How LA Is Becoming America's New Industrial Tech Hub | Odd Lots

Odd Lots hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway tape live in Los Angeles on America’s attempted reindustrialization, with Hadrian’s Chris Power and Impulse Space’s Tom Mueller.

Power opens with a theory of the decline of American manufacturing that doubles as a theory of imperial decline. Every great power, he argues, does four simple things: maintains a generative population, builds the best industrial capacity, wins on the battlefield, and is handed the world's reserve currency. Industrial power earns the currency, yet within four or five generations of softness the sequence reverses: the industrial base is traded for financial power, the spreadsheet replaces the factory floor, and the currency is mistaken for a birthright. The Netherlands ceded power to the British Empire, which ceded power to the American Empire, each handoff marked by that same trade of industry for finance, roughly every hundred and fifty years. America has spent its inheritance trading manufacturing returns for hedge-fund returns, hollowing out the middle of its society.

Mueller answers from the factory floor. When he left SpaceX, the company was launching a hundred and sixty-five rockets in a single year and had put ten thousand satellites in orbit. That record is his reply to the premise: tell me again why we cannot build in the United States.

Mueller describes Impulse Space as the sprinter vans and trucks to the launch industry's container ships: its vehicles take over where the launch vehicle leaves off, moving cargo to other orbits. One vehicle is a precision-maneuvering satellite, with three already on orbit. Another, called Helios, is a kick stage that can escape Earth's gravity and multiply the payload a Falcon Nine can send to the moon or Mars by about five. Power describes Hadrian as factories as a service, running on a software platform plus a technician base the company trains from scratch. Hadrian takes any design and builds it, producing everything from spaceflight components to entire missiles to thirty percent of a Virginia-class submarine. Power corrects the idea that a factory full of humans is a robotics problem: ninety percent of advanced manufacturing is high-judgment work, and Hadrian's software lets an engineer load a fiddly task into a robot cell and walk away without fearing a crash.

Impulse is vertically integrated, like SpaceX, while buying many parts from Hadrian. Mueller's rule is that you build development engines and spacecraft yourself so you learn all the hard lessons of machining and assembly; only once you truly understand a part do you outsource it for production, keeping the genuinely secret parts at home. The Prompt Engineering channel argues agents fail in production not from lack of intelligence but from broken context — structured, surgical context beats a bigger flagship model.

Three failure modes open the argument, each grounded in a customer support agent. The first is the dead end: the agent pulls the support ticket, but no relational path connects it to the order record because they live in different silos, so the model does what language models do when blind and hallucinates. In all three cases the model's reasoning was fine: the failure was providing the right context at the right time, which can quietly break agentic systems without anyone noticing.

Prompt engineering is about structuring the initial instruction: a single string whose phrasing you tweak, onto which you add short examples and a schema. Context engineering operates an entire layer above that: the real-time system deciding what state the agent sees on every step — which customer record to fetch, which live states to inspect, and how to deliver them fast enough for production. Prompt engineering is what you say to the model; context engineering is the runtime environment you build around it, and most agentic failures trace back to wrong or broken context.

Production-ready context, the video contends, must satisfy four mechanical rules. It must be navigable: the agent can traverse relationships between entities like users, orders, and tickets instead of doing blind keyword lookups over raw text. It must be fresh, because an agent acting on stale state acts confidently wrong. It must be fast, because dozens of subqueries per task compound query latency into broken workflows. And it must compound — the rule most setups ignore. Today, when an agent session ends, its context is wiped to zero, so the next day the model must relearn who the user is, what conventions they use, and what broke last time. A production system has to get smarter the more it is used.

The reason most teams cannot build this, the video argues, is organizational fragmentation. Team A sets up an isolated vector database that works great. Team B builds an agent with custom persistence scripts over a separate store, also great. Team C stitches together a few tools. None of them talk to each other, and six months later there are a dozen disconnected data pipelines, nobody can guarantee freshness, and every new agent spends most of its time rebuilding context plumbing. This, the video claims, is the state of most engineering organizations right now.

To make context compound across sessions, an agent needs two tiers of memory. Tier one is working memory, the active task scratchpad, wiped when the session ends. Tier two is long-term memory, storing durable facts that survive across sessions — project decisions, environment configurations, debugging edge cases. The trick is how facts transition between them. Users will not manually tag facts, so the system needs an automated memory layer that parses conversations and extracts durable facts in the background. When a fresh session boots, it runs a semantic search to pull only the historical facts relevant to the active prompt, while the engine handles duplication and expiry, dropping transient noise and pinning permanent project rules.

Jev is what he calls a System One model: machine-native, programmable AI built for code rather than chat. You send it structured state and typed questions; it returns typed, probabilistic answers with confidence values. It cannot write sentences. There is no chat box. The name comes from the Jevons paradox, and the single metric the company optimizes is intelligence per dollar.

The launch numbers frame the conversation. The Jev launch video passed roughly forty million views, the company's Discord swelled past a hundred thousand members, and within days TypeSafe was serving more than a trillion Jev tokens a day, with machines — not people — calling the API through the night. The intellectual core of the episode is Almeida's autopsy of RLHF, the technique he helped pioneer. He traces three meanings of the term — Christiano's 2017 robot-backflip work, the 2020 learning-to-summarize effort, and his own 2022 InstructGPT paper on instruction following — and argues that the important part was never the PPO algorithm but the North Star: optimize for pleasing the human in the loop. That North Star, he says, produced models that are extraordinary at being liked and terrible at being trusted. He spends real time on mode collapse, the under-discussed downside of RLHF: reward models punish visible errors so harshly that models learn to drop minority modes and generate conservatively, warping their probability distributions. He loves Yann LeCun's famous "LLMs are doomed" slide precisely because it looks mathematically obvious yet fails empirically — and mode collapse is why. A model trained to never be caught erring cannot give you epistemically honest probabilities, which makes string-based chat models, in his phrase, a bad time for decision-making. Sycophancy, overconfidence, hallucination, the bold-italic-emoji house style that wins LM Arena — all of it, he argues, is downstream of optimizing for human preference on strings.

Jev's answer is a new North Star he calls RLCD: reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions, a still-unpublished technique that trains for honest probabilities on what he calls System One tasks — the fast, intuitive, single-judgment calls that Daniel Kahneman's System One describes, as opposed to the slow deliberative reasoning that RLVR has pushed to impressive but fragile extremes. He is careful to say he is not anti-reasoning; he is awed by what RLVR achieved. But he scores the history of LLM post-training as RLHF one full North Star, RLVR a generous fraction of one, and RLCD the third. The practical consequence is the API: three new primitives called Choice, Score, and Noulli (from Bernoulli, deliberately not called Bool). A Choice maps to a switch statement on an enum, a Noulli to an if statement, a Score to sorting or thresholding. Everything — state, instructions, criteria — can be structured JSON rather than prompt templates, because, as Almeida puts it, system messages are disgusting global variables and turning numbers into strings is something you do for printing, not for computation inside the machine.

Safety alignment, in his framing, means following someone else's instructions — OpenAI's or Anthropic's — which is legitimate for a first-party product but unacceptable in infrastructure, and every such intervention fractures the model's intelligence further. He would rather TypeSafe be like a database, which is not asked to police what the CIA does with it. Second, benchmarks. In 2020 he left to work on scaling bigger models, founding MosaicML, which took off after ChatGPT landed in 2022; Databricks bought it in 2023, and it is now about a quarter of Databricks' total revenue. His new company, Unconventional AI, is the rest of the talk: an attempt to rebuild the computer from physics first principles.

The core argument is energy. Google serves more than 3.2 quadrillion tokens a month, and at ten joules per token, on the low end of the spectrum, that single company's AI serving comes to about twelve gigawatts. The United States puts roughly forty gigawatts into data centers today, and the whole world is under a hundred. If models get bigger and demand keeps growing, he estimates the world runs into the wall in about three years: a trillion-dollar market growing exponentially on top of an energy supply that grows roughly linearly, and the gap between those two curves is the problem technology has to close. Data center thinking has already shifted accordingly. It used to be about floor space, then networking, then GPUs, and today it is about energy first: you secure the power contract and then figure out how to fill it. About half the cost of serving a token is energy, the rest hardware and space, so his business case is simple: monetize every watt a thousand times better than existing hardware.

Biology is his proof that this is possible. The human brain runs on about twenty watts, a monkey's brain on about one watt, and a squirrel's brain on eight milliwatts while producing behavior precise a thousand jumps out of a thousand. The striking comparison is information movement: the human cortex moves only about sixteen billion bits per second, while a high-end computing system moves nearly thirty trillion bits in and out of memory per second, and ten to a hundred times that inside the chip. Most of the energy in a computing system goes into moving information around, not into computing. That is an artifact of the machine we have had since ENIAC in 1945: memory on the outside, compute somewhere else, bits shuttled back and forth, very fast. We built computers to be fast because the alternative was humans doing calculations by hand, and that never contemplated energy efficiency. Transistor scaling, the free efficiency of Moore's law, has largely ended, so the architecture itself has to change.

What Unconventional AI is building is what he calls a dynamical computer, or 4D computing: the fourth dimension is time, expressed in the dynamics of the system, plus the three physical dimensions of 3D die stacking. The conceptual move is to cut out the middleman of abstraction. Digital itself is an abstraction: a transistor has states between zero and one, but we engineer it to behave as a clean bit, and then we stack abstractions all the way up to neural networks, each one lossy. Your brain has neurons but no linear algebra and no floating-point math; the physics of the neurons gives rise to intelligence, and he wants to mimic some of that with semiconductors. The idea is dynamical systems theory, the study of how simple individual behaviors produce emergent properties, the way birds in a flock create complex patterns from each bird just watching its neighbors. His intuition pump is a row of metronomes on a rolling plank: they synchronize purely through the physics of the system, and that kind of emergent behavior is what he wants to build circuits out of.

He asked whether such a system could do real computation and connect to generative AI. The answer so far is UNO, an open-source image-generation model built on a set of oscillators, the first demonstration that this scales to trained, useful output. One surprise along the way is sparsity: fully connecting a thousand elements means a million connections, which does not scale, but it turns out you can throw away connections and get a more trainable system. He calls it a holy grail, one of those rare things that is more efficient, more scalable, and higher-performing at once, and it works in real physical systems, not just simulation.

Then came the announcement. The first physical dynamical computer ever built was designed in five months; the company started in earnest in January without a team, taped out on June first, and the chip is already back in the lab. The first-ever images generated by such a computer were shown on stage, and Rao said this was literally the first time the company was talking about it publicly. The headline number: about five hundred nanojoules per image, against millijoules per image on a GPU, many orders of magnitude, for the simple reason that the machine does not move information around. It fuses compute and memory into one thing; there is no memory interface, and each individual computing element is itself a memory. The goal, revised inward from five years to three and a half because progress has beaten expectations, is a thousandfold improvement in power efficiency. Then the overarching goal: beat biology. We sit about ten billion times away from the thermodynamic limit, the floor physics allows, while mammalian brains are within one or two orders of magnitude of it. The vision is compute everywhere, including billions of robots, and a shift from a few gigawatt-scale data centers to many small, local, adaptive ones.

If that efficiency arrives, the market does not shrink; it explodes. That is the Jevons paradox: when the cost of an asset drops, consumption grows by more than the drop, and he thinks it creates the largest market humanity has ever seen. Chamath Palihapitiya joined for the closing Q&A and pressed on the path to product. Rao's answer: within about two years, a rack-scale data center product, tokens in and tokens out over a network cable, with completely different guts. Porting happens at the model layer, not the operations layer, so existing models will run; the computation can be characterized as matrix multiplication but is implemented as time-varying behavior. The developer surface will be Python libraries rather than CUDA. And the team spans dynamical-systems theorists to chip builders, two groups he says don't talk to each other, so coordinating them into one machine is the hardest part of the company.

When a thousandfold efficiency gain makes intelligence vastly cheaper, does the Jevons paradox demand curve actually materialize, or do energy and political constraints cap it first? And what does "beating biology" mean for safety when the most efficient substrate for intelligence is no longer the human brain?

How I Review AI Code - (Meta Senior Staff Engineer)

John Kim, a Meta senior staff engineer, on how he reviews AI-generated code — what’s changed about the reviewer’s job now that the author is an agent.

John Kim, a senior staff software engineer at Meta, opens with the question dividing the engineering world right now: are you even reading your code anymore, or do you just let agents generate it and ship it into the wild without review? He describes two camps. One says that if you're still reviewing your code, you're not moving fast enough — agents are already better at reviewing than you are, so reading code is a waste of time. The other says agents still make dumb mistakes, the output is AI slop, and you need to slow down and actually read what gets written. Kim's answer to which camp he's in is, in his words, "it depends" — but he means something specific and practical by it, and the rest of the video is his system for making that judgment call.

The first thing he wants viewers to internalize is scale. With the advent of AI coding, the amount of code people generate is going through the roof — more features, more tests, more everything, because one person can spin up multiple agents and produce a ton of code. It's become almost impossible for a single engineer to review even their own code, let alone other people's. So his core framework is to stop treating code review as a binary — read or don't read — and start treating it as a gradient. AI agents should be part of the entire journey to production: planning, building, reviewing, and launching. Each phase feeds the answer to how much you review and when.

The big mental model he introduces is to think of the codebase like a tree. Some parts are the trunk — the main entry points, the core infrastructure like image processing and networking, the code that everything else depends on. Other parts are leaf nodes — isolated components, one-off pieces that touch nothing else. Review effort should track the blast radius of a change. If you're touching core image-rendering infrastructure that half the app depends on, you spend a lot of time reading that code. If it's a leaf node, simple agent validation — unit tests, snapshot tests, a visual check — with proof attached to the PR is enough.

That leads to his insistence on feature gating, which he says should start at the planning stage, not as an afterthought. Feature gating means branching logic in the code that lets you turn things on and off — he mentions LaunchDarkly-style toggling as the popular option. From the beginning of a feature, plan in broad strokes and gate the major integration layers: separate the leaf nodes that don't touch existing code from the parts that integrate with production, and gate those integration points hard. This gating is what makes the rest of the system safe, because it gives you the ability to take decisions back.

The centerpiece of his review process is requiring proof in every PR. Agents should anchor their diffs with evidence: unit tests (and he adds a side note — be careful here, since agents will write useless tests unless you build a skill that forbids them), runtime logs that give the agents and reviewers more visibility, and visual evidence like screenshots or screen recordings. He shows an example where a UI change to a card-saving flow comes with a screenshot of the save button in the built UI — seeing that visual proof immediately makes him trust the AI's output. He also asks agents to report a confidence level on their code. The principle is agentic validation: the more proof is attached to the PR up front, the less code you need to read. And the payoff compounds — more proof up front means you can move faster, not slower.

When he does read, the trunk-versus-leaf distinction governs everything. Leaf code — written in isolation, fully gated off — gets a skim: is anything obviously wrong? Trunk code gets real scrutiny. His example is a reducer holding shared state: if a change alters existing behavior and isn't gated, he reads it carefully because it changes production and carries trickle-down side effects. And everything anchors on safety, which he frames as three questions: what is the control experience that must not change, how bad is it if this goes wrong, and can I roll it back? That last one comes straight back to feature gating.

One thing he explicitly declares dead is the stylistic nit. The old code-review culture of arguing over how code should look — personal writing preferences, formatting tastes — doesn't survive the age of agents writing most of the code. He'd rather lean on static checking, type checking, linting, and agent reviewers to handle that entire category. Code is more fluid now, refactors are cheaper, and as long as validation exists, nits don't belong in the review conversation. He does still read code, he says — in fact more than before — but the review is governed by a knob: is this a trunk change with lots of dependencies? Then the knob turns up. Is it gated, is it safe, can it be rolled back? Then the knob turns down. One-way-door changes get the most attention.

For the agent-assisted part of review, he thinks AI is already genuinely good at reviewing code — better than most humans, in his opinion — but most people use it wrong. The critical rule: use an independent agent for review, a fresh sub-agent that didn't write the code. The coding agent will cheat — it has all the previous context, it'll anchor on the earlier conversation and just be agreeable with you. A separate reviewer without that context gives you an honest read. He also recommends good review skills and PR templates, noting that his biggest pet peeve is a PR whose summary and test plan are longer than the code changes themselves.

He demos two specific tools. Codex offers a GitHub integration that runs a reviewer on new PRs, trained specifically for review. And he shows using "goals" to babysit a PR: tell the system to check the PR every hour, address any change requests from the Codex reviewer, rerun the test suite, run the agentic validations, update the PR notes, and resend. These are the kinds of behaviors he says you should build into your own skills.

The part of the video that lands hardest is his distinction between merge-ready and launch-ready. If you code the way he describes — agents building broad strokes behind gates while you skim leaf code — you'll be about 80% done when everything merges. The last 20%, getting something launch-ready, is where most of his human effort goes, and it takes as long as the first 80%. Before launch he has agents audit the entire written codebase, and then he personally does refactoring, cleanup, and careful review of performance, bugs, and security — plus details like animations. That's where his code becomes production-clean. Finally, he strongly recommends running experiments or canaries on launch: trickle traffic to the gated feature, watch for crashes or alerts, and roll back fast. His summary image is an "agentic garden" — lots of agents doing verification work, all anchored on safety and the ability to take back bad decisions through gating.

He closes by admitting his answer keeps changing. Right now he's comfortable skimming some code depending on where it sits in the tree. After a few more model generations, he says, he might tell you not to read any of it. The one durable advantage, he adds, is knowing your codebase — because if you understand it, you can instantly tell what's touching the dangerous zones and what's just a leaf.
