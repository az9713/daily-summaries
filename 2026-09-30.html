Rails World 2026 Opening Keynote - DHH

The Ruby on Rails channel's "Rails World 2026 Opening Keynote - DHH" is DHH's hour-long opening address from Rails World 2026 in Austin, delivered in front of a room of Rails programmers. He diagnoses himself with "AI euphoria" rather than the AI psychosis his critics might prescribe, and spends the next hour making the case that this is the most exciting thing computers have ever done, with receipts from 37signals to back it up.

His framing is history. He opens with portrait painters in the eighteenth century, where a master painter spent months on a single commissioned portrait, then traces the long arc of the camera: its arrival around 1840, the Kodak Brownie in 1900 putting a picture in reach for about a dollar, the painters pivoting to cubism and impressionism, then a long plateau until camera phones made photography frictionless and the world started taking two trillion photos a year. Technology moves in fits and starts, he argues, and the point is that the old craft did not die. It found a new home.

The story has a personal hook he clearly loves. The Danish portrait painter in it, Laurits Tuxen, is his great-great-grandfather, and the woman in the pink dress in the painting is his great-grandmother Yvonne. Tuxen embraced having his own portrait taken by the very technology that had ended his old profession. DHH is holding that up as the model he wants for programmers now.

His date for software's Kodak Brownie moment is November 24, 2025, when Opus 4.5 arrived. There was a trough of disillusionment from February through May 2026, when new models felt like steps backward. Then Fable 5 and Mythos landed in June, GPT-6 Astra in September, and DeepSeek-4-1 Flash a week later broke the idea that frontier intelligence belongs to a few American companies. The old debate over the 10x programmer is settled, he says, and the live question now is whether the gap between programmers with these tools and those without them is a hundred times or a thousand times.

Then it gets concrete. A couple of weeks ago, 37signals went pencils down on hand-written code. Writing code by hand is now an exceptional state there, treated like a bug showing up in Sentry, a sign the machine is broken. He polled the room: about five hands went up for still writing material amounts of code by hand every week. Basecamp 5 was the trial run. In the spring, designers vibe-coded the finishing features and the architecture came back looking, in his words, like Swiss cheese. The team concluded the technology was not ready. Wrong conclusion, he says now. Give it five minutes and you get Fable, and everything works as intended.

The new work is HEY Next, a new version of his email service, and the first decision was to stop making it a web app. A week into the effort, six high-fidelity native applications were already on stage being demoed. He points to Shopify's tiny team rewriting the Shop app as native proof this is coming for every web app that was only a web app because a small team could not afford better. The backend goes further. He hates Rust and says so at length, but the agent-written Rust backend uses ninety-nine percent less CPU and ninety-five percent less memory, and his back-of-the-envelope math says HEY's peak traffic could be served on a single Raspberry Pi. The division of labor is simple: he tells the agent what to do, the agent writes the Rust, he never looks at it.

Where does that leave Ruby and Rails? The web still matters for apps that cannot ask anyone to install anything, and Rails is well positioned because convention over configuration maps directly to token efficiency. Agent evals run by Evil Martians for the Rails Foundation saturated the first benchmark at ninety-five percent, so they had to build a harder one. His practical advice is to not be overly prescriptive, keep a beginner's mind, and give every app a command line interface, because agents operate through interfaces, not screens. The HEY CLI lets him search by concept instead of keyword, and it found a five-year-old email about sneakers and a podcast that no keyword search ever could.

The personal numbers are the knockout. Across twenty-one years he wrote about thirty thousand lines of production Ruby a year. In August, he wrote a hundred and fifty thousand lines in one month. And there is, he says, a programming language he likes better than Ruby. It is called English. Writing programs in English turned out to be a tremendously satisfying experience.

Then comes the line that defines the keynote. He has retired from being a professional programmer, probably around March. He does not mean it as an eulogy. He spent a quarter of a century chiseling code by hand and loved every minute of it, and he wants everyone to look back on that era with joy rather than regret. It is simply over as an economically productive enterprise, and on the other side is a new career as a professional maker of things.

He takes the same rethink to software architecture. Abstractions were invented to avoid repeating ourselves, but the price of repetition has fallen near zero, so the choke points that abstractions create no longer buy what they used to. Nobody has the blueprint yet, he admits, and the audience is invited to help draw it.

Omarchy, his Linux setup, gets its own victory lap. Twenty million dollars raised. Anoush from AMD installed it on a laptop in thirty-five seconds. In the lab, a full operating system install is down to nine seconds. And this very talk was built in Hype, new presentation software he wrote that week, built on Markdown, with a binary of half a megabyte. Every optimization is now within reach, he says, because an agent can work on it overnight.

He gives the skeptics their due, briefly. Security is the one legitimate concern he names, with a recent Rails image-library vulnerability as the example, and HotCell as one defensive effort. Then he treats the rest as prediction error. No economist can call the stock market six months out, so they do not know what a paradigm shift looks like either. He retells the ATM story: the nineteen fifties panic that thirty thousand bank tellers would be out of work in eighteen months, followed by the Jevons paradox punchline that teller employment actually grew. His prescription is P(bloom) over P(doom). If you are sad about it up front and it turns out wonderful, what a waste of time.

The closer is the case for total optimism. Agent Luther, he says, will disintermediate the cleric class the way Martin Luther did, and make programmers of everyone. Kids trying a kids version of Omarchy are not stressed about unlearning five hundred layers of habit. Take the white pill, lean in with gusto, and be happy about the future, because the rational choice, he insists, is to be happy about it. The future, in his words, is galactically awesome.

Open questions

  1. If writing code by hand is no longer an economically productive enterprise, what becomes of the craft knowledge and taste that hand-written code used to teach?
  2. HEY's rewrite bets that native apps are now cheap to build and maintain; what happens when six native apps drift apart and the team has to reason about behavior it never wrote?
  3. Security is named as the one legitimate worry while everything else is treated as prediction error; is P(bloom) a strategy, or just a bet that the ATM analogy holds at a scale no prior technology has matched?

OpenAI Dev Day 2026: The releases that actually matter

This is a summary of the How I AI episode "OpenAI Dev Day 2026: The releases that actually matter," posted September 29, 2026. Claire Vo spent the day in San Francisco at OpenAI's Dev Day, from the keynote through the afternoon, and got early access to several of the releases. Her framing is simple: the good news is that OpenAI released a lot of stuff, and the bad news is that OpenAI released a lot of stuff, so this episode picks out what she thinks actually deserves your attention.

The big release of the morning is Dots. A Dot is an always-on, long-running agent that works for you across ChatGPT, Codex, its own cloud computer, your local computer, and your phone, through text or a real phone call, and even inside Slack. Right now each user gets a single Dot, available on Pro, Business, and Enterprise plans. Vo's own Dot is named Ba, a pink blob with glasses and googly eyes. Dots are positioned as a replica or delegate of you, a master thread that can kick off Codex threads and chat threads to do work on your behalf.

Vo's early take on Dots is that the bones are good. It found the right shoes while shopping for her, coded well for her, and reminded her proactively about her kids' swim class schedule. But the user experience has rough edges, and the information architecture is confusing: it is not clear what a Dot thread is versus a chat thread versus a Codex thread. She is not doing a full Dots review yet, partly because OpenAI is clearly still building it out by the hour, and partly because her own habits lean toward Grok bots for work, where she hires micro-agents for specific jobs rather than one agent to rule them all. She finds Muse's experience a little more consumer-friendly and approachable.

The release she calls underhyped is Spaces: a place in ChatGPT where humans and agents collaborate on documents, artifacts, and slides, with proper sharing, permissions, and data controls built in. Her Dot made her a space called "your personal scratch pad" for shared to-dos, and she sees this as what enterprises have been waiting for: an AI-native collaboration surface with governance. She also notes the parallel with her own earlier project, a shared notebook where all her agents could collaborate on her to-do list.

Related to Spaces is the Sites update. Sites is OpenAI's built-in website and app builder, and it now lets you bundle connectors and plugins into a deployed site. Her example: a custom dashboard pulling from your Snowflake connector, where a teammate who logs in sees the report through their own connector, and data they lack access to stays behind the controls. She calls this OpenAI's answer to the number one enterprise problem she hears about: what to do with the flood of vibecoded internal tools and prototypes. The open competition here is between Claude artifacts and OpenAI sites for the enterprise source of truth.

On models, OpenAI released GPT-6.1 Sol, described as near-Astra intelligence at Sol speed and price: $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, versus $10 and $50 for Astra. More interesting to Vo is the Decisions API, OpenAI's competitor to constrained-decision models like "little Jeff," except this one has vision. She built a demo that scanned 100 frames of a 40-minute episode in under 10 seconds to find thumbnails where neither guest was making an awkward face, a job she says the vision-less Jeff could never do, plus the classic hot-dog demo at 100% confidence in under a second. The Decisions API is not released yet; she plans a head-to-head against Jeff when it ships.

The release she most wants, though she admits she cannot really afford it, is Astra ultrafast. Models in Codex can run in normal, fast, or ultrafast mode, where ultrafast runs about eight times as fast at six times the cost. Her two demos show why she cares: The Other Pencil, a collaborative sketch pad where Astra draws SVGs behind her strokes in near real time, and Little Starship, a 3D app where prompts render new scenes live, from a bathroom with a rubber ducky to a tropical island to a zero-gravity float party. Thirty minutes of her kids playing Little Starship cost her about $97. Latency is still a little slow for true real-time, but she calls it magical.

Around the edges, the keynote also covered the Agents API with computer use, the Codex developer primitives, plugin updates with rumored monetization for plugin builders, and a new Pro 500 plan. Her what-to-try-first advice: give Dots a whirl and report back, look at Spaces and Sites if your company struggles with AI collaboration, and pay attention to the models, because a developer who builds both with and on these models finds the Decisions API and ultrafast genuinely exciting.

Open questions

  1. Will Spaces become the enterprise source of truth for AI-generated work, or will companies keep their collaboration in Slack, Google Drive, and Dropbox? Vo is watching adoption closely, and the push and pull between OpenAI-hosted documents and existing platforms is unresolved.
  2. When the Decisions API actually ships, will vision make it the missing piece in AI architecture that Vo expects, or will the simpler Jeff-style models keep winning on cost and simplicity? The promised head-to-head evaluation has not happened yet.
  3. What will frontier intelligence at near real-time speed actually enable once the price comes down? Vo's demos point at new kinds of products, but a thirty-minute demo costing about $97 shows how far the economics still have to go.

We're Reaching the Physical Limits of Chips

This is a summary of the Sourcery with Molly O'Shea episode "We're Reaching the Physical Limits of Chips," posted September 30, 2026. Molly O'Shea sits down with Steven Latre, VP of AI at imec and a Thema board member, and Adam Chambers of Strike Capital, to talk about what comes after the GPU.

Latre starts by explaining imec, which he calls the most unknown hidden gem in semiconductors. It is a research institute in Belgium that works at the very earliest stage of chip building, with a clean room facility nobody else in the world has. The biggest chip providers come to imec first to design the next generation chip, a process that takes five to ten years, and then take the designs home to commercialize. Latre says almost no chip in the world today has not been touched by imec in some way. The institute has been driving the chip roadmap for forty years.

His core claim is that Moore's Law is plateauing. Transistors have shrunk to about one nanometer deep, roughly one hundred thousand times smaller than a human hair, and he says we are at the end of physics of what we can still do. The era ahead, in his view, will require hardware that is far more inventive than anything of the last ten to twenty years if chips are to keep getting faster.

Chambers maps the bottlenecks from an investor's seat. GPUs are very good at training, he says, but the binding constraint is now in the inference layer: power and energy, memory, cooling, and data transfer. Getting useful memory to a GPU as fast as possible, with more bandwidth and lower latency, is the most interesting territory, and he expects liquid cooling to move closer and closer to the chip over the next five to ten years, with a lot of the value going to the manufacturing side.

Memory is already the number one component that is broken. Only three companies in the world produce memory, Micron, SK Hynix, and Samsung, together holding about ninety five percent of the market. Hyperscalers have taken over much of their inventory, and prices for the memory part of the chip have risen seven hundred percent this year. Chambers frames this as a hardware era renaissance, with fantastic chip margins while software gets eaten by AI.

Latre pushes back on the idea that the pyramid has flipped. He calls today's software market immature: the last four or five years of AI were tremendous in software, but large language models are still a brute force approach, and the value extracted is limited. New software evolutions tied much closer to hardware will follow, and the value will grow there too. On the flood of new chip startups, he says demand looks endless and the real bottleneck is manufacturing, not the number of companies. Every company can bring a different unique design, and more diverse algorithms will need more diverse hardware beyond GPUs.

He is also blunt that models will not keep getting bigger. The newest open-source models already carry more parameters than the human brain, yet the chip needed to fuel such a model is roughly a million times less efficient than the brain. He says the era of going from one hundred seventy five billion parameters to ten trillion is going to stop, and companies are already abandoning the scaling hypothesis to solve memory bottlenecks instead.

The technology both guests are most excited about is photonics. Moving data as light instead of through copper attacks three bottlenecks at once: energy, cooling, and data transfer. Today photonics sits between clusters as linear pluggable optics, but Chambers expects optical components to move closer to the actual GPU within five years, toward co-packaged optics. Latre calls it the killer technology that will enable the chips of the future, and points to the human brain's three-dimensional structure as proof of what efficient data movement can do.

For hottest takes, Latre predicts that in five to ten years we will laugh at how old fashioned AI was today, the way people now laugh at dialup internet tones. He expects an actual software revolution to follow. Chambers says he is biased, but his take is to recruit and invest in people who combine a European academic and technical background with American business sense.

Open questions

  1. Will photonics really reach co-packaged optics within five years, or will the optical-to-electrical conversion bottleneck keep it at the cluster edge? The timeline decides who captures the value in AI infrastructure.
  2. If the scaling hypothesis is truly over, where does AI value accrue next: in hardware, in software tied closely to hardware, or in entirely new model architectures? Latre expects a software revolution, but neither guest describes what it looks like.
  3. Is the memory crunch a cyclical supply story or a structural constraint? Three producers hold ninety five percent of the market and prices are up seven hundred percent this year, yet neither guest lays out what the relief valve is.

Opus 5.5 Is Good Again. My Proof Cost 50 Cents.

On his channel AI News & Strategy Daily, Nate B. Jones opens this episode with a claim he can measure: Opus 5.5 makes him want to give Claude more work, because the writing is easier to steer and the model finishes tasks on far fewer tokens. He proves it the fun way. He had Opus 5.5 turn his beanie-hat logo into a working 514-piece Lego model, complete with a 63-page instruction booklet, 58 build steps, a parts list, a geometric connection checker, and an LDR file that verifies the bricks can physically connect.

The token bill for the whole build was 89 million tokens. That cost him about one percent of his weekly usage, roughly 50 cents of the subscription he is actually paying for. At API pricing, the same work would have run at least 44 dollars. Jones's thesis is that this is the metric that matters for the model race. Not benchmark scores, but cost per finished task: how many reads, attempts, re-explanations, and churned outputs it takes to get the whole job done.

Opus 5.5's list pricing helps: 4 dollars per million input tokens and 20 per million output, 20 percent below Opus 5 and 60 percent below Fable 5.1. Anthropic says typical workloads cost around 40 percent less, and Jones finds that plausible. Others agree: GitHub and Lovable report fewer steps, Spotify reports cheaper and faster runs, and individual users are saying the same on X. He cautions that 40 percent cheaper does not mean 40 percent fewer tokens, since input, output, cache, and context all bill at different rates. You have to measure the full job, not just the first pretty screenshot.

The second pillar is writing. Jones says Opus 5.5 feels like Opus 4.6 again, the release everyone loved, but with far more intelligence behind it. After 4.7, 4.8, and Fable made steering harder, he sees community frustration reflected everywhere, from Brahm Cohen's widely read article on Claude's argumentative behavior to Anthropic's own GitHub issue tracker. His definition of good AI writing is that it preserves your intent. A revision that shortens your paragraph by deleting the argument that made it worth reading is AI slop, no matter how polished it sounds. Opus 5.5, he finds, takes direction cleanly and gets there fast.

For long-running work, he offers a rule: give the model clear stop conditions. Anthropic's announcement cites Sean Hanks at Cleo leaving Opus 5.5 on an engineering task across six repos unattended for 18 hours. Jones ran his own overnight jobs and found the same thing. Opus 5.5 loves to keep going, so defining the universe and the definition of done up front keeps it from burning tokens modeling worlds you never asked for.

The larger story is that AI is building AI. Anthropic says that since May, Claude has authored more than 80 percent of the code being merged into its own codebase, and OpenAI describes researchers using agents to write code and run experiments. Jones connects the dots: users report what makes a model hard to work with, and the people fixing it now have AI tools that let them investigate and ship fixes fast. Major releases are arriving roughly every 18 days. The consumer feedback loop has become the product roadmap.

His practical advice is to build a real assignment test. When a new model drops, rerun a task you have run before with the same starting point and the same prompt, track total tokens and weekly usage against API pricing, and keep the failures in your notes too. That is how he decided to move a 90-some element design project to Opus 5.5. The test, not the leaderboard, told him where the work should go.

Open questions

  1. Does the task-efficiency gain hold evenly across task types, or is it concentrated in visual and coding work while research or reasoning workloads lag behind?
  2. If the labs keep using AI to accelerate their own development, can release cycles compress much further before users lose the ability to test each new model on their real work?
  3. Can cost per task ever become a shared benchmark, or is it inherently personal, since no two users' tasks, correction styles, and subscription plans are alike?
