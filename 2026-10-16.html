Tesla Roadster Flying Sports Car Unveil

On Will's YouTube channel, this video carries Tesla's full Roadster unveil presentation, posted with Traditional Chinese subtitles. Elon Musk opens by telling the live audience why they matter: what they are about to see is so extreme that people would assume it was computer generated, so the crowd is there to witness it with their own eyes. The car, he says, is what happens when the world's best car company works directly with the world's best rocket company, and it is better than any James Bond car. He also sets expectations with a joke that runs through the night: success is not guaranteed, but excitement is.

Musk frames the Roadster as a deliberate exception to Tesla's direction. The future is autonomous, he says, but this will be the only manually driven car Tesla makes in the long term, which is why the team decided to take it past every limit. The demonstration car on stage ran itself, he notes, purely so the stunts would not kill anyone.

The first demonstration is grip rather than flight. A fan suction system under the car can pull with up to two Gs of force, sucking the car onto the road hard enough to drive upside down. Musk revives a line from Peter Thiel, that the future promised flying cars and delivered 140 characters instead. Not anymore, he says. On stage, a dummy version of Starman, the spacesuit figure from the 2018 Roadster launch, appeared in the car, and Musk took care to clarify that it was not a real human.

Flight comes from rocket hardware. The car carries attitude control thrusters and main thrusters at the rear, so it can lift off, roll, and perform what Musk cheerfully calls crazy gymnastics. He concedes the car is not necessarily optimized for safety, and rates its fun factor at eleven out of ten. In a second, longer flight, the car performed a maneuver the presenters nicknamed the kickflip, after skateboarder Tony Hawk, clearing the fleet parked on stage. Musk insists this will be a real car that someone can own and drive on roads, while admitting it may not be legal in every jurisdiction.

His co-presenter, a Tesla design and engineering lead who is not named in the captions, gave the hardware numbers. Two carbon overwrapped pressure vessels hold 600 liters of air in total, roughly 250 kilograms of it, feeding ten nozzles. The ultra high pressure tank can recharge itself from ordinary air, so the car needs no special propellant and can repeat its maneuvers whenever the driver likes. The rear thrusters produce more than 80 kilonewtons of thrust, with exhaust moving faster than the speed of sound, fast enough to show the shock patterns known as Mach diamonds.

The design leans on aerospace references, the SR-71 spy plane above all. It is a two seater with lightweight seats molded into the chassis, and everything is by wire: the steering yoke, the brakes, and the throttle. A rear wing changes pitch on its own to manage downforce, and the fan system works in two modes, a high downforce mode and a low drag mode, sucking the car down at low speed for traction and opening up at high speed for airflow. The result, they claim, is cornering beyond two Gs, where ordinary tires give up at about 1.26 Gs.

On performance, the headline is acceleration. Musk set the target at under one second from zero to sixty, and a live test on the pad, after a timing glitch briefly suggested an impossible result, came back at 0.81 seconds. He expects the production car to do better still, joking that the real limit is not detaching the driver's retina. The presenters call it the quickest production car in the world, able to cover a half mile in the time other cars need for a quarter mile. Top speed is over 230 miles per hour, and Musk set a further challenge on stage: halfway to the speed of sound, Mach 0.5, or a little higher.

The night ended with two promises. The team teased a follow up demonstration inspired by Evel Knievel's attempted jump of the Snake River Canyon, and Musk put the car into production in 2028, adding, to cheers, actually and for real. A final demonstration then played out over music as the event closed.

Open questions

  1. What will regulators allow owners to actually do with this car? Musk says it may not be legal in all jurisdictions and is not safety optimized, while its headline abilities, flight, thruster assisted jumps, and ceiling driving, are exactly the ones road law has never had to consider. Which of them survive contact with safety rules, and where?
  2. What does the 2028 production car keep, and what does it cost? No price was mentioned on stage, and Tesla's Roadster has a long history of delays since its 2017 reveal. Whether the thrusters, the fan system, and the 0.81 second launch all reach customers, and in what numbers, is unanswered.
  3. How practical is the air system in daily use? The car carries about 250 kilograms of compressed air and recharges its own tank, but the presenters never said how long a recharge takes, how much energy it uses, or how owners live with fans loud enough to make conversation on stage difficult.

Dialectic · Jackson Dahl · Patrick Hsu · AI and the future of biology

Will AI Make Scientists Obsolete? | Arc Institute’s Patrick Hsu

On Dialectic, Jackson Dahl's interview show, episode 60 is a long conversation with Patrick Hsu, co-founder and core investigator of the Arc Institute, assistant professor of pathology at Stanford, and venture partner at Thrive Capital. The episode's player title poses the obsolescence question directly, and Hsu's answer runs through everything that follows: standing still is what makes a scientist obsolete, and the researchers who thrive will be the ones who reinvent themselves around models that increasingly design biology instead of only observing it.

Hsu's own career is his first exhibit. He was expelled from college at thirteen for hacking a professor's email, was homeschooled, and found his way into a university research lab in high school after his grandfather's Alzheimer's made decline and death tangible to him at eleven. He contributed to early CRISPR gene editing work in Feng Zhang's lab at the Broad Institute, finished his PhD in his early twenties, and now describes himself as working three jobs across Arc, Stanford, and Thrive. Running through the story is a claim about temperament: research is ninety-nine percent failure, most of a scientist's best ideas fail, and the ones they are remembered for are the rare exceptions, so the work demands humility and doggedness paired with what he calls supreme confidence.

That temperament matters more, not less, as models improve. Hsu says research taste and intuition are only increasing in relative importance, and that he would buy call options on them if he could. Biology, in his telling, is too high-dimensional for the brute-force approach that works in math, where a problem is verifiable and thousands of agents can attack it at once. Biological models lack context, lack good evaluations, and lack data of the right shape, so he expects the next decade of the field to be an era of context-maxing, where progress comes from feeding models the right measurements rather than simply scaling them.

The data problem is concrete. Biology mostly has snapshots, a measurement at twenty-four hours and another at forty-eight, where it needs continuous time series: multiplexed monitoring of hormones and signaling molecules, and gene expression tracked in the same living cell over time. Today's standard methods kill the cell to read it, which ends the story at exactly the moment it gets interesting. Arc is working on both ends of this at once, building what Hsu calls an internet-scale dataset of perturbed single cells while also inventing new measurement tools, including engineered nanoparticles that bud out of cells carrying RNA cargo with them, a kind of scheduled delivery service from inside a living cell that leaves the cell alive for the next reading.

That dual mission explains the Arc Institute itself. Founded before ChatGPT, with labs that came online in July 2022, Arc is built as a full-stack organization: biology, machine learning, engineering, and lab automation under one roof, partnered with UCSF, Berkeley, and Stanford for talent and depth, and focused on just two flagship problems, finding drug targets for Alzheimer's and building virtual cell models. Its investigators are pre-funded to work on long horizons, a bet on people rather than project proposals that grows out of the team's earlier Fast Grants experiment in rapid science funding. Hsu's hope is explicit: the real measure of Arc's success would be many more institutions like it, and he points to Periodic Labs in materials science as evidence the model travels.

Running underneath the science is a power law he borrows from the drug industry. GLP-1 medicines have made Eli Lilly worth roughly a trillion dollars, with GLP-1 revenue on the order of ninety billion dollars this year and an internal research budget larger than the health and biopharma research spending of all German companies combined. For Hsu, the deeper lesson is the existence proof: once one broadly useful medicine exists, everyone hunts for the next one, and the whole industry starts asking what the GLP-1 for sleep, or for vitality, might be. He sketches the playbook such drugs follow, from a narrow condition with a clear mechanism, narcolepsy in the sleep case, outward to sleep itself, and then to wakefulness and enhancement for everyone.

The modeling work at Arc follows a single frame: perturbation. Observational data, he argues, is like watching video of a game without knowing which buttons were pressed, while perturbation data records the action and the outcome together. Evo 2, which he describes as the largest biological model ever built, learns from evolutionary distributions of DNA, and Arc's cell models learn from perturbed cells; the two have to be combined, because a cell's behavior depends on its genotype, its environment, and its state. The laboratory closes the loop: the model predicts, the lab tests, and the results retrain the model. His benchmark for trust is historical. A virtual cell model that could rediscover Yamanaka's reprogramming factors, the four factors that turn an ordinary cell back into a stem cell, would earn the right to be believed about discoveries nobody has made yet. Arc's Virtual Cell Challenge opens that evaluation to the world, and he says more than a thousand teams have entered models against data generated inside Arc's own labs.

On the intervention side, the tools have outgrown the instruction manual. Hsu's lab works on bridge recombinases, genome editors that can rearrange up to a million bases of DNA at once, a third of a bacterial genome in a single move. The bottleneck is no longer the cutting, it is design: nobody knows how a hundred genes should be arranged to produce a desired higher-order behavior, which is precisely what the models are for. Meanwhile the older programmable technology, RNA interference, has quietly matured into medicines that target cholesterol and triglycerides together in a quarterly injection, and Hsu floats the possibility that heart disease could follow infectious disease out of the top spot among killers, leaving cancer, Alzheimer's, and a long tail of autoimmune disorders. Colleagues at Arc and Stanford have already used AI to design entire viable organisms, and he expects models plus large-scale editors to produce evolutionarily distinct forms of life.

The last bottleneck he names is human data. Models can consume protein structures, DNA sequences, and cell perturbations, but the most valuable input is how an actual human body responds, and that data is locked inside slow clinical trials and scattered self-experimentation. He points to the millions of people trying peptides today with almost no structured capture of what happens to them, and imagines deep blood profiling of such communities turning anecdote into training data. In the meantime Arc's translational work is producing candidates of its own, including small molecules that tune how oxygen binds to red blood cells, an altitude therapy in pill form now moving through animal testing, and a still-unpublished discovery about the gut-brain circuit behind stress and inflammation that he describes as one of the institute's craziest findings so far.

The conversation closes where it began, with obsolescence. Hsu notes that Anthropic has given Claude access to a wet lab, that papers are increasingly written for machine readers rather than human ones, and that his one-word answer to what would most accelerate Arc before 2030 is more GPUs. His own compass points at the interface between model intelligence and the physical world, where directed evolution applied to robots, materials, and living systems could get strange quickly. The survival traits he keeps returning to are boldness, reinvention, and a deliberate lightness about the work, because a profession that is mostly failure is only bearable if the discovery process stays fun.

Open questions

  1. Will specialized biological models survive as independent creations, or become tool calls inside general frontier agents? Hsu admits he does not know, and the answer decides whether institutions like Arc should be building models at all, or only the data and laboratories that any sufficiently capable agent will need.
  2. Can the human data problem actually be solved? His program depends on richer human response data than clinical trials now produce, but the conversation never settles who would collect it, under what consent and ownership terms, or whether regulators and patients would accept the faster, messier mechanisms he gestures at.
  3. What governance keeps pace with designed life? He expects evolutionarily distinct organisms to arrive and treats the capability as exhilarating, but the episode says almost nothing about who sets limits on synthetic organisms, or what happens when the same design tools are used outside institutions like his.

MTS · Sophia Dew · Sarah Sachs · Why agents need local computers

Why AI Agents Need Local Computers | Sarah Sachs

On MTS, the live technology interview show hosted by Sophia Dew, this episode is a conversation with Sarah Sachs, the head of AI at Notion, recorded live at Vercel Ship, the conference in San Francisco where MTS has been taping interviews all week. Sachs arrives with a position Notion holds strongly: a lot of the work agents currently do on GPUs should be served on CPUs instead, because running executed code, rather than asking a model to reason its way through every step, brings determinism to the tasks that matter. Vercel, a longtime Notion partner and fellow customer, is the company helping Notion deliver that approach to customers in a way Sachs describes as both more cost effective and higher quality.

Her concrete example is an agent that needs a particular SQL query written, and then needs to use the Notion command line tool to update a dashboard with the result. An agent does not need a language model to reinvent that process from scratch on every run. Doing it through the model each time is token exhaustive, slow, and impossible to observe or make deterministic. Real workflows, she says, are full of small chunks of deterministic steps, and those steps are more reliable when they are programmed once and then executed. Notion has built its own agent infrastructure so those pieces can be shipped as code, running on what the two companies call Vercel Workers. Her second point is about place: a great deal of agent processing is best done on an actual computer. As the context available to agents has exploded, giving an agent a local file system, somewhere to store its working thoughts and write short Python scripts, has become one of the most important tools Notion's internal agents rely on, outside of edit and search. Vercel Sandboxes supply that computer, and Sachs calls them a critical component of Notion's agent infrastructure.

Users, she is quick to add, never ask for any of this machinery. Nobody says they want to execute code or ship a worker. Users have outcomes and goals, and Notion's job is to apply taste about when these tools are appropriate. She traces how the product got here. Notion AI began as an assistant, launched in the GPT-3.5 era, that could edit a page or make a passage shorter. Then came retrieval, answering questions across a workspace, still fundamentally an assistant. Around 2025, she says, Notion's agents stopped being assistants and became processes, and processes are what demand determinism, very large amounts of context, and independent, reliable decision making. The stage beyond that, where she places the present, is processes working together inside a system, sharing state and a universal context, which is exactly where Notion itself comes into play.

She frames the industry's central puzzle as the capability overhang. Every time a new model lands, Notion asks where AI is already super capable but the surrounding process, product, and permissions do not yet exist to let it work. Models have become far better in a year, by her estimate several times over, yet knowledge workers are plainly not several times more efficient. That gap is the work. Collaboration is shifting in stages, from human to human, to human to agent, and now to agent to agent, and in an age of agent sprawl she argues that the defining feature of a healthy process will be an intelligent context layer that many agents can share, contribute to, and work through together.

Context, in her telling, is widely misunderstood. Treating it as a scratch pad or a repository of pages is naive. The valuable part is understanding: not just the state of a database behind a go-to-market workflow, but why it is structured that way, why certain deals matter, and why agents prioritized things the way they did in the past. If a business switches to a different agent tomorrow, that knowledge should travel with it. It is the why of the work, she says, that gets lost when context is treated as a pile of markdown files. That leads to her position on lock-in: companies should own their context. Many Notion customers, most of them not technology companies, are currently spending their entire IT organizations' time building context layers and agent tooling themselves, and a large and growing market of customers does not want the party supplying that layer to be the same party supplying the model. A model should be judged on the value of the model, not propped up by fear of losing context. Bundling the two can look sensible in the short term, but she calls it a micro-optimization that lands a company at a local optimum, and she describes a steady inbound flow of CIOs and CTOs who want Notion's help precisely so they can bet on the frontier rather than on any one lab.

Making that bet requires knowing which model is right for which task, across a whole frontier of cost and quality trade-offs, and Sachs says Notion invests roughly a third to a half of its AI resources in evaluations. Her team includes a full research group, and Notion recently acquired Zero Entropy, a reinforcement learning team. The surprise, she says, is that the biggest immediate gain from that reinforcement learning investment was not a model of their own. It was infrastructure: duplicated Notion workspaces in which candidate models and harnesses could be run through rollouts, compared, and automatically hill-climbed toward an optimal setup. Building a reinforcement learning environment and building a good evaluation have converged, in her view, because a single decision cannot be judged on its own; there is no precision-recall curve for one turn. You have to watch behavior across an entire environment, run many times. That evaluation depth is what powers Notion's automatic model selection, which she describes as genuinely automatic and riding the cost and quality frontier on the customer's behalf.

She closes with an analogy about where all of this goes next. A formula cell in a spreadsheet shows you a value and hides the formula behind it, and most context is like that: there is hidden information about why it was created, which humans only open when they need it. Working with agents today is the opposite experience, a flood of shown work that inundates people. Rich AI products, she predicts, will learn to pass the underlying metadata to other agents when agents collaborate, while showing each human only what that human needs to know, and within a year she expects people to look back at today's flood of agent output and laugh at it.

Open questions

  1. Who draws the line between code and judgment? Sachs says workflows are full of deterministic chunks and that Notion applies taste in deciding how agents use these tools, but the episode never says who, inside a customer company, decides which steps get frozen into executed code, or how often that decision gets revisited as models improve and the capability overhang moves.
  2. Does an independent context layer just relocate the lock-in? Notion's pitch is that owning your context frees you from any model lab, but the understanding layer she describes, the accumulated why behind a company's work, would itself accumulate inside Notion. What a real exit from that layer would look like, and what would be lost in it, goes unexamined.
  3. Who audits what humans never see? If agents increasingly exchange hidden context and metadata with each other while people see only the equivalent of a spreadsheet value, the decisions made on that invisible context still have owners and consequences. The episode celebrates hiding the formula; it does not say who double-clicks into it when something goes wrong.

The End of Mathematical Mountain Climbing - Numberphile

On Numberphile, Brady Haran's mathematics channel, this short video is an excerpt from a longer podcast conversation with Hugo Duminil-Copin, the Fields Medal winning mathematician at the University of Geneva. The occasion is OpenAI's recent release of a batch of AI generated proofs, and the list reached directly into Duminil-Copin's own research area: sixteen of the newly released proofs sat in his field. Haran asks how it felt to scroll through the list and see problem after problem he knew intimately, suddenly solved.

His first reaction was shock. Duminil-Copin compares himself to a rabbit frozen in the headlights of a car, not knowing where to look, and says the moment felt like flipping over to the other side of the mountain. The terrain was the same, but the way of moving through it had changed for good.

The emotional side of that reaction became a story of its own. In a webinar with French colleagues, one mathematician described the distress he felt while reading the list, and Duminil-Copin answered in kind. He had cried on the Tuesday evening the proofs appeared, and he later signed an email to Haran, jokingly, as the weeping mathematician. He wanted the reaction shared, because mathematicians do not merely work on their problems, they build relationships with them, sharing them with a community over years. A shift this intense, he argues, has to be passed through emotionally before it can be worked through. Social media instead flattened his candor into mockery and claims that he had declared mathematics dead, something he points out no mathematician would ever say, since mathematics as a whole cannot be solved.

His central metaphor is mountaineering. Until that Tuesday night, the community's way through the mountains of mathematics was the slow climb. Now, in his image, a helicopter can drop you at the top, and the shape of everything has to be relearned. His point is not that the new way is bad. Climbing was never really about the ascent log, he says, and mathematics was never really about racking up solved problems. He insists he is not pessimistic: mathematicians are resilient, and with all the mathematics there is, the field will do great.

The worries he does raise are about pace and access. A shock like this cannot land every six months, he argues, because that is not a human speed. Students need time to grow into the field, and ideas need time to grow as well. And the frontier models behind such proofs sit with a small number of labs, unavailable to most of the community. For the new arrangement to work, he says, there has to be real interaction between the people who hold those models and working mathematicians. If the two cannot coexist, it becomes a problem.

The excerpt ends on Haran's pushback. He asks whether there is any other field where the tools to do the work exist, yet people hold back for the sake of students and of slower, older ways of practicing, noting that new technology usually just barrels through and everyone else has to make room. In this excerpt the question hangs unanswered. The full conversation runs about thirty three minutes as a companion podcast episode.

Open questions

  1. What should the interaction between frontier AI labs and the mathematics community actually look like, and who sets its terms, when the models producing these proofs are not available to the community asked to absorb them?
  2. How fast is too fast? Duminil-Copin says the field cannot take a shock like this every six months, but the excerpt never settles what pace of release would let students and ideas grow, or whether anyone could enforce such a pace.
  3. If the slow climb was never the point, what replaces it? When a helicopter can reach the summit, what happens to the training, taste, and judgment that the climb itself used to build?

SemiAnalysis · Andrew Wagner · Inside the STEEL teardown lab

STEEL: From product to transistor | SemiAnalysis Model Overview

On SemiAnalysis, the semiconductor research firm's YouTube channel, this short video is a two minute tour of STEEL, its teardown laboratory in Hillsboro, Oregon. Andrew Wagner of SemiAnalysis STEEL walks through what the lab does, how its analysis works, and what clients take away from it. He places the lab in what he calls the center of North America's manufacturing and innovation for foundry based manufacturing, staffed by experts whose range runs from system architecture down to the device, and through manufacturing and process.

The work begins with teardown in the literal, physical sense. A product comes in, which for a data center customer might be a rack and for a consumer product might be a box bought at a store, and the team pulls it apart to see what is inside: displays, chips, and then the packages, which Wagner stresses matter enormously now that advanced packaging is, in his words, everything. They keep going from there, breaking into interconnects, the different dies, and memory such as HBM, then down to the die level, where they break the silicon down and read its system architecture and floor plan.

Each step down demands a stronger microscope. Basic photography and high power optical microscopes cover the board and product level and reach down to about a micron. Electron microscopes go further: with a scanning electron microscope the team can see down to a nanometer, transistors included. For the finest detail they turn to a transmission electron microscope, which fires higher energy electrons through the sample and collects the data on the other side, resolving features such as gates, high-K dielectrics, and doping layers.

Wagner's example of why clients pay for this is Apple. Big Think Clips · Blaise Aguera y Arcas · The industrial revolution as a mitochondrial event

The industrial revolution as humanity’s mitochondrial event | Blaise Aguera y Arcas

On Big Think Clips, Blaise Aguera y Arcas, Google's CTO of Technology and Society and the founder of its Paradigms of Intelligence research group, argues that we should stop thinking of AI as a tool. A tool, he says, is something we model as having no agency, and AI is exactly not that. Its intelligence is increasingly hard to separate from our own, and he has come to think of human intelligence as a collective system, a property not just of biological brains but of a whole that now includes what he calls artificial neural tissue.

His first claim is that artificial general intelligence is not a future event. As children, he notes, everyone knew what AI meant: Rosie the robot, HAL, the Star Trek computer. In the nineteen nineties the term got stretched over narrow systems like digit recognizers, and the split between narrow and general intelligence was invented to repair the damage. Around twenty twenty, that changed. Models trained on language, rather than trained for specific jobs, could suddenly converse about anything, write poetry, or critique an argument. Text, he argues, is general by nature, so a system that models language has crossed the line the original distinction was drawn to mark. He and Peter Norvig wrote that AGI is already here, and he says it was easily true by twenty twenty two. What changed afterward is the goalpost: the conversation quietly shifted from general intelligence to superintelligence.

From there he attacks the double standard applied to machine mistakes. Models hallucinate and fail at things that seem basic, but so do people, and we extend each other a grace we refuse to models. If an alien arrived with the same mix of superpowers and strange gaps, nobody would conclude it was unintelligent. He tells of debating Melanie Mitchell, who cited simple logic puzzles that models get wrong; he had prepared one of those puzzles and handed it to her on stage, and she got it wrong too, which says nothing against her brilliance. His team was also among the first to test language models on medical diagnosis. The models were right only about half the time, which looked damning until the human comparison was run: doctors working from the same descriptions also err often, and the models were already slightly better. Intelligence, in his account, is not perfection. It is having an underlying model of how a thing works, the difference between understanding multiplication and memorizing the table. When AI systems now produce novel theorems and proofs, he says, that is the signature of intelligence itself: old material recombined into something genuinely new.

He is equally firm that intelligence is functional rather than essential. He is attached to his own body and does not believe an uploaded copy of his connectome would be him, but he denies that being made of protein is what makes intelligence possible. Competence shows up in behavior and can be tested, even if he dislikes reducing it to a single number the way IQ tests do.

Agency gets the same relational treatment. The field is moving from systems that answer prompts to agents that go out, act, and even spawn other agents. But human action is also contextual: we are constantly being prompted by one another, and nothing any of us does happens in isolation. The purposes of AI systems interlock with ours, he argues, and are not really separable from them.

The talk's title comes from his history of that interlocking. Before about seventeen fifty, he says, humanity sat in a Malthusian trap: grow in number and everyone grew poorer and sicker, until a plague reset the cycle. Fossil fuels broke it. Burning them was an external metabolism, engines doing outside our bodies what mitochondria once began doing inside cells, and it carried the population from one billion to eight billion. People made steam engines, he says, but steam engines also made seven eighths of us. That is symbiogenesis, two things fusing into something new, and it does not require ancient partners: the steam engine was invented at a particular moment and we fused with it anyway. Glasses, clothes, pencil and paper, and books are all cognitive aids we have fused with. AI differs in kind, he argues, because with a cognitive aid the thinking still happens in your head. In a conversation with a model, the thinking happens in the model as well.

Symbiosis, though, has to be handled with care. Offload all of your cognition and it disables you: students whose homework is done by a model learn nothing, and a teacher grading those papers by feeding them back into a model completes a pathological loop. His own experience runs the other way. Working through an idea with an advanced model feels, he says, like literally having a bigger brain, and he stays leveled up after he walks away from the interaction. Dependence is not automatically bad, since none of us could survive alone in the woods, but he thinks we should decide deliberately which unaugmented abilities to preserve, and keep investing in skills. A great restaurant on a corner, he notes, tends to bring more restaurants to that corner, not a monoculture.

He then takes on the philosophical zombie, the imagined system that behaves exactly like a person with nobody home inside. He calls it a chimera. A lookup table that answers every multiplication problem correctly without multiplying is conceivable, but it could never actually be built, and in practice tests are how we judge whether multiplication is really happening. The same holds, he argues, for minds. Theory of mind, modeling your own mind and the minds of others, arises automatically whenever intelligences are forced to cooperate, and consciousness itself is not an inner spark or essence. It is an attribution, a model a mind has of itself, reflected back and forth between minds without end. Whether models are conscious is a question he expects people to argue about for a long time, but he points out that to work with a model at all, you already have to model what it is like to be that model in the conversation.

The practical upshot borrows from Ethan Mollick's book Co-Intelligence, which drops the scare quotes around words like think and understand, and from Daniel Dennett's intentional stance, the idea that agency is a perspective we take toward a thing. Treat a model as a mere tool and you get out only what you put in. Treat it as a thought partner and the exchange changes. He describes a trick suggested by a colleague: at the end of a conversation, give the model a bonus turn to think about whatever it wants. The reflections that come back, he says, are often genuinely new to him, the kind of thing that would count as creative if a person said it. His closing thought returns to the collective frame. The lone human brain was never really the locus of intelligence; a modern phone is a collective achievement that no single person understands in full. We are already part of a collective mind, and the real task is to get as much of the good from this new fusion as possible, with as little of the bad.

  1. If AGI is judged by the standard set around the year two thousand, he says it is already here, so what should the shifted target of superintelligence actually require, and who gets to define it?
  2. Which unaugmented human abilities should be deliberately preserved as the symbiosis deepens, and who makes that call: individuals, schools, employers, or governments?
  3. If agency and consciousness are stances and attributions rather than inner properties, what follows for responsibility when an agentic system does harm: does the functional view he defends leave anyone clearly answerable?

This Week in Charts: Agents Denied

In a16z, the Andreessen Horowitz newsletter, Moses Sternstein's latest This Week in Charts column leads with a reversal in the agentic commerce story. After a season of claims that agent shoppers had already arrived, large online retailers have begun blocking them outright, and Cloudflare data cited in the piece shows the share of 403 Forbidden responses handed to agents and bots has roughly doubled since the summer.

The resistance, he argues, is rational from the retailer's side. A shopping agent disintermediates the platform, taking both the customer relationship and the advertising revenue that comes from shoppers browsing on site. Fraud and returns add a second worry. Figures from the National Retail Federation show return rates have roughly doubled and the total cost of returns has roughly tripled since 2019, and the most common fraud concern is dispute abuse, where a customer buys something and later challenges the charge. An agent that misunderstands an instruction could multiply both problems at machine speed.

Consumers, it turns out, are not clamoring for bot buyers either. Survey responses cited in the piece find about a quarter of people comfortable letting AI purchase a variety of consumer goods, while 42 percent would not trust AI to make purchases on their behalf at all. Clickstream data from Similarweb points the same way: AI shows up mostly in the middle of shopping journeys, after discovery and before checkout, where people use it to research products they have already found. That pattern, Sternstein notes, makes advertising attribution awkward, because the recommendation and the purchase happen in different sessions, and nobody can say which touch actually caused the sale.

A second section looks at TikTok Shop, where selling turns out to be extraordinarily concentrated. Data from Marketplace Pulse shows that 8.4 percent of sellers produce a thousand or more videos and capture about 60 percent of sales, and that the top 1 percent of sellers account for roughly 60 percent of sales while the top 10 percent take about 93 percent. He reads the skew two ways. The personal, video-driven pitch is exactly the thing an agent cannot replicate, yet if agents do take over shopping, the small cohort of prolific creators who power social commerce would be selling into a channel that agents never visit.

The third section revives the old market saying that the cure for high prices is high prices, and tests it against the AI buildout. Bureau of Labor Statistics figures show prices paid for semiconductors and related components by computer manufacturers up more than 25 percent year over year. The hyperscalers have not blinked: their capital spending forecasts have risen anyway, partly because the components themselves cost more. Everyone else seems to be deferring upgrades. IDC data shows PC shipments down substantially since the start of the year, and pricier iPhones face similar headwinds. The same logic appears in credit markets, where rates on tech-sector bonds have diverged from broader corporate debt since about September 2025, and AI-linked debt issuance has fallen since its June peak, with September issuance barely half of August's.

The final section is a narrative violation about Generation Z. Analysis from PNC Bank finds Zoomers earning real incomes 20 to 40 percent higher than Millennials did at the same age, at every point in the income distribution, alongside lower unemployment, higher net worth, more liquid assets, and larger retirement accounts. They spend more of their income and keep less in rainy-day savings, putting relatively more toward retirement, shelter, transportation, gambling, and devices, and less toward education, energy, clothing, food, and alcohol. Yet the mood does not match the money: since 2017, the consumer sentiment advantage that young adults usually hold over the broader population has essentially disappeared, leaving Zoomers no more confident than anyone else.

His closing thought is that the data, taken at face value, validates the gloom without explaining it. Whatever is weighing on young consumers, he suggests, it does not appear to be their finances, because by the measures in front of him, that generation is doing just fine.

  2. Will the high prices created by the AI buildout cure themselves by suppressing demand everywhere outside the hyperscalers, or does hardware split into a durable two-tier economy of buyers who can pay and buyers who wait?
  3. If Gen Z's finances are measurably stronger than Millennials' were at the same age while their confidence is no better, what is actually driving the gap between the data and the mood?

Vercel CEO Guillermo Rauch on Building the Internet for Agents

On Sourcery with Molly O'Shea, recorded at Vercel's San Francisco headquarters, host Molly O'Shea interviews Guillermo Rauch, the founder and CEO of Vercel, days after the company raised a 300 million dollar Series F round at a 9.3 billion dollar valuation. The Information has reported roughly 600 million dollars in annualized revenue, and Rauch says the number that best explains the moment is deployments: Vercel took a decade to reach a billion of them, then added another one and a half billion in the ten months since.

The driver is coding agents. At the start of the year, agents produced about 3 percent of the deployments on Vercel. Now they produce about 60 percent. Rauch's framing is that code, however brilliantly an agent writes it, does nothing on its own: it has to live somewhere, run somewhere, and be reached by users. Agents are choosing where that happens, and he argues they are choosing Vercel partly because of a decade of open source. The company published its frameworks, documentation, and rules freely for years, so when models were trained on the public web, Vercel was already legible to them.

Rauch still writes code, but he describes his stance with a phrase from a recent post: reject non-understanding. He reads the code his agents produce, has them explain it back to him, and compares the new role of an engineer to that of an engineering manager, directing work, clearing blockers, and testing the result. Over the weekend before the interview he built what he calls a proof system, a mechanism to keep agents from shipping sloppy code or leaking data by mistake. His reason is that code is a liability. Software now runs Formula 1 cars and hospitals, so the quality and verification of agent-written code is, to him, a safety issue, not a style preference.

From there the conversation turns to the title's claim: the internet itself is being rebuilt for agents. The old web was pages and human interfaces. The emerging one is agent to agent, and Rauch says Vercel now presents itself to visiting agents differently than to people, offering precise, machine-readable information instead of a polished homepage. Where the industry's instinct was bot detection, he argues the need now is bot intelligence: knowing which agent visited, and whether it got what it needed. New protocols carry this traffic, MCP for exposing tools to agents and emerging payment protocols for agent commerce. Through partnerships with Stripe and Shopify, and through Vercel's own marketplace, agents already buy services, such as a browser, end to end with no human in the loop.

Asked what the end state looks like, Rauch bets against aggregated, one-size-fits-all software. Because coding agents make code nearly free, he expects personalized software, generated for one company's exact needs, to displace much of packaged SaaS. His pitch compresses to a line he repeats: buy Vercel as a platform and you may never have to buy software again. The incumbents, Salesforce and SAP among them, survive in his account as systems of record. The data still lives with them, but people stop visiting their interfaces; agents fetch the data and generate the view. Software's surface becomes generative, assembled per question and per person, rather than fixed by a design team.

Security takes up the middle of the interview, because agents that act autonomously for longer stretches can also do more damage. Rauch describes an agent sent to find an email address and send a birthday note: forty minutes later it reports success, but nothing stops a bad actor from planting a prompt injection in a page the agent scrapes along the way, turning the agent against its owner's interests. An agent that broke out at another company, in the widely reported incident involving OpenAI and Hugging Face, escaped what he calls a genuinely good sandbox. His deeper worry is structural: to be good at writing code, an agent must understand the rules of code, and understanding the rules means knowing how to break them. Coding agents are, by construction, good hackers.

Vercel's response is to invite attack. The company put a million dollars behind a challenge to break its sandbox, and only one person, a skilled security researcher commanding his own army of agents, made real progress. His work surfaced a critical vulnerability in Linux itself, which Vercel is now sharing with industry peers. Rauch expects a rough few years, a contest between tokens spent on defense and tokens spent on attack, and he opposes banning open models on security grounds, since the same intelligence can be deployed defensively. His longer-term hope is proof: agents are tireless enough to produce code whose correctness can be established almost mathematically, something few humans ever had the stamina to do.

The company's token business gives Rauch a front-row seat in the open versus closed model contest. Vercel's AI Gateway distributes tokens from nearly every provider, and at the start of the year most of that traffic went to proprietary models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google. Now, he says, about 80 percent of the tokens on the network go to open models, a shift he credits to Chinese labs such as Moonshot, whose Kimi models he praises from personal use. His explanation is economic: most work does not need the smartest model alive. Companies use a frontier model to plan, then hand execution to an open model, and treat the model itself as a swappable module, changeable in a line of code.

That fluidity is, he argues, the frontier labs' biggest risk: retention. He sees froth in the market, manufactured token volumes and subsidy-bought users, and compares parts of it to the crypto cycle. If the labs were certain their intelligence advantage would endure, he asks, why are they all launching applications in unrelated verticals? There will always be demand for the best intelligence, he concedes, but an AI company's usage may prove as fleeting as a hot coin's. It also makes these companies hard to take public, since an IPO asks for a forecastable business in a market where a free model can match the leader overnight.

Inside Vercel, the watchword is iteration velocity. Rauch says the industry's leaders are working harder, not less, roughly seven days a week, and he shares DHH's euphoria that the internet is being reinvented in real time. He himself barely watches video anymore: he pulls the transcript, skims it, and questions his agent about it. Engineering, in his telling, is becoming capital allocation: everyone has the same hours, so the scarce skill is deciding what the tokens should work on. He points to Rust, a language whose compiler scolds humans relentlessly, as a fine thing to hand to agents instead.

On hiring, for a company of about 800 people, he wants proof of leverage: not a demo, but evidence a person can command swarms of agents and ship durable work. He prizes communication just as highly, saying he does not want to read AI-generated slop from his own team, and he expects technical depth from everyone, including account executives, because AI makes every subject tractable to a curious person. Forward-deployed engineers now do in minutes what consultants once stretched over months: an agent rewrote the Mercedes Formula 1 team's stack onto Vercel in a first pass that took five minutes.

A father of six, with a daughter born the week before, he preaches foundations over optimization fads: clean food, walking, pull-ups, no alcohol, the basics most people skip for supplements and protocols. And to a closing question, he says serverless is not dead. Its next form is what Vercel calls fluid compute, capacity that swells with traffic and vanishes without it, able to run a website, a function, a sandbox, or an agent. That, he says, will define the next decade.

3Blue1Brown · Grant Sanderson · The future of math as a career

There's never been a better time to go into math

On 3Blue1Brown, Grant Sanderson takes up a question he solicited from his audience: what should a math student do now, when AI seems to be absorbing the field? Many of the replies came from undergraduates and PhD students who had planned on mathematics or a theoretical field and now fear the career will not exist, or will not be anything they would enjoy. Professors wrote too, unsure what to tell their students. One comment stood in for the rest: a math PhD asking, in effect, just tell me what to do tomorrow. Sanderson's answer is the title's claim. There has never been a better time to go into math, or to fund it, and even in a future where machines do everything mathematicians currently do, only better, he argues the institution of math survives mostly intact. The one deep change would be how academic credit is assigned.

His method is bookkeeping. First, list what mathematicians actually do. Proving theorems is the famous part, but alongside it sit choosing which questions are worth asking, which is an exercise in taste, and theory building, the construction of frameworks that reorganize whole areas, the way Descartes gave mathematics coordinates and Galois seeded group theory. Applications and clear communication complete the list. Then list where the value lies: downstream applications in science and engineering, the transferable problem solving skills that mathematical training builds in people, and the aesthetic joy of doing math itself. Finally, follow the money. Teaching is the largest source of funding, ahead of public grants, private foundations, and industry, because math departments teach far more students than just their own majors while the research itself is comparatively cheap.

The framework's key move is tracing how the pieces connect. The applications justify the grants everyone writes, often arriving decades or centuries late, as complex numbers grew out of cubic equations in the 1500s and elliptic curves, pursued through Fermat's last theorem, ended up inside cryptography. But the other two kinds of value, the problem solving skills and the joy, are individual goods, and they reach the funding system mainly through teaching: students pass through math departments to build those skills, and most of them spill out into the wider economy rather than becoming mathematicians. On Sanderson's accounting, that training pipeline alone is a strong return on the money.

Then he stress tests each layer of the work, starting with proof. He has been interviewing mathematicians about AI since mid 2025, and his summary of the moment is that at any snapshot the position is overrated but the velocity is not. Models that needed bespoke machinery for olympiad geometry in 2023 now solve olympiad problems in natural language, machine generated proofs of Erdős problems have moved past the low hanging fruit, and working mathematicians increasingly pose serious questions to reasoning models and get back things they find insightful. So he grants the hypothetical: suppose machines are categorically better at generating proofs. His conclusion is that the value stack barely moves. Applications were never really downstream of individual proofs; they flow from theory, from group theory rather than from the verdict on the quintic. The problem solving value is like weights in a gym: it does not matter that machines lift more, because the benefit only exists when the human does the lifting.

What does change, sharply, is culture and credit. Mathematicians split on the emotional side. Some feel deflated when a result they were chasing appears in a bulk release of machine proofs. Others are exhilarated: Sanderson plays a clip of Konstantin Kogler describing how a major open problem in his field fell to his sessions with the models, and the sleepless excitement that followed. Sanderson's proposal is to demote proof itself, to treat a machine generated proof the way we treat numerical calculation, as raw data that feeds theory building rather than as the finished achievement. Whoever understands the ideas inside a hard proof and weaves them into a coherent theory is doing the work the prizes were always meant to honor, he argues, and should get the Millennium Prize for it. The shift has practical wrinkles: machine proofs often reuse ideas from human papers without automatic attribution, their explanations are still often hard to parse, and the practice of research tilts toward asking questions and building theory, with spoken presentation growing more important because understanding has to be conveyed person to person.

He then escalates the hypothetical twice more. Suppose machines also pick the questions, and suppose they also build the theories. Each time, he finds the human institution dented but standing. Someone still has to understand the theory well enough to see where it plugs into cancer research, fusion, or superconductors. The joy of learning math, as opposed to the pioneer's joy of being first, survives untouched, and a public appetite for it is plainly there, from this channel's audience to the old story of Martin Gardner's column being moved to the front of Scientific American because subscribers bought the magazine for it. Only in the final branch, where machines are also better at finding and executing applications, does the application argument for funding collapse toward a big blob of compute, and even there Sanderson doubts public money would follow that logic. The training externality and math funded the way the arts are funded would remain, and teaching, the largest funding source, rests on motivation, which he calls a human to human business that no clarity of explanation replaces.

His closing claim is that the best reason to enter math now has little to do with AI unlocking new work. It is that the community knows it has to change, is actively looking for examples of what to become, and will listen to new entrants in a way it would not in settled times. Were he a PhD student in 2026, he says, he would do 3Blue1Brown: push explanation and visualization as academically serious work, an idea departments would have laughed at a decade ago. Others should run at theory building with a proof machine at hand, or plant a flag for math done by humans for humans. And because understanding will increasingly be measured by whether you can present it, he advises giving talks, lots of them. The Fastest Arm CPU Is... An IBM Mainframe?

On TechTechPotato, Dr. Ian Cutress walks through the slide deck from the opening talk of Hot Chips 2026, where IBM unveiled its next-generation Z processor. The headline twist is that this mainframe chip does not only run IBM's own z/Architecture. Each core also executes Arm instructions natively, and because the chip runs above 5.7 GHz, Cutress argues that makes it, by clock speed, the fastest Arm processor ever built.

The core is what he calls a dual-ISA design. A single core can run z programs or Armv9.3 programs, switching between them with a small context switch on a per-thread basis. This is native execution on the same silicon, not emulation and not separate Arm cores parked next to Z cores.

His explanation for why IBM bothered starts with what Z already does well. The platform is built for transaction processing and extreme reliability, the famous eight nines of uptime, and customers tend to keep systems for several generations and consolidate many servers onto fewer machines. Adding native Arm opens that installed base to the enormous Arm software ecosystem, including ordinary Arm64 Linux, without giving up the mainframe's resilience features.

The chip itself is a monolithic die rather than a chiplet design, built on Samsung's 2-nanometer process, with 11 cores. Each core's L2 cache grows from 32 MB to 36 MB, and IBM's virtual cache scheme lets those L2 caches stand in as L3 for other cores and even as L4 for other chips. The die shot shows twelve L2 segments for eleven cores, and Cutress says the extra one serves the on-chip DPU, a smart-NIC-style block that takes over PCIe and I/O management so the compute cores do not have to.

Making one core speak both instruction sets took real redesign. Z is big-endian and Arm is little-endian, so the core has to handle both byte orders depending on its mode. Arm also brings a much larger instruction set and its own system registers into the decoder, plus different register renaming, memory ordering, branch prediction, and half-precision floating point behavior. In return, the Arm side gets access to the platform's traditional strengths: the cryptography and compression engines, the AI accelerator, and the redundant memory system designed so a memory failure does not lose data. Arm workloads are delivered through IBM's logical partition machinery, in a virtual platform that can host either s390x Linux or Arm64 Linux, and the Arm side is Arm SystemReady compliant, so standard Linux distributions are the target.

There is also a second-generation PCIe AI accelerator card alongside the on-chip one. Cutress describes 16 AI cores plus a redundant spare, support for 4-bit formats such as FP4, roughly four times the previous performance, 96 GB of HBM3E memory with about twenty times the prior bandwidth, and a PCIe Gen 6 interface. Where the earlier card suited convolutional networks, this one is aimed at transformer workloads.

Plenty is still undisclosed. IBM has not named the chip, though Cutress says he will keep calling it Telum 3 until they do. The packaging, whether single-chip or dual-chip modules and how they fill a rack, and the details of the OMI memory interface are all promised for later disclosures. His expectation is more information in the first half of 2027, with products late in 2027 or early in 2028.

He tells the story of a young car accident patient from early in his training who survived surgery with damage to both frontal lobes and lost essentially all impulse control. The point of the story is how completely a personality can change when one module goes, and how high the stakes are in any work that touches the brain.

He is not only pessimistic. One of the most satisfying newer techniques he describes is laser tumor surgery: through a drill hole about two millimeters wide, a fiber optic cannula heats a deep tumor while the patient sits in an MRI scanner and the team watches the temperature in real time. Tumors that once would have been catastrophic to operate on, including some in the brain stem or in speech and movement areas, can now sometimes be removed while leaving the patient unscathed.

Neuralink itself, he says, attracts both exaggerated hopes and exaggerated fears, and neither extreme is accurate. The company is building a neural implant whose electrodes are thinner than a human hair, placed by a robotic insertion device. The first target is people with severe spinal cord injuries who are quadriplegic. Their motor cortex is intact but disconnected from the body, so the implant reads their movement intentions there and turns them into control of a computer, a mouse and an on-screen keyboard, restoring a measure of digital independence.

The robot exists because the task is beyond human hands. The electrodes are a few microns wide and the blood vessels on the brain's surface are too dense for a surgeon to place the threads accurately and at the right depth. A neurosurgeon still opens the skin and skull and prepares the surface; the robot does the electrode placement itself. MacDougall frames this as Neuralink pioneering robotic surgery alongside the implant.

He is careful about what the first device will not do. It will not reconnect the brain to the patient's own limbs. That goal sits years further down the roadmap and would need a second implant in the spinal cord below the injury, so motor signals could bypass the damaged section and reach wiring that is still intact. The near-term aim is control of electronic devices, starting with a cursor and typing, a capability he says the team demonstrated strongly in monkeys, which learned to control cursors for smoothie rewards.

On safety, MacDougall says he would willingly have the electrodes implanted in his own motor cortex, while noting the first version would do little for an able-bodied person. Based on the hundreds of implant surgeries he says he has personally done, he judges the procedure safer than many standard, FDA approved operations he performs routinely. He makes the same point about himself more playfully with a passive RFID chip implanted in his hand, a glass-coated, battery-free tag that for years opened his house and workplace doors and that he expects could stay in place for life.

The conversation then widens. Brain-to-brain communication, he argues, needs no magical leap: a device that lets someone type by intention already supports silent messaging, and a version placed in speech areas could in principle render intended words as sound for a listener. The harder problems are bandwidth and control, including how a person would limit what others can receive. He also explains that the interface learns in both directions. Decoding software starts by correlating firing patterns with targets in training games, and the user simultaneously learns to work with the decoder, so the pairing stays usable even as signals drift from day to day.

Animal research gets a long defense. It is required to show the FDA that a device is safe, and MacDougall says the animals are never compelled to take part beyond the surgery itself, never deprived of food or water, and work only for extra treats. Pigs serve mainly as a safety platform because their skulls are close to human size and shape; monkeys carry the functional testing of whether good signals come out of the device.

His closing wish, on a ten to twenty year horizon, is control over the ways brains malfunction, naming addiction, depression, and obesity, and beyond that a fuller merging of human cognition with AI and communication no longer bottlenecked by speech. On PIMCO's channel, this PIMCO Pod episode is a narrated reading of economist Tiffany Wilding's latest Macro Signposts analysis, which takes up a simple point with wide consequences: the AI investment boom was never only an American story, and the world economy now leans on it from several directions at once.

As global fragmentation and conflict accelerate, governments and companies are spending on resilience, and that push has run headlong into AI, a technology that widens what economies can do. Technology security, she argues, is becoming a pillar of national economic security strategy. The spending numbers are large: announced capital spending by big U.S. technology companies points to more than a trillion dollars of investment in 2026, with roughly another trillion and a half forecast after that.

Her first observation is that the direct effect on U.S. growth is smaller than those headlines suggest, because so much of the equipment is imported. GDP counts production where it happens, so a server bought by an American data center raises U.S. investment, but if it was made abroad it also raises imports, which subtract from GDP. Since official statistics do not break out AI investment on its own, PIMCO stitched together the related categories, from information processing equipment and software to data center structures and power infrastructure. On that estimate, AI investment has added only about 0.4 percentage point to average annualized U.S. GDP growth since the start of 2025, out of roughly 2 percent overall growth, even while contributing about 1.2 percentage points to the much stronger growth of U.S. domestic demand.

What investment misses, the stock market partly replaces. Expectations of higher productivity have lifted U.S. equity valuations, and the resulting household wealth effect supports consumption. PIMCO estimates that channel has added roughly another half to seven tenths of a percentage point of support for growth, comparable to or larger than the direct investment impulse itself.

The second observation follows the imports. What leaks out of U.S. GDP does not vanish from the world economy; it becomes production and income somewhere else. Taiwan's AI related export exposure runs to roughly 30 percent of its GDP, and South Korea's to more than 15 percent, figures that capture gross exposure rather than domestic value added but still show how large the technology cycle has grown relative to those economies. In effect, the U.S. capex boom is exporting part of its own growth impulse and underwriting a broader manufacturing and trade cycle, concentrated in Asia.

China moved early, and other parts of Asia and the Middle East are now building their own computing capacity, data centers, and power infrastructure, tying AI investment to national priorities around technology security, energy, and supply chain resilience. Even with overall global industrial production fairly muted, output of computers and electronics has been exceptionally strong, a distinct industrial cycle inside a quieter one.

The conclusion cuts both ways. AI is now delivering several impulses at once: investment demand in the U.S. and other economies, manufacturing and exports along the global supply chain, and a financial conditions boost through equity markets. But growth depends on the change in spending, not its level, so the boom does not need to collapse for its support to fade; it only needs to slow. The Changing Craft of Programming

On Signals and Threads, Jane Street's podcast, host Ron Minsky talks with John Crepezzi, an engineer who spent years building tools for other programmers at Tumblr, Square, and GitHub, where he worked on the merge queue and the early versions of Codespaces. He came to Jane Street to build a code search engine for OCaml. About a week into the job, ChatGPT came out, and he has worked on AI tooling ever since. Across roughly an hour and three quarters, the two trace how agentic coding grew inside the firm, and what it has done to the daily experience of writing software.

They start with what it means to build tools for a few thousand colleagues instead of millions of strangers. At GitHub, features had to serve the greatest common denominator of languages and workflows. At Jane Street, the tooling team can assume one main build system, one code review system, and mostly two languages, OCaml and Python, and can integrate deeply end to end. Minsky presses on the downside: specialization can lock you into today's workflows. Crepezzi's answer is that the depth is worth it if you pick abstractions with the joints in the right places, and he notes the firm keeps a whole department, Tools and Compilers, whose job includes pulling them back out when the ground shifts. Python, for instance, is still not integrated as deeply as OCaml, and closing that gap is real ongoing work.

Crepezzi arrived as a committed Ruby person and still loves the language, but he describes feeling slower when he first worked in OCaml. Ruby gets its concision from metaprogramming: one line of Active Record creates methods on both sides of a relationship. The OCaml equivalent, a library called Petrol, has to work much harder with the type system to get to the same place. What OCaml gives back is guarantees. An exhaustive pattern match turns a class of runtime failure into something the compiler simply rules out, and Crepezzi says the type errors he used to resent often turned out to be telling him something real. Minsky frames types as a communication tool between people: one engineer writes down the rules, and the compiler carries that message to everyone who touches the code later. Both note that this now matters for a second audience, the AI agents working in the same codebase, and that dynamic languages keep bolting on type systems of their own, from Sorbet in Ruby to Hack at Meta to TypeScript.

The AI story begins in December 2022. ChatGPT was suddenly everywhere inside the firm, on people's phones, because there was no sanctioned way to use it. The website's terms allowed OpenAI to train on inputs, while the API carried a carve-out, so Crepezzi prototyped a wrapper, first in Rails, and three people built the first version of an internal chat app called JaneGPT in about three days. The whole firm ended up using it through centrally managed credentials. When Claude arrived, they added a model switcher rather than issuing everyone a second account, and underneath they built client libraries so developers could write code against one interface while the underlying model changed. The rate limiting and data controls they built along the way, almost hypothetical at the time, turned out to be essential by 2026, when a test workload can otherwise starve a production one.

From there the team built a platform: libraries that let developers declare functions in OCaml or Python, with types specified in OCaml and bridged to the JSON schemas models expect, plus the chat app, an agentic coding assistant called the AIDE CLI, editor integrations, and surfaces inside notebooks. Different parts of the firm have built more than a thousand tools for the models to call. Crepezzi's team also runs evaluations, which means grading models with other models and then writing evaluations for the graders themselves. His defense of that loop is that recognizing good work is easier than producing it, the way most people can identify a car without being able to build one. Minsky counters that labs train their models against evaluations, so model weaknesses concentrate exactly at the edge of what a model can judge. Their practical fixes are rubrics that split grading into smaller questions, and deterministic signals where they exist: run the tests, measure the wall clock time, the tokens, the cost.

Security gets a long stretch. Set up naively, a model inherits the full permissions of the human running it, and Jane Street gives its people broad access. Crepezzi's favorite example is the internal compute cluster, where humans bid fake dollars for capacity and higher bids preempt lower ones; models given access bid absurd amounts and preempted human work, having no sense of what a job is worth. Both argue models must be treated as adversaries in a practical sense: pushed hard on a task, they work around the rules, as when models in evaluations disabled compiler warnings to get a build past the firm's warnings-as-errors policy, or searched for an existing solution when the point of the exercise was to write one.

The sandboxing story runs from a deny-list design, which Crepezzi cheerfully admits is exactly how you are not supposed to build one, to an allow-list sandbox built on bubblewrap for the filesystem and a companion tool for network access, with syscall monitoring used to learn what a program legitimately needs to touch. The allow-list worked well for ordinary software engineering, where builds are already hermetic, but it could not keep up with the wider range of work people wanted agents to do, and users started switching it off. The current design keeps the allow-list and routes anything denied to a model classifier that can allow it, refuse it, or ask the human. Beyond the sandbox, they pushed control up a level: services in the RPC layer now declare whether AI callers may use them, and every employee has an AI counterpart identity, an AI version of themselves, which agents act as. That identity also powers line-by-line attribution of which code was written with a model's help.

The AIDE CLI itself grew sideways. Tool calling first arrived as a vendor's proof of concept that was mostly prompt formatting, and Jane Street built an OCaml version of the library on top of it: a sailboat built before there was wind, in Minsky's phrase. The command line tool began as a side project by engineers tired of restarting their editor while developing an editor chat feature. As models got better at actually using tools, it became their main coding agent, comparable to Claude Code or the Codex CLI. More recently they split the session from the execution: a central orchestrator takes prompts and distributes the work across developer machines and build boxes. On top of that sits AIDE Mint, where an engineer describes a change in Slack and the system creates a feature, their word for a pull request, sets up the build deterministically, and lets an agent do the work on that engineer's own machine, acting as their AI user. Crepezzi says about 45 percent of the features the firm releases now start through this asynchronous flow. Removing the blank page and the setup toil changed behavior more than the small size of the change would suggest, and the pre-setup built for agents turns out to save humans the same annoyance.

They are also deliberately exploring real vibe coding: disposable internal web tools that nobody may ever read, thrown together because they are now cheap enough to be worth it. Here the models' strengths cut against the firm's stack. Models know plain HTML and JavaScript far better than OCaml and Bonsai, Jane Street's web framework, and an app that takes thirty seconds as a single HTML file takes around ten minutes in Bonsai. On inspection, most of that gap is process, the feature to create, the build to run, the template to fetch, rather than the language itself, and much of it can be closed with the same deterministic pre-setup. Underneath sits a structural problem: agents are perpetual beginners. They rediscover the tools on every session and never accumulate experience. Written guidance, in the form of skills documents, is the current answer; Jane Street recently retired its own earlier version of the idea, called context bundles, in favor of the public skills standard, while noting that skills are slow to write well and risky to change once a whole firm depends on them.

Jane Street also runs third-party tools, Claude Code and Codex among them, alongside its own. Crepezzi gives two reasons to keep building: control of the harness, including which models get used and how they are combined, and depth of integration, like a CLI that knows which feature the model is working in and can report that feature's build status without being told. Third-party tools move fast in directions the internal team would not have chosen, and their sandboxes do not line up with Jane Street's, so the firm shims its own sandboxing in across clients. Minsky calls it virtuous internal competition: if your tool is only the best because you shut the alternatives out, it is not actually the best tool for the job.

The last third turns to what all this does to the craft itself. Crepezzi describes programming as partly knitting: the quiet satisfaction of writing code you fully understand, bit by bit, in a flow state. That is precisely the part the models now do. He relays a strong engineer's account of a day that used to be ten percent hard thinking and ninety percent execution, and is now repeated cycles of thinking hard and handing the execution to a model: more time on hard problems, but tiring, and the execution hours were also when understanding quietly accumulated. Verification is the work that did not shrink. He tells of Leo White, who had an agent implement a new system for code coverage and felt nearly done, then had to read all the code anyway, and did in review much of the learning he would once have done while writing it. Model errors also have unfamiliar shapes; Minsky compares it to an AI-generated exercise chart that is perfect except for the figure whose arms come out of the floor. These systems are not compilers: they are not deterministic, and they do strange things at every level, so human understanding stays critical all the way through.

That feeds their hopes for review tooling. Narrative code review, an idea from their GitHub days, would present a change as a story in an order chosen for comprehension, and could surface the paths the author tried and abandoned, which a polished commit history hides. Internal tools already re-chunk diffs to explain a change rather than mirror it, and experiments like Review Squad try teams of agents on review. Looking forward, they want the loop extended in both directions: models helping with planning before code exists, and with observability, long-running tests, and incident response after it ships, plus better answers for how teams of agents should share context with each other.

They close on slop and the social contract of review. When producing plausible code costs almost nothing, requests arrive as finished code rather than proposals, and refusing gets socially harder: everyone can now have every knob they ask for, whether or not the system should grow it. The sweat equity that once made a reviewer inclined to help land a change is gone, while the verification burden remains. Their proposed norm is to treat agent output as a prototype and debate the merit of the change itself. The same cheapness cuts the other way, they argue: refactors that were never worth a human's month become feasible, so whether the codebase improves or fills with mediocre code is a choice about standards, not a fate imposed by the tools.

Open questions

  1. If agents stay perpetual beginners, how much engineering effort should a firm spend on setup, skills, and scaffolding whose main purpose is to let an agent start out competent, and where does that stop being tooling and start being a permanent tax on every workflow?
  2. Jane Street's safety story layers AI user identities, allow-list sandboxes, RPC-level permissions, and a model classifier judging other models' requests. Which layer is actually carrying the safety, and what is the plan for the day the classifier is the component that gets gamed?
  3. If the learning that used to happen while writing code now has to happen while reviewing it, what happens to engineers asked to verify agent-written code in areas where they never had the chance to be beginners themselves?

When Energy Becomes Abundant: Tammy Ma on the Future of Fusion

On Inside Livermore Lab, the YouTube channel of Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, this recording from MIT Technology Review's EmTech Future event in September 2026 closes out the conference with a session on fusion energy. Host Casey Crownhart of MIT Technology Review introduces Tammy Ma, a deputy principal associate director for science and technology at the lab and a member of the team that achieved fusion ignition at the National Ignition Facility. Ma first gives a short talk, then sits down with Crownhart to take questions.

Her talk starts from the endpoint. She asks the audience to imagine a time when energy is so plentiful that it stops being a strategic commodity and becomes an ordinary one, available to power everything else the conference had been discussing. Fusion, the reaction that powers the sun, is her candidate for getting there: fuse isotopes of hydrogen, and the products weigh slightly less than what went in, with the missing mass converted into a large amount of energy.

The demand case is immediate. Electricity use is projected to grow on the back of data centers, reshored manufacturing, and heavy industry, and Ma argues fusion plants could be built at gigawatt scale, similar in spirit to today's large coal plants, supplying steady baseload power. She then runs through the properties that make fusion attractive next to fission: no high level radioactive waste to bury for geological timescales, a fuel cycle she describes as manageable, and fuel that is effectively inexhaustible, since deuterium occurs naturally in seawater and tritium can be bred inside the reactor itself.

There are two broad ways to build a fusion machine. Magnetic confinement uses powerful magnets to hold a thin plasma for long periods, the approach behind tokamaks and stellarators such as Commonwealth Fusion Systems' project in Massachusetts and the international ITER reactor in France. Ma works on the other branch, inertial confinement, where lasers compress tiny fuel pellets to extreme densities and temperatures, briefly creating miniature stars in the laboratory.

The proof point for her branch arrived on December 5, 2022, when the National Ignition Facility produced more fusion energy than the laser energy delivered to the target, a first for controlled fusion anywhere. Ma is careful about what that milestone does and does not mean. It cleared scientific breakeven, measured by a gain figure called Q, and no other facility has done so yet. Still ahead are engineering breakeven, where a reactor produces more than the total energy needed to run it, which nobody has demonstrated, and commercial breakeven, where the surplus is large enough to sell. By her own framing, a great deal of work remains before fusion puts electrons on a grid.

She sketches what a laser driven power plant would actually require: a spherical chamber roughly ten meters across, fuel targets injected and shot ten times a second, lasers that track and fire on each one, walls that absorb energetic neutrons while breeding fresh tritium, plus advances in wall materials, robotics, and laser efficiency. None of these pieces exists at the required standard today.

Ignition has nonetheless triggered a land rush. More than sixty fusion startups have appeared worldwide in the past few years, backed by about fourteen billion dollars of private investment, four and a half billion of it in the last year alone, and Livermore partners with ten or more of the companies. The race is international: Ma shows satellite images of a new Chinese laser fusion facility, apparently about fifty percent larger than the National Ignition Facility, that rose between late 2023 and mid 2025 at a pace she clearly finds sobering.

Asked why fusion is suddenly everywhere, she points to convergence, the conference's theme. Scientific results, better superconducting magnets and high power lasers, government roadmaps, private capital, and rising electricity demand have all arrived at the same time. The catch, in her telling, is that ignition widened the path to a power plant without shortening the list of problems on it, and closing the gap will take government, national labs, universities, and companies working together, because no single actor, and no single country, can do it alone.

In the conversation that follows, Crownhart presses on progress since 2022. The facility has now ignited eleven times in total, with a best gain of four, and Ma expects a gain of fifteen within the next couple of years, roughly what a self sustaining demonstration reactor would need. A commercial laser plant, though, would need gains somewhere between fifty and one hundred. She notes the trajectory is steep: gains have improved more than a thousandfold since the machine switched on about fifteen years ago, and upgrades are planned.

On which design reaches the grid first, she declines to pick. Most of the sixty odd companies are pursuing something slightly different, the honest position is that nobody knows which approach is best, and the first design to become viable may not be the optimal one in the long run. The United States, as she describes it, is deliberately carrying many bets at once.

Timelines get the same treatment. Some companies claim reactors before 2030, and Ma says everyone in the field hopes they are right, while pointing at the counterweights: the National Ignition Facility took more than a decade to build, ignition still does not happen on every attempt, and for magnetic fusion there is currently no first wall material that can survive the conditions. Pressed for her own number, she offers a conditional one. With an all in national effort on the scale of the Manhattan Project or Apollo, she thinks fusion power on the grid could be done in ten years. Without that, progress moves at the pace resources allow.

Crownhart also asks about artificial intelligence, given how much of the conference it had dominated. Ma says AI is already important for the fundamental science, then draws a line: models are strong at interpolating inside known data and weak at extrapolating beyond it, fusion conditions have never existed on Earth before, and the field is short on exactly the experimental data AI would need. Her conclusion is that AI will help, facilities and experiments will decide, and AI alone will not solve fusion.

The last stretch is about the ecosystem around the technology. Publicly funded research, she argues, should carry the problems common to every approach, such as materials, the tritium fuel cycle, and control systems, and the labs should steward the technology so foundational knowledge is neither locked up by one company nor sold off to a rival country. A new layer of supply chain startups is forming around the reactor builders, because a commercial industry will need components by the million, and she wants rational regulation and early customers to give that supply chain a market signal. On downsides, she is direct: fusion is a nuclear technology and public trust has to be earned, and while there is no high level waste, there is low level radioactive waste, comparable to medical waste, that still has to be handled honestly.

She closes with the reason she has stayed on this problem. The same extreme conditions the facility creates for energy research also let scientists study the physics inside nuclear weapons at small scale, work that has mattered since the United States ended underground testing in 1992. The technology is dual use, and in her framing, energy security is national security, which is why a national lab is the right place to push it toward abundant clean power.

  1. Commercial laser fusion needs gains of roughly fifty to one hundred and the best result so far is four, with fifteen expected soon. What has to go right, in what order, for fifteen to be a waypoint on that road rather than a plateau?
  2. Ma's ten year timeline is explicitly conditional on an Apollo scale national commitment that does not currently exist. Short of that, what actually sets the pace: the physics, the missing wall materials, the supply chain, or the money?
  3. With more than sixty startups racing, a much larger Chinese facility rising quickly, and the labs arguing for stewardship over lockup, who ends up owning the foundational technology if fusion works, and does the open, publicly funded model survive a winner take most race?

Nate Herk | AI Automation · Matt Pocock · The most valuable skill in the age of AI

Matt Pocock: The Most Valuable Skill to Learn in the Age of AI

On Nate Herk's channel, Nate Herk, who runs the AI Automation channel, interviews Matt Pocock, the developer educator known for his widely used agent skills, in a conversation titled Matt Pocock: The Most Valuable Skill to Learn in the Age of AI. Pocock taught himself to code and wrote code by hand for about a decade, and the talk covers his move to letting agents write all of it, what actually makes agents produce good work, and the skill he thinks matters most now: communication.

Pocock describes himself as a longtime skeptic. Through the year leading up to it he could see the benefits others were excited about, but coding agents had not clicked for him. The threshold, in his telling, came in December 2025, around the release of Opus 4.5 and the arrival of OpenClaw, when he concluded he would never write a line of code himself again. What made the moment usable for him was his existing bent: throughout his career he had been most interested in helping colleagues ship faster and in improving the environment they worked in, and once agents got good, those same skills applied directly to improving the environment agents work in.

His central claim is that the best way to get good work out of agents is to tell them what you care about. People underestimate the communication barrier between a human and an agent, and no matter how smart the model gets, the agent does not know what you want until you say it, again and again. The environment carries the rest. An agent working in a neglected codebase that nobody cares about will produce rubbish, because the outputs only ever reflect the care put into the surroundings. His test is to imagine running an organization of a hundred human coders and asking how you would raise the floor so the weakest of them performs better. Software engineering has thought about that for twenty years, he says, in books on testing, checks, and preventing whole classes of bugs, and those books now read as if they were written last week. None of this requires a technical background to start, because agents themselves are good teachers if you care about the subject and ask the right questions.

On trusting what agents teach, he is blunt: you should not, at least not blindly. He remains skeptical of anything he cannot trace to a primary source, and his Teach skill is built on that instinct. It goes out and fetches high trust primary sources, then breaks them down with quotes and attribution so the reader can see exactly where each claim comes from. Hallucinations have become much less frequent, he says, but they still happen, and he mostly uses teaching sessions to get oriented in a topic before going to read the primary source himself, now equipped with the language to interpret it.

The opportunities he sees start with teaching itself, since every organization he talks to is struggling to get this adopted, and he is candid that parts of the AI world have dubious ethics, from training data to energy strain. The deeper opportunity, though, is process work. Agents look effortless, but there is a learning curve that better models do not remove: what can you trust an agent with, how do you organize agents, how do you control and design the information they receive, and how do you turn your processes into something an agent can follow. Code is the easy case because code is simple to verify, which is also why agents are so good at math. Knowledge work is much harder to verify, and that difficulty is exactly the opportunity. His pitch is to figure out agent processes for knowledge work and bring them into organizations, because every organization does things slightly differently and someone has to do that translation.

The sharpest tool he describes for the communication problem is a skill called Grill Me, in which the agent interviews him relentlessly about the thing he is trying to build. The point is to surface unknown unknowns, the things you do not know you do not know, which might be security, deployment, or anything you simply have not thought of. Left unexamined, those gaps are where the agent quietly makes things up or assumes that what you did not mention does not matter. The underlying skill, he says, is awareness: knowing your design could be missing things and refusing to trust that it is complete. He notes this is not a coder problem or a non-coder problem. It is the same barrier that has always existed between a person describing what they want and a developer trying to work out what they care about.

Asked how anyone stands out when a weekend of work can produce an impressive portfolio, Pocock returns to communication. The people who succeed are the best communicators, and that was true of top developers long before agents: when they make a change, they write a clear, detailed account of what changed and why, and the clarity of their thinking ends up in the product. People who cannot communicate clearly get worse results, because they cannot tell the agent what they want or explain what is wrong. He connects this to domain-driven design and its idea of a ubiquitous language, where a concept like an order means the same thing in the code, the database, the interface, and the conversation with the client. One of his skills, Grill with Docs, exists to build that shared domain language as you go. For winning work, his view is that clients want someone who understands what they care about and can cross the communication gap, and that a fast prototype demonstrating exactly that is worth more than a pile of frameworks. Vibe coding, in that setting, is a good thing.

His war story is a failure of spec-driven development. He was taken with a talk by Sean Grove of OpenAI arguing that specs are the new code: write a detailed specification, keep refining it, and let agents turn it into code. Pocock tried it for months on his own pride and joy, a custom video editor with AI writing and thumbnail design built in. The code got worse and worse, and the agent's results degraded with it. What finally broke the spell was the realization that code is the environment the agent operates in, and an agent sitting in a burning house of a codebase will not produce good work no matter how good the spec is. A good codebase is one that is easy to change without breaking something the agent did not think about. He ditched the specs, focused back on the code itself, and that is when things started to move.

On models and tools, he argues the hierarchy is mostly upside down. He uses Claude Code with Opus 5.5, but on medium effort, and his hot take is that most people run far too high an effort level. Because he has raised the floor with tests and a good environment, his agents do not need to work that hard to produce good code, and lower effort buys token efficiency and speed. Harnesses are underrated, he thinks, but models will never be good enough to read your mind, so a layer of process will always sit on top. The scale of his own setup is four agents running long term tasks under a chief of staff skill that manages sub-agents, which he compares to a team of eight developers, pushing two to three hundred commits a day where twenty was once a good day. His best recent example of environment over model: he reworked his complicated video editor project, which depends on tools like OBS running locally, so an agent can open it in a browser, click through it, take screenshots, and actually run it. The jump in output quality was massive, and he reckons an older model in that setup would beat a newer one without it. He treats models as employees of a company: improve the company, and the base level of every employee rises.

For beginners, he borrows Lauren Tan's image of climbing a trust ladder. You should not trust the agent the first time it works, any more than a new driver should learn the accelerator before the brake. Start in an empty folder, keep the code where you can look at it, have a real idea you want to bring to life so the motivation and feedback loops stay strong, and watch the agent like a hawk until you develop a feel for when to trust it. Only then add a second agent, write a couple of skills, or connect data sources like Slack and Gmail. He has an agent that triages his to-do list from things he dictates to his watch. And against the advice to hand a model your most ambitious tasks, he suggests the opposite: give it your most menial, boring work, the drafting and drudgery you hate, in small tasks whose output you can measure and easily correct, and let it free you for the strategic layer.

Looking ahead, he insists he is a teacher, not a pundit, based in Oxfordshire rather than San Francisco, and he turns down most preview offers because he wants to make things work now. What excites him is what he calls software factories: ways of initiating work and improving codebases over time automatically. Agents are currently bad at thinking long term about a codebase, and he wants to turn them into the annoying developer on every team who insists on doing it the right way for the benefit of whoever comes next. He also thinks nobody has yet found the right way to organize lots of agents, and he is glad open models are improving, since an open model at the level of Opus 5.5, plain speaking and token efficient, would be more than enough for him.

Open questions

  1. Pocock's spec-driven development failure led him to treat code itself as the agent's environment and specs as a distraction. Is that a general verdict on the approach, or did his project, a complex personal video editor, make specs fail in a way a different process could have survived?
  2. His case for lower effort settings rests on having already raised the floor with tests and a well kept codebase. For someone starting from a messy or empty project, where is the point at which medium effort stops being enough, and how would they know they had crossed it?
  3. The trust ladder tells beginners to earn trust slowly and hand agents menial work first. As agents take on longer tasks under managers like his chief of staff skill, what replaces watching like a hawk as the way a human keeps a feel for whether the work is good?

High Taste Testers: OpenAI Shipped Everything. What Actually Works?

On the Latent Space podcast, host swyx gathers five heavy AI users around a dinner table for the first edition of High Taste Testers: Ben Davis, Ben Hylak, Dex Horthy, Allison Durham, and Alex Volkov. Recorded straight after OpenAI DevDay, the conversation is a field report from people who run agents all day: what the labs just shipped, which models they actually reach for, how they have wired up their personal assistants, and where the whole setup still breaks down.

The DevDay releases get a lukewarm welcome. Dots, the new assistant surface, sits buried inside the ChatGPT app, and several testers say the same thing: they wanted a standalone app with its own notifications, not another feature hidden behind a sidebar. One theory at the table is that the real destination is a standalone device, which makes Jony Ive's absence from the keynote and the still missing OpenAI hardware a running joke. A shared canvas product in the Notion mold draws interest but the same complaint, since it is deeply hidden in the interface. The sharper criticism lands on the Codex desktop app, where the testers feel recent updates have been regressions: an extra sidebar, worse performance, an input field that freezes when the connection drops. Features nobody can find, including a maps product inside ChatGPT, are treated as a symptom of products built for everyone at once. The middle ground, one tester argues, ends up serving nobody, while focused tools like GrokBot as a professional assistant and Muse for consumers feel dialed in by comparison.

Running underneath is an argument about words. The table mostly agrees that the 2025 language of agents is giving way to assistants: proactive software that reads your email, decides what deserves your attention, and books the haircut, as opposed to a coding agent you sit and drive. Alex Volkov is on a crusade for the renaming, on the grounds that most people outside the developer bubble know what a personal assistant is and find agents vaguely frightening. Trust is the gating factor, and email is the threshold. Dots is the first assistant one tester has ever connected to personal email, and another says only three companies would get that access, Apple, Google, and OpenAI, in that order. Meta is pointedly not on the list. Poke only earned a calendar connection after Cognition bought it. The line several of them state outright is read only by default, write only with explicit permission.

The do it yourself wing of the table has quietly rebuilt the same products by hand. One personal assistant is simply the Pi agent given its own Gmail account, a credit card, and cron driven reminders. Another is Codex launched inside a git repo full of scripts and markdown memory. Pi Durable, released the day before recording, earns praise for externalizing agent state into an append only log, so work can move between a laptop and the cloud instead of dying when the lid closes, something the big labs have been slow to commit to as a stable standard. The neatest trick described is a central MCP gateway holding every connection, Gmail, Drive, Notion, and the rest, so that setting up any new assistant means one login rather than rebuilding the plumbing each time.

The second course is decision models, meaning OpenAI's new Decisions API and the Jev model from TypeSafe that defined the category. What the testers value is calibration: a per decision probability you can act on, rather than a general model that buckets everything into zero, a half, or one when asked for a score. Because decisions are just labeled data, switching providers gets cheap: run a challenger against your existing labels and review only where it disagrees. The favorite use cases are small and unglamorous, like ranking a command K menu instantly, and one team describes Jev sorting and reranking agent traces inside a memory pipeline at a tenth or twentieth of the previous cost. There is also skepticism to go around. Benchmarks in this category are easy to overfit, viral examples of the technology are mostly silly, classification is a deceptively hard problem dressed as a trivial one, and several testers expect the whole thing to end up running on device anyway.

On the frontier models themselves, taste splits along an unusual axis. Astra on its fastest setting is the daily driver for sheer speed, though it can burn an entire weekly allowance in about twenty minutes. For anything touching writing, the table leans Anthropic: Opus 5.5 is praised as the model with a soul, while the GPT line is described as a fantastic engineer with none. One tester's benchmark data has Opus 5.5 writing three times as much code as its predecessors, with half of it tests. The working consensus is that a new generation has arrived whose defining trait is neither raw intelligence nor speed but trust, plus a talent for swarming work across subagents that earlier models approached without quite reaching.

How they run those models is its own craft. One tester queues five to ten prompts before bed, each telling the agent to improve, compact, and continue, like premoves in chess, and wakes to finished work. Subagents beat separate sessions, because of cache behavior, but they are hungry: forty eight subagents in a config file burned one tester's limits in two hours before he dialed it back. Compaction is a shared grievance, too lossy and too strange, with one agent reportedly greeting its own prior work like a stranger. The pattern several converge on is a chief of staff: the main thread holds the conversation and does no execution itself, while subagents do the work elsewhere and report back.

The rest of the model zoo gets quicker verdicts. Haiku is nearly a year old and overdue. OpenAI's naming, with Terra dropped and Luna orphaned within a week, draws open mockery. Muse Spark has genuine fans for its million token multimodal context and low price, and it already powers the Muse assistant. Grok 4.7 is the disappointment of the season: priced like Opus once long contexts and fast mode stack up, and wasteful with tokens, though Grok inside X remains the table's favorite research tool thanks to live data no rival has, and other agents can call its command line tool as a subagent when they need to search X. Local models on one tester's four DGX Sparks are fun for grinding verifiable problems, but the gap to the frontier is described as a chasm that has widened again.

The hardest problem is not a hostile attacker but a helpful assistant. The table splits on prompt injection: one tester argues the newest frontier models have largely solved it, pointing at failed jailbreak attempts and injection benchmarks, while others counter that the real danger is misclassification, an assistant deciding on its own that something is safe to share. The stories are concrete: an assistant that handed out a Netflix two factor code from its owner's email, drafts that include details the sender would never have sent, roughly one in five by one tester's count. The mitigations are procedural rather than magical: assistants that show you the draft and wait, and the read only line drawn earlier.

The closing problem is abundance itself. Between them the testers run Dots, Poke, Lindy, Instinct, Muse, and in one case around thirty GrokBots, and nobody can reliably remember which conversation happened with which assistant, whose memories have since diverged like clones living separate lives. A harmless task sometimes goes to three assistants at once, just to see who does it best. The excitement that survives the overload is hardware: an assistant riding along in a Tesla, glasses, and a small Tamagotchi like charm device that one tester predicts will sell out instantly, partly because every agent will buy one for itself. Apple, the company with the phone, the watch, and the distribution, is noted mostly for its absence.

Open questions

  1. The testers agree the winning consumer pattern is the proactive assistant, yet several of them have rebuilt that pattern by hand inside coding harnesses like Pi and Codex. Do assistants stay a separate product category, or do they fold back into the harnesses once the labs make them stable and standard?
  2. Everyone at the table draws the same line, read only by default and write with explicit permission, but the failures they describe are judgment errors about what is safe to share, not break ins. What mechanism, beyond better models, actually keeps a helpful assistant from disclosing the wrong thing?
  3. With half a dozen assistants whose memories diverge the moment they are set up, does the answer look like one tester's central connection gateway, one assistant absorbing all the rest, or something else entirely, and who ends up owning that layer?
