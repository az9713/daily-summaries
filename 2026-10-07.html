How is AI transforming medicine? - A Question of Science with Brian Cox

Host Brian Cox opens this episode of A Question of Science, recorded at the Francis Crick Institute in London, by noting that a previous series asked whether AI can be harnessed for good. This time the panel narrows the question to medicine: what is AI already doing to diagnosis, drug discovery and everyday care, and what could go wrong.

The panel brings four perspectives. Mihaela van der Schaar is a Cambridge professor of machine learning and medicine and the Crick's Chief AI Scientist. Indra Joshi is a former emergency medicine doctor who now leads product work at Optum UK. Patrick Vallance has been an NHS doctor, an academic, an industry executive and a government science minister. Samra Turajlic is a cancer physician and researcher who leads a Crick laboratory and directs the Cancer Research UK Manchester Institute.

Asked what excites them most, the panel keeps returning to time. Vallance hopes AI gives doctors and nurses more of it to spend with patients. Turajlic, who treats cancer, measures everything in time: to diagnosis, to treatment, and through a drug development path that can stretch across decades while her patients cannot wait. Van der Schaar lists four fronts: guiding people from prevention through follow-up, running health systems with scarce resources, fixing clinical trials that fail or recruit unrepresentative patients, and helping scientists find new disease mechanisms. Joshi points to decision support for clinicians and patients, and to AI speeding up the building of the tools themselves.

On healthy ageing, the panel describes a shift from treating disease to predicting it. Turajlic cites UK Biobank, which has followed half a million people for two decades, and a recent Crick result using machine learning on that data to flag lung cancer risk years ahead of diagnosis. Vallance argues that combining genetic data, tests and wearables makes individual risk readable for the first time, and points to Our Future Health, a programme aiming to enrol five million people in the UK, with about half that number already signed up. Van der Schaar adds a quieter point: AI can also work out how little information is actually needed. In work with a UCL colleague, a lung cancer risk estimate that once took many variables was cut down to three things a person can measure at home.

Diagnosis draws a caution. Joshi warns that spotting whether a scan looks normal is the easy part; the danger is alert fatigue, the tired clinician at three in the morning who starts ignoring the machine's flags. The workforce, she argues, has to be trained to question the tools and know what to do when they disagree. Turajlic describes the hours clinicians spend assembling a patient's scattered records before a visit, work AI could take over so that the appointment itself is spent on the person. Van der Schaar imagines AI watching over decisions, prompting a clinician with possibilities they might have missed, so that a patient anywhere gets something closer to world-best expertise.

The liveliest exchange concerns ambient scribes, AI tools that listen to consultations and draft the notes. Joshi says the vendors' productivity claims are real but early, and that in side-by-side tests against doctors the output is roughly nine-tenths accurate, which is good and still not good enough to file unchecked. Her firm's safeguard is to codify the draft and keep a human in the loop before it enters the record. Cox observes that this trades one admin burden for another. Joshi replies that checking is faster than writing, and the real gain is eye contact during the visit. Turajlic raises two patient concerns: who owns the record that results, and whether value is being extracted from their data. She also stresses that a consultation holds far more than words, a patient's finances, caring duties and hopes all shape a decision, and no transcript captures that nuance.

On early screening, Turajlic describes wearables already used to watch for treatment side effects at home, while population-wide prediction from behaviour and body signals remains early work. Van der Schaar suggests the same forecasts could nudge people, showing a phone user how their risk is moving and what might change it.

Bias gets blunt treatment. Vallance notes that skin lesion tools trained mostly on pale skin perform worse on darker skin, and that the fix starts with better datasets, through efforts such as the Health Data Research Service, plus regulators checking tools do what they claim. He adds that skewed evidence is older than AI; trials were unrepresentative long before algorithms arrived. Turajlic backs this with figures: most genetic data comes from people of European ancestry, and a large share of UK trials over a decade never even recorded ethnicity. Joshi points to how symptoms get described and recorded differently, giving how women's pain is heard as an example, and says the culture is slowly changing.

On access, Turajlic offers computational pathology: slides exist wherever tissue is taken, but expert pathologists are scarce, and AI triage could carry that skill to places that have none. Van der Schaar wants AI to learn from the natural variation in how clinicians worldwide treat the same condition, shrinking the differences that come from error or lack of access rather than from the patient. Joshi widens the lens to communication, recalling how messaging groups carried public health guidance across India and Nigeria during the pandemic.

Pandemic preparation is where Vallance sees the largest prize. AI can design vaccines against how a virus is likely to mutate, not just against one variant, and can search for treatments far faster than the scramble of 2020. The goal set by the G7 in 2021, a diagnostic, a vaccine and a treatment within a hundred days of a new outbreak, looks reachable to him now, and AI is the only route to it. COVID itself, he concedes, was fought with little of today's AI. Van der Schaar adds logistics: predicting beds, ventilators and the effects of closing schools by learning from every country's experience at once.

On drug discovery, Vallance retells the AlphaFold leap: protein structures that once took years of crystallography now arrive at the press of a button, and the great majority of known structures have been solved since. Biology beyond that step is messier, he grants, but AI is good at sorting mess when the data exists. Turajlic draws the line between discovery and development: with thousands of possible therapy combinations and too few patients to test them, the practical problem is matching the right combination to the right person, factoring in diet, microbiome, sleep and other illnesses, and that matching is where AI can already help.

Data protection closes the argument. Vallance accepts that companies will profit from medical AI and says the terms matter: benefits must flow back to the health service, and data should sit in trusted environments that firms can query but cannot carry away. Van der Schaar warns against any single company owning the space. Pressed on the fear that risk scores could force treatment or make someone uninsurable, she describes privacy-preserving synthetic data built at the Alan Turing Institute, designed so no individual can be identified while the data stays useful. Joshi notes that NHS suppliers already work under strict contracts as data processors, and that the real exposure sits outside them, in consumer tools where people paste their own health details without thinking.

The final question turns the tables: where do the experts themselves go for quick health advice? Van der Schaar admits she increasingly asks AI, including to double-check what she was told, while insisting the future she wants is clinicians, patients and AI deciding together. Joshi says that at two in the morning, the chatbot is her friend. Vallance pairs AI with a phone call to an expert he knows. Turajlic phones a friend, and would not mind if the friend used AI for support. Cox closes on the thread running through the hour: the prize is expert-level advice for everyone, and the UK's best claim to leadership may be the validation and regulation that makes the public trust it.

Open questions

  1. Ambient scribes are about 90 percent accurate in early testing. What accuracy, checking process and legal ownership of the record should be required before a drafted note becomes part of a patient's file?
  2. Prediction now runs on datasets skewed toward European ancestry and historic trial populations. Who is responsible for proving a medical AI works across the whole population it will serve, and what happens to tools that cannot prove it?
  3. If AI risk scores can guide care, they could also guide insurers and employers. What rules should keep a personal health forecast from becoming a reason someone loses coverage or is pushed into treatment?

OpenAI Releases 722 Mathematical Results, Breakthroughs on Century-Old Problems Across Many Fields | Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis | Hodge Conjecture | Unique Games Conjecture | Kakeya Conjecture | Free Group Factors | NP-Hardness | Lean Formalization | Terence Tao | Intelligence Explosion

The channel Best Partners TV, hosted by Da Fei, takes a long look at the day OpenAI put mathematics on an assembly line.

On October 6, 2026, OpenAI uploaded a GitHub repository with a plain name: math. Inside were 722 manuscripts grouped into 372 result families, spanning 17 areas of mathematics. Theoretical computer science is the largest slice with 40 families, followed by combinatorics and by algebraic and complex geometry, with 31 families in number theory.

Most of the results came from a single unreleased internal model. OpenAI says each problem took on average about three hours of ChatGPT Pro compute, and the model was asked roughly 4,000 questions in total.

The folders carry dates. Nearly all of them fall between September 23 and October 5, and two of those days account for almost 200 manuscripts each. In other words, the bulk of the collection was generated in under two weeks.

About 63 percent of the families, 235 of 372, ship with Lean formalization files alongside the PDFs, and OpenAI also published 10 summaries of the model's reasoning. The README concedes that some unformalized results may contain errors and will be corrected over time.

The host then walks through the heaviest results. One claims a form of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis: a fixed zero-free region for the Riemann zeta function and all Dirichlet L-functions, with the threshold at seven eighths, formally checked in Lean, including a uniform exclusion of Landau-Siegel zeros. Rutgers professor Alex Kontorovich reacted on social media with disbelief, saying a human proof of this would be an instant Fields Medal. The full Riemann hypothesis, which concerns the line at one half, remains out of reach.

Another result addresses the Unique Games Conjecture from a new angle. Rather than proving the conjecture itself, the work shows that the NP-hardness of approximation at the basic semidefinite programming threshold follows from P not equaling NP alone, with no conditional assumption about unique games. That matters because since 2008 the optimality of basic SDP has rested on the unproven conjecture; removing the assumption locks in the ceiling for efficient approximation algorithms. Other results touch the Hodge conjecture for complex multiplication abelian varieties, four-dimensional Kakeya questions, free group factors, the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer formula on large twist families, Hilbert's tenth problem over the rationals, the irrationality of Catalan's constant, and the Mahler conjecture.

The narrator is also interested in the reaction. In August, OpenAI privately briefed around 40 leading mathematicians and asked how results like these should be released. The advice was to publish slowly as proper papers. Attendees say OpenAI indicated it would not release everything at once; a company spokesperson later said OpenAI does not recall any such commitment. In September, Terence Tao helped lead an open statement signed by 25 Fields Medalists criticizing AI companies for treating mathematics as a benchmark. Tao now argues the field suffers from proof indigestion: machines produce and even formally check proofs far faster than people can translate, absorb, and teach them. Tao had urged colleagues to embrace AI only months earlier; his tone has since reversed.

The video also flags a fairness worry. One report claims OpenAI poured thousands of agents into a Navier-Stokes problem after hearing that two outside researchers, working with OpenAI's own tools, were close to a breakthrough, and one of those researchers has accused the company of jumping ahead of their private work.

Open questions

  1. When independent mathematicians finish auditing the strongest claims, such as the quasi-Riemann zero-free region and the Hodge cases, how many survive intact, and how long does that audit take compared with the two weeks it took to generate them?
  2. Will OpenAI give outside researchers access to the same model, so they can pose their own problems, or does mathematics split into a small group that picks the questions and a large group left verifying answers?
  3. What replaces peer review as the field’s quality filter if release volume keeps growing this fast, and can formal verification plus community checking carry that weight on its own?

Mapping the Robotics Market on One Whiteboard

On Lightwork, the Lightspeed Venture Partners show, the episode "Mapping the Robotics Market on One Whiteboard" has Claire Zau sitting down with Lightspeed partner Amber Yang to make sense of a crowded robotics field. Yang draws the whole market on two axes: how general the model is, and how general the body is. The episode moves quadrant by quadrant, explaining who is building what and where the open ground might be.

Yang starts at the model layer. Older robotics companies, she argues, struggled because every new action, body, or hardware tweak demanded fresh adjustments. The field has since shifted toward vision-language-action models, which take in images and words and predict the robot's next move, and the frontier is now moving again toward world action models that first imagine a future state of the world, then work out the actions needed to get there. In-context learning is the other shift she flags: a robot that updates its behavior in real time from its own sensor data, rather than from human demonstrations it must translate onto its body.

Data, in her telling, is the field's central constraint. Language models trained on the internet; robots have no equivalent corpus, so companies collect teleoperated demonstrations, egocentric video from head-mounted cameras, and synthetic data from simulation. A whole market of robotics data vendors has formed around that need. Hardware carries its own trap: actuators make up roughly 40 to 60 percent of a humanoid's build cost, and that supply chain, rare earth magnets included, runs through China, which is why Yang doubts humanoids get cheap quickly.

The map itself splits into four strategies. Some companies pair a specific model with their own body and sell deployments, not research: warehouse and factory players like Dexterity, Tacta, and Reflex Robotics, whose moat is customer relationships and years of operational trust. Others, like Ultra, skip training entirely and fine-tune a general model such as Physical Intelligence's on a day or so of factory data. The general-model, many-bodies quadrant holds Skild and Physical Intelligence themselves, plus newcomers claiming robots can learn a task from a single human video. And the general-model, own-body quadrant is the humanoid race: Sunday Robotics, with a sensor-dense hand that captures high-quality data as it works, alongside 1X and Figure.

Yang's most forward-looking call is the DeployCo: companies that take someone else's model into a restaurant, nursing home, or laundromat and run it as a service, with the customer never knowing what is under the hood. She also sees the language model infrastructure stack replaying itself in robotics: inference chips built for low-latency physical work, data quality tooling, and above all evaluation and observability, since robotics still has no shared benchmark and every company grades its own homework. For a field this tied to safety, she suggests, that gap is itself an opportunity, much as autonomous vehicles had to build their own testing discipline from scratch.

Open questions

  1. First, does a truly general robot model actually arrive, and if it does, do the deployment-first companies survive as customer owners, or get absorbed into the model makers' stacks?
  2. Second, can a DeployCo business stay model-agnostic and durable, or do the foundation model companies eventually capture the deployment layer too?
  3. Third, who sets the shared safety and performance benchmarks for robotics, and will they emerge from an industry body, a dominant company, or a regulator?

Why Texas Is Making Data Centers Wait

In a16z's energy newsletter, Ryan McEntush explains how Texas went from the easiest place in America to power a data center to a state that has paused new permits and is auditing nearly five hundred gigawatts of proposed load.

The starting problem is the queue. ERCOT, which runs most of the Texas grid, had about 63 gigawatts of large new customers waiting at the end of 2024. By June 2026 the figure had reached roughly 474 gigawatts, more than five times the state's record peak demand, and about 90 percent of it was data centers. Much of that paper demand is speculative. Developers often file the same project at several sites, sometimes with no customer in place, which leaves planners unsure which loads will ever exist and leaves ratepayers at risk of funding upgrades for projects that never arrive.

Texas answered with a pause. Permits for new data centers are frozen while an audit checks the projects in ERCOT's Batch Zero study group, and switch-on approvals for loads of 75 megawatts or more are on hold, including 17 that had finished every other step. McEntush adds that politics sits behind the pause as well: noise, water, and power bills worry nearby communities, and as of June only 28 of 377 companies had answered a state survey about their resource use.

His main subject is delivering power at all. For the last decade, a hyperscaler could find a utility with spare network capacity and sign a long-term contract that matched its use on paper. That slack is gone. Almost any new large load now triggers upgrades, and a full grid connection can take five to ten years. Colocation fights show the friction: Amazon's campus beside Talen's Susquehanna nuclear plant ended with federal regulators rejecting a bid for more direct supply, and Texas approved CyrusOne's 760 megawatt Freestone campus beside a Constellation gas plant only on condition the campus can cut its load within 30 minutes of an ERCOT call.

Cost allocation is evolving alongside. Regulators have proposed charging large loads by all twelve monthly peaks rather than only summer ones, and new Public Utility Commission rules effective October 8 set a 100,000 dollar study fee plus a 50,000 dollar per-megawatt deposit. McEntush calls that deposit a thin filter, because a project that falls two years behind mainly risks about a fifth of it, while the cost of costlier regional lines stays shared.

Speed beats purity meanwhile. SemiAnalysis counts roughly 75 gigawatts of equipment orders for on-site power, and because idle GPUs cost more than electricity, developers will accept gas plants, engines, and batteries years before the grid can serve them. Campuses are being designed to run off-grid for years, yet nearly all still intend to connect, because on-site power almost always costs more. An island, as the piece details, gives up real advantages: spare capacity spread across more than 1,460 generating units, plus inertia, fault current, steady voltage, and black start. Reliability on an island is expensive. One gigawatt running through a 14 hour winter night takes about 14 gigawatt-hours of batteries, and Meta has gone as far as tents with no backup generators, trading uptime for time.

Law decides who may string a private wire. Supplying only yourself and your tenants does not make you a utility; selling to the neighbor does. McEntush points to proposals that would loosen this, such as consumer-regulated electricity and Utah's off-grid option for very large loads, and expects permissive states to win more of the buildout if demand stays this hot. Wind of a different kind also matters: campuses that can trim their draw or export power are easier for a grid to accept, and Texas already has live pilots in home battery fleets. Planners, though, still size upgrades for a flexible campus's full planned load, which he wants changed.

Zoomed out, the demand dwarfs planned supply. A steady 100 gigawatts equals about a fifth of US electricity use in 2025, while the generation developers told the EIA they planned for 2026 would deliver roughly a sixth of that. Gas leads because it runs when needed, but equipment is the bottleneck, transformers now take more than two years, and he flags the emissions cost of gas at that scale along with the China-heavy supply chain behind solar and batteries. His closing Texas prescription is narrow: audit Batch Zero quickly, exempt developers who have funded a first phase or run emergency-only generation from the blanket freeze, give credible projects a dependable connection date once they post deposits and pay for reserved capacity, and make utilities answer for upgrade delays. His broader claim is that AI companies will pay almost any price for power, so letting them fund grid upgrades the country needs anyway is better than pushing them onto private islands and pushing those costs onto everyone else.

Open questions

  1. After the audit's December report, will Texas move credible projects quickly, or does the freeze quietly become normal permitting delay?
  2. If permissive states let private grids and off-grid campuses win the next wave of building, how do shared grid costs stay fair when the biggest loads opt out?
  3. Will planners start crediting flexibility, batteries, and advanced transformers in real time, so a flexible campus can stay flexible for good instead of being sized for its full planned load?

Your AI Product Faces The Same Test As Gemini 4 Argon. Topping A Leaderboard Won't Pass It.

Nate B Jones opens with a blunt claim: leaderboards have stopped telling you anything useful about AI. Google's new Gemini 4 Argon model sits at the top of the Vals Index, yet almost nobody can actually get their hands on it, so nobody can say whether it is any good in practice. His replacement test is customer obsession. The products that win are built by people who use them constantly, notice what breaks, and fix it.

He maps the major labs to the customers they serve. Anthropic is aimed at enterprise and professional work. OpenAI has a growing enterprise arm alongside a very large consumer business. Meta is deep on consumer products. Google is the odd one out in his telling: it earns heavily from compute, serves search users, and ships Gemini, but he cannot see which of those customers Argon is for. He wants Google in the game and says he would love to see it succeed, but he is asking for a release that shows where the model fits and why it beats what people already have.

The argument then turns to anyone building AI inside a company. Models now change every week, and the old product roadmap, updated every month or two, no longer matches that pace. Making money with AI means building on intelligence that will not sit still, and that only works if a team starts from a specific customer and a specific job. Benchmark news, he argues, is a distraction from that discipline.

His evidence is personal. On a recent flight he used ChatGPT's Dots connectors to pull subscriptions from banking and email sources into a single financial model covering both his personal and business accounts, then handed the spreadsheet to Muse to act on, cancelling and deciding item by item because it could move quickly on the web. He uses the two tools differently: Dots for slow, connector-heavy jobs run over days, including chewing through more than 20,000 of his X likes and bookmarks to surface 741 books, and Muse for fast, direct action. His point is that you only learn where a tool sings and where it stumbles by pushing it on your own real work.

He also thinks the market has room for many winners, because people have wildly different needs. He points to a product that lets kids identify trees and plants on forest walks as the kind of narrow, parent-aware design he wants more of, and he complains that most AI products still copy the ChatGPT chat window, which he calls a tyranny of the chatbot. A friend's story makes the same point emotionally: after a child hurt an eye on a toy (the child and the eye turned out fine), Muse offered calm perspective and nudged the parent to give the kid a minute rather than a lecture, empathy he counts as customer obsession.

His closing tally is that he has logged 26 separate agent use cases in three months, from tax preparation to travel planning to school schedules. The failures he hits now, he says, rarely come from the models. They come from product experience. He ends with the rumored Fable 5.5: whatever it scores, he expects it to land with enterprises because that is the customer Anthropic obsesses over, and he will judge it on customer value rather than rank.

Open questions

  1. If leaderboards stop being the shared scoreboard, what replaces them when buyers compare models and products?
  2. Can Google connect a frontier model like Argon to a named customer and a clear job before rivals take those customers?
  3. As models keep changing weekly, how should product teams plan, price, and support AI built on ground that keeps moving?

TechTechPotato · Dr. Ian Cutress · ASML · EUV lithography

I Sat Down With ASML's CEO - Here's What He Told Me

TechTechPotato, hosted by Dr. Ian Cutress, published this fireside chat from Hello Tomorrow in Amsterdam under the title "I Sat Down With ASML's CEO - Here's What He Told Me". The guest is ASML chief executive Christophe Fouquet, speaking to an audience of founders and early stage investors.

Fouquet treats Moore's Law less as a slogan and more as a demand curve. The old rhythm of doubling transistors about every two years is, in his account, too slow for current AI systems. Advanced AI products need much faster growth in both logic and memory capacity. That means transistor scaling still matters, but it has to be multiplied by denser integration, including advanced packaging and stacked dies or wafers. If one path slows, the other has to carry more of the load.

ASML's own model grew out of that same integration problem. Fouquet describes the company as a Philips spin off that chose system integration rather than trying to own every layer itself. ASML works with customers to learn what the next machine must do, then assembles best in class partners around the problem. Zeiss for optics is the long running example. He extends the same logic to newer partners in EUV and AI.

EUV is the cautionary tale inside the success story. It took roughly two decades, with long stretches of failed designs and painful dead ends, especially around source power. Customer co investment mattered because the work was too expensive and too slow for ASML to fund at the needed pace alone. Roadmaps stretching five, ten, or fifteen years require trust, since partners have to share plans without fearing that the information will be used against them.

His advice to builders is blunt in substance. Start with the exact problem, find the people who live with that problem every day, and work with them early. A strong idea is not enough by itself. It has to survive manufacturing, supply chains, service, and repeatable operations. Many companies can make one hard thing. Far fewer can ship hundreds of them and keep them running.

On AI, Fouquet argues that the scarce asset is usually not the base model. It is the data a company uniquely generates and understands. General models mostly learn from the public internet. Industrial value comes when a firm turns its own operating data into customized models that improve design, performance, and production. That is the logic of the Mistral partnership. ASML does not see itself as an AI company, but it produces enormous amounts of machine and process data. Mistral is there to help build the architecture and tailored models around that data. Fouquet says Mistral being European is welcome, but he frames the choice as an industrial fit after looking at partners inside and outside Europe.

For startups, he says money from a large partner is only the first layer. The better question is whether the large company can help with engineering, operations, and mistakes it has already made. A big partner can also damage a small company if the fit is poor. The same point shapes his view of Europe. Europe has strong education, research, and technical founders. The weak point comes at scale up, when companies need capital, space, equipment, and rules that help rather than slow them down.

He is cautiously positive on policy direction. The newer European chips agenda looks broader than factory building alone and pays more attention to the surrounding market. His reservation is institutional. Industry has to lead industry. Governments should enable the sector, not manage each project. He also thinks Europe often changes only after urgency becomes impossible to ignore, so recognition is improving faster than execution speed.

Demand has flipped quickly. Six months earlier, ASML was looking at a healthy but flattish market and could not confirm growth. Then customers began treating AI infrastructure as a multi year buildout, leaving the supply chain trying to catch up.
